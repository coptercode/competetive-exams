# Introduction

Biology is the scientific study of **life and living organisms**. The Earth is home to millions of different organisms that vary in size, shape, habitat, and mode of life. To study this enormous diversity systematically, biologists classify organisms based on their similarities and differences.

This chapter introduces the **characteristics of living organisms**, the **need for biological classification**, the **three domains of life**, **binomial nomenclature**, the **taxonomic hierarchy**, and various **taxonomical aids** used for identification and study of organisms.

---

# 1. What is the Living World?

The **Living World** includes all living organisms present on Earth, ranging from microscopic bacteria to giant trees and animals.

Living organisms show a wide variety of forms but possess certain common characteristics that distinguish them from non-living things.

---

# 2. Characteristics of Living Organisms

Living organisms possess several unique characteristics.

---

## (A) Growth

Growth is a permanent increase in size, volume, or number of cells.

### In Plants

- Growth occurs throughout life.
- Growth is unlimited (indeterminate growth).
- Meristematic tissues are responsible for growth.

### In Animals

- Growth occurs up to a certain age.
- Growth is limited (determinate growth).

### Important Note

Growth alone is **not** a defining characteristic of life because non-living objects like crystals can also increase in size.

---

## (B) Reproduction

Reproduction is the process by which living organisms produce offspring of their own kind.

### Types of Reproduction

### 1. Asexual Reproduction

Only one parent is involved.

Examples

- Amoeba – Binary fission
- Hydra – Budding
- Yeast – Budding

### 2. Sexual Reproduction

Two parents are involved.

Examples

- Humans
- Flowering plants
- Birds

### Exceptions

Some living organisms do not reproduce but are still considered living.

Examples

- Mule
- Worker bee
- Sterile human beings

Therefore, reproduction alone cannot define life.

---

## (C) Metabolism

**Metabolism** is the sum total of all chemical reactions taking place inside the body.

It consists of two types:

### Anabolism

- Building-up reactions
- Require energy
- Example: Photosynthesis, protein synthesis

### Catabolism

- Breakdown reactions
- Release energy
- Example: Respiration, digestion

### Importance

Metabolism is considered one of the most important characteristics of living organisms because metabolic reactions occur only in living cells.

---

## (D) Cellular Organization

All living organisms are made up of one or more cells.

- Unicellular organisms consist of a single cell.
- Multicellular organisms consist of many cells.

The **cell** is the basic structural and functional unit of life.

---

## (E) Consciousness

Living organisms respond to environmental stimuli.

Examples

- Plants bend towards light.
- Humans respond to sound.
- Animals run away from danger.

The ability to sense and respond to the environment is called **consciousness**.

---

## (F) Adaptation

Living organisms possess structural, physiological, or behavioral features that help them survive in their environment.

Example

- Camel stores fat in its hump.
- Cactus has spines instead of leaves.

---

## (G) Homeostasis

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment despite changes in the external environment.

Example

- Human body temperature remains around **37°C**.

---

## Summary of Characteristics

| Characteristic        | Description                      |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Growth                | Increase in size and cell number |
| Reproduction          | Production of offspring          |
| Metabolism            | Chemical reactions within cells  |
| Cellular Organization | Presence of cells                |
| Consciousness         | Response to stimuli              |
| Adaptation            | Survival in the environment      |
| Homeostasis           | Maintenance of internal balance  |

# 3. Diversity in the Living World

Scientists estimate that millions of species exist on Earth.

Different organisms vary in:

- Size
- Shape
- Habitat
- Nutrition
- Reproduction
- Cellular organization

This diversity is called **Biodiversity**.

# 4. Need for Classification

Studying millions of organisms individually is difficult. Therefore, classification is necessary.

### Importance of Classification

- Makes the study of organisms easier.
- Helps identify organisms.
- Reveals evolutionary relationships.
- Avoids confusion caused by local names.
- Facilitates scientific communication.

---

# 5. Three Domains of Life

Based on modern molecular studies, living organisms are classified into **three domains**.

| Domain   | Characteristics                         | Examples                          |
| -------- | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Archaea  | Primitive prokaryotes, extreme habitats | Methanogens, Halophiles           |
| Bacteria | True prokaryotes                        | _Escherichia coli_, Cyanobacteria |
| Eukarya  | Organisms with true nucleus             | Plants, Animals, Fungi, Protists  |

# 6. Taxonomy

**Taxonomy** is the branch of biology concerned with the identification, naming, and classification of organisms.

It includes three major activities:

- Identification
- Nomenclature
- Classification

---

# 7. Binomial Nomenclature

**Binomial Nomenclature** is the scientific system of naming organisms developed by **Carolus Linnaeus**.

Each organism is given a **two-word scientific name**.

### Format

```
Genus species
```

Example

```
Homo sapiens
```

---

## Rules of Binomial Nomenclature

1. Every scientific name has **two words**.
2. The first word represents the **Genus**.
3. The second word represents the **Species**.
4. The Genus name begins with a **capital letter**.
5. The Species name begins with a **small letter**.
6. Scientific names are written in **italics** when printed.
7. If handwritten, both words are **underlined separately**.
8. Scientific names are generally derived from **Latin** or are Latinized.

---

## Examples

| Common Name | Scientific Name    |
| ----------- | ------------------ |
| Human       | _Homo sapiens_     |
| Mango       | _Mangifera indica_ |
| Tiger       | _Panthera tigris_  |
| Rice        | _Oryza sativa_     |
| Housefly    | _Musca domestica_  |

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **Genus** with **Species** while writing scientific names.
- Writing both words of a scientific name with capital letters.
- Forgetting to italicize or underline scientific names.
- Assuming that growth alone defines life.
- Believing that all living organisms reproduce (e.g., mules and worker bees are exceptions).
- Confusing **taxonomy** with **systematics**.
- Treating **Kingdom** as the smallest taxonomic category instead of **Species**.
- Confusing a **Herbarium** (preserved plants) with a **Botanical Garden** (living plants).

---

# Chapter Summary

- The **Living World** consists of all living organisms that exhibit characteristics such as **growth**, **reproduction**, **metabolism**, **cellular organization**, **consciousness**, **adaptation**, and **homeostasis**.
- Due to the immense diversity of organisms, **classification** is essential for their systematic study and identification.
- Modern biological classification recognizes **three domains of life**: **Archaea**, **Bacteria**, and **Eukarya**.
- **Taxonomy** involves the identification, naming, and classification of organisms.
- **Binomial nomenclature**, introduced by **Carolus Linnaeus**, assigns every organism a unique two-word scientific name following internationally accepted rules.
- The **taxonomic hierarchy** progresses from **Species → Genus → Family → Order → Class → Phylum/Division → Kingdom**.
- **Taxonomical aids** such as **Herbaria**, **Botanical Gardens**, **Museums**, **Zoological Parks**, and **Taxonomic Keys** play a vital role in the identification, conservation, and scientific study of biodiversity.
- Understanding the living world provides the foundation for all subsequent topics in biology, including classification, anatomy, physiology, genetics, ecology, and evolution.

# Introduction

The Earth is home to millions of organisms with remarkable diversity in size, structure, nutrition, reproduction, and habitat. To study this diversity systematically, scientists have developed various systems of classification. One of the most widely accepted systems is the **Five Kingdom Classification** proposed by **R.H. Whittaker (1969)**.

This chapter explains the **Five Kingdom Classification**, the characteristics of **Kingdom Monera**, **Protista**, and **Fungi**, and introduces important acellular entities such as **Viruses**, **Viroids**, **Prions**, and the symbiotic association called **Lichens**.

---

# 1. Biological Classification

**Biological classification** is the systematic arrangement of organisms into groups based on their similarities, differences, and evolutionary relationships.

### Objectives

- To simplify the study of organisms.
- To identify organisms accurately.
- To understand evolutionary relationships.
- To provide a universal system of classification.

---

# 2. Basis of Five Kingdom Classification

R.H. Whittaker classified all living organisms into five kingdoms based on:

- Cell structure
- Cell organization
- Mode of nutrition
- Reproduction
- Phylogenetic (evolutionary) relationships

---

# 3. Five Kingdom Classification

The five kingdoms are:

1. Monera

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Why are bacteria placed in Kingdom Monera?

**Solution**

Because bacteria are **prokaryotic**, lack a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, and are unicellular.

---

### Example 2

Differentiate between viruses and viroids.

**Solution**

Viruses contain nucleic acid enclosed within a protein coat, whereas viroids consist only of naked RNA without a protein coat.

---

### Example 3

Why are lichens considered a symbiotic association?

**Solution**

The alga prepares food by photosynthesis, while the fungus absorbs water and minerals and provides protection. Both partners benefit.

---

### Example 4

Name one organism responsible for red tide.

**Solution**

**Gonyaulax**, a dinoflagellate, is responsible for red tides.

---

### Example 5

Why are Deuteromycetes called imperfect fungi?

**Solution**

Because their sexual stage is absent or has not yet been observed.

---

### Example 6

State one important difference between fungi and plants.

**Solution**

Plants contain chlorophyll and are autotrophic, whereas fungi lack chlorophyll and are heterotrophic, obtaining nutrients by absorption.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **Monera** (prokaryotes) with **Protista** (eukaryotes).
- Thinking all bacteria are harmful; many are beneficial.
- Confusing **cyanobacteria** with true algae.
- Assuming viruses are complete living organisms—they reproduce only inside host cells.
- Mixing up **viroids** (RNA only) and **prions** (protein only).
- Forgetting that fungal cell walls are made of **chitin**, not cellulose.
- Confusing **mycelium** with **hyphae** (mycelium is a network of hyphae).
- Believing that all protists are photosynthetic; many are heterotrophic.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Biological classification** organizes living organisms based on similarities, differences, and evolutionary relationships.
- **R.H. Whittaker's Five Kingdom Classification** groups organisms into **Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae,** and **Animalia** based on cell structure, organization, nutrition, reproduction, and phylogeny.
- **Kingdom Monera** includes prokaryotic organisms such as bacteria, archaea, cyanobacteria, and mycoplasma.
- **Kingdom Protista** consists of unicellular eukaryotes, including **chrysophytes, dinoflagellates, euglenoids, slime moulds,** and **protozoans**.
- **Kingdom Fungi** comprises heterotrophic eukaryotes with chitinous cell walls that reproduce by vegetative, asexual, and sexual methods.
- **Viruses** are acellular particles containing either DNA or RNA enclosed in a protein coat, while **viroids** contain only RNA and **prions** contain only protein.
- **Lichens** represent a mutualistic association between algae and fungi, serving as important ecological indicators of air quality.
- Understanding biological classification helps explain the diversity of life and forms the basis for advanced studies in microbiology, botany, zoology, and evolutionary biology.

# Introduction

The **Plant Kingdom (Kingdom Plantae)** includes a vast variety of multicellular, eukaryotic, and predominantly autotrophic organisms. Plants differ greatly in their structure, habitat, and reproductive methods. To understand this diversity, plants are classified into major groups based on their body organization, presence or absence of vascular tissues, seed formation, and reproductive structures.

This chapter covers the classification of plants into **Algae, Bryophytes, Pteridophytes, Gymnosperms,** and **Angiosperms**, along with their important characteristics, life cycles, and the concept of **Alternation of Generations**.

---

# 1. General Characteristics of Kingdom Plantae

Plants share the following common features:

- Multicellular and eukaryotic.
- Mostly autotrophic due to the presence of chlorophyll.
- Cell wall made of cellulose.
- Food stored mainly as starch.
- Exhibit alternation of generations.
- Mostly non-motile.
- Reproduce sexually and/or asexually.

---

# 2. Classification of Plant Kingdom

The Plant Kingdom is broadly classified into:

```
Kingdom Plantae
│
├── Algae
├── Bryophytes
├── Pteridophytes
├── Gymnosperms
└── Angiosperms
```

Based on:

- Body organization
- Presence of vascular tissues
- Seed formation
- Nature of reproductive organs

---

# 3. Algae

Algae are simple, chlorophyll-bearing, autotrophic plants found mainly in aquatic habitats.

## General Characteristics

- Mostly aquatic.
- Thalloid plant body (not differentiated into root, stem, and leaves).
- Cell wall made of cellulose.
- Photosynthetic.
- Reproduce vegetatively, asexually, and sexually.

---

## Economic Importance of Algae

### Useful

- Produce oxygen.
- Primary producers in aquatic ecosystems.
- Source of agar and algin.
- Used as food (e.g., _Porphyra_).
- Biofertilizers (_Anabaena_, _Nostoc_).

### Harmful

- Algal blooms may reduce dissolved oxygen.
- Some species produce toxins.

---

- Cell wall made of cellulose.

- Mostly marine.

### Examples

---

### Characteristics

### Examples

- The **Plant Kingdom** comprises multicellular, eukaryotic, autotrophic organisms with cellulose cell walls and chlorophyll pigments.
- Plants are broadly classified into **Algae, Bryophytes, Pteridophytes, Gymnosperms,** and **Angiosperms** based on body organization, vascular tissues, and seed formation.
- **Algae** are simple thalloid plants classified into **Chlorophyceae, Phaeophyceae,** and **Rhodophyceae** according to their pigments and stored food.
- **Bryophytes** are non-vascular plants with a dominant gametophyte generation and require water for fertilization.
- **Pteridophytes** are the first vascular plants and reproduce through spores; some exhibit **heterospory**, an important evolutionary advancement toward seed development.
- **Gymnosperms** produce naked seeds borne on cones, while **Angiosperms** produce flowers and fruits with enclosed seeds.
- Plants exhibit **Alternation of Generations**, involving alternating **gametophyte (haploid)** and **sporophyte (diploid)** phases.
- Depending on the dominant generation, plant life cycles are classified as **Haplontic**, **Diplontic**, or **Haplo-diplontic**.
- Understanding plant classification and life cycles forms the basis for advanced studies in botany, plant physiology, genetics, and evolution.

# Introduction

The **Animal Kingdom (Kingdom Animalia)** includes a vast diversity of multicellular, eukaryotic, heterotrophic organisms. Animals differ in body organization, symmetry, body cavity, segmentation, and the presence or absence of a notochord. These characteristics form the basis for their classification.

This chapter explains the **basis of animal classification**, the important characteristics of **non-chordates and chordates**, and the distinguishing features of different classes of vertebrates.

Animals possess the following common characteristics:

- Multicellular and eukaryotic.
- Heterotrophic mode of nutrition.

- Mostly reproduce sexually.
- Embryonic development begins from a zygote.

# Introduction

**Anatomy** is the branch of biology that deals with the **internal structure** of organisms. In flowering plants, anatomy involves the study of different tissues and the internal organization of roots, stems, and leaves.

This chapter explains **plant tissues**, the internal anatomy of **dicot and monocot roots**, **dicot and monocot stems**, **dorsiventral and isobilateral leaves**, and the process of **secondary growth** in dicot stems.

---

# 1. Plant Tissues

A **tissue** is a group of similar cells having a common origin and performing a specific function.

Plant tissues are broadly classified into:

```
Plant Tissues
│
├── Meristematic Tissues
└── Permanent Tissues
```

---

# 2. Meristematic Tissues

Meristematic tissues consist of actively dividing cells responsible for plant growth.

## Characteristics

- Cells divide continuously.
- Thin cell walls.
- Dense cytoplasm.
- Large nucleus.
- Vacuoles absent or very small.
- No intercellular spaces.

---

## Types of Meristems

### (A) Apical Meristem

Located at the tips of roots and shoots.

**Function**

- Increases the length of the plant.
- Responsible for **primary growth**.

---

### (B) Intercalary Meristem

Located at the bases of leaves or internodes.

**Function**

- Rapid elongation.
- Regrowth after grazing.

**Examples**

- Grass
- Bamboo

---

### (C) Lateral Meristem

Located along the sides of stems and roots.

**Function**

- Increases girth.
- Responsible for **secondary growth**.

**Examples**

- Vascular cambium
- Cork cambium

---

# 3. Permanent Tissues

Permanent tissues are formed from meristematic tissues after differentiation.

They lose the ability to divide.

#### Functions

- Mechanical support.

### Sclerenchyma

- Dead cells.

- Narrow lumen.

#### Types

#### Functions

### Example 1

Differentiate between meristematic and permanent tissues.

**Solution**

Meristematic tissues consist of actively dividing cells responsible for growth, whereas permanent tissues are differentiated cells that have lost the ability to divide and perform specialized functions.

### Example 2

Name the four components of xylem.

**Solution**

- Tracheids
- Vessels
- Xylem fibres
- Xylem parenchyma

---

### Example 3

Why does secondary growth occur only in dicot stems?

**Solution**

Dicot stems possess a vascular cambium, which produces secondary xylem and secondary phloem, increasing the girth. Monocot stems lack cambium and therefore generally do not undergo secondary growth.

---

### Example 4

State one anatomical difference between a dicot and a monocot root.

**Solution**

Dicot roots usually have **tetraarch xylem** and a small pith, whereas monocot roots have **polyarch xylem** and a large, well-developed pith.

---

### Example 5

What are bulliform cells?

**Solution**

Bulliform cells are large, thin-walled epidermal cells present in the upper epidermis of monocot leaves. They help in leaf rolling during water stress, reducing water loss.

---

### Example 6

What is the function of lenticels?

**Solution**

Lenticels facilitate the exchange of gases between the internal tissues of woody stems and the external environment. They also permit a small amount of transpiration.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **parenchyma**, **collenchyma**, and **sclerenchyma** based on cell wall thickness and living status.
- Assuming that all vascular bundles contain cambium; **monocot stems** have **closed vascular bundles** without cambium.
- Mixing up the arrangement of vascular bundles in **dicot** and **monocot** stems.
- Confusing **secondary growth** with **primary growth**.
- Forgetting that **bulliform cells** are characteristic of monocot leaves.
- Assuming that xylem is entirely made of dead cells; **xylem parenchyma** is living.
- Confusing **cork cambium** with **vascular cambium**.
- Believing that monocot roots undergo secondary growth under normal conditions.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Plant tissues are broadly classified into **meristematic** and **permanent** tissues.
- **Meristematic tissues** (apical, intercalary, and lateral) are responsible for plant growth through continuous cell division.
- **Permanent tissues** include **simple tissues** (parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma) and **complex tissues** (xylem and phloem), each performing specialized functions.
- The **dicot root** typically has **tetraarch xylem**, a small pith, and may undergo secondary growth, whereas the **monocot root** has **polyarch xylem**, a large pith, and lacks secondary growth.
- In **dicot stems**, vascular bundles are arranged in a ring and contain cambium, while **monocot stems** have scattered, closed vascular bundles.
- **Dorsiventral (dicot) leaves** have differentiated mesophyll and stomata mainly on the lower surface, whereas **isobilateral (monocot) leaves** have undifferentiated mesophyll, stomata on both surfaces, and bulliform cells.
- **Secondary growth** increases the girth of dicot stems through the activity of the **vascular cambium** and **cork cambium**, resulting in the formation of secondary tissues, bark, annual rings, and lenticels.
- Knowledge of plant anatomy helps explain the internal organization, growth, transport, and adaptation of flowering plants.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **tendons** with **ligaments**.
- Assuming all epithelial tissues perform the same function; different types are specialized for protection, absorption, secretion, or movement.
- Mixing up **skeletal**, **smooth**, and **cardiac** muscles.
- Forgetting that the cockroach has an **open circulatory system**.
- Assuming oxygen is transported by blood in insects; in cockroaches, oxygen is delivered directly through the **tracheal system**.
- Confusing **Malpighian tubules** (excretory organs) with **tracheae** (respiratory organs).
- Forgetting that cockroaches are **uricotelic** and excrete **uric acid**.
- Confusing the **crop** (food storage) with the **gizzard** (food grinding).

# Chapter Summary

- Animal tissues are classified into **epithelial**, **connective**, **muscular**, and **nervous** tissues, each performing specialized functions in the body.
- **Epithelial tissue** provides protection and participates in absorption and secretion, while **connective tissue** supports and binds organs together.
- **Muscular tissue** enables movement, and **nervous tissue** coordinates body activities through the transmission of nerve impulses.
- The **cockroach (_Periplaneta americana_)** is a representative arthropod with a segmented body divided into **head, thorax,** and **abdomen**.
- Its digestive system consists of the **mouth, crop, gizzard, midgut, hindgut,** and **anus**, with digestion and absorption occurring mainly in the midgut.
- Cockroaches possess an **open circulatory system**, a **tracheal respiratory system**, **Malpighian tubules** for excretion, and a **double ventral nerve cord** for coordination.

- Biomolecules are the chemical substances that constitute living organisms and are classified into **inorganic** (water, minerals, gases) and **organic** (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids) compounds.
- **Carbohydrates** serve as the primary energy source and occur as monosaccharides, disaccharides, and polysaccharides such as starch, glycogen, cellulose, and chitin.
- **Proteins** are polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds and exhibit four levels of structural organization: primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary.
- **Lipids** function in energy storage, membrane formation, insulation, and hormone synthesis.
- **Nucleic acids** (DNA and RNA) store, transmit, and express genetic information through nucleotides.
- **Enzymes** are biological catalysts that increase the rate of biochemical reactions and function according to substrate specificity, with their activity influenced by temperature, pH, substrate concentration, enzyme concentration, and inhibitors.
- Proteins may undergo **denaturation**, resulting in the loss of their functional three-dimensional structure.
- **Primary metabolites** are essential for growth, whereas **secondary metabolites** play important ecological and defensive roles.
- The study of biomolecules provides the molecular basis for understanding metabolism, genetics, physiology, biotechnology, and all life processes.

# Introduction

Growth, development, repair, and reproduction in living organisms depend on the ability of cells to divide. The **cell cycle** is the sequence of events through which a cell grows, duplicates its genetic material, and divides to produce new daughter cells.

This chapter explains the **cell cycle**, **mitosis**, **meiosis**, cytokinesis, significance of cell division, and the differences between mitosis and meiosis.

---

# 1. Cell Cycle

The **cell cycle** is the series of events that occur from one cell division to the next.

It consists of two major phases:

```
Cell Cycle
│
├── Interphase
└── M Phase (Mitotic Phase)
```

---

# 2. Interphase

Interphase is the longest phase of the cell cycle.

It is often called the **preparatory phase** because the cell grows and duplicates its DNA.

Interphase occupies about **95%** of the total cell cycle.

---

## Phases of Interphase

```
Interphase
│
├── G₁ Phase
├── S Phase
└── G₂ Phase
```

---

### (A) G₁ Phase (First Gap Phase)

This is the first growth phase.

#### Events

- Cell grows in size.
- Proteins and RNA are synthesized.
- Organelles increase in number.
- Cell prepares for DNA replication.

---

### (B) S Phase (Synthesis Phase)

DNA replication occurs during this phase.

#### Events

- DNA doubles.
- Histone proteins are synthesized.
- Chromosome number remains the same.
- DNA content becomes double.

---

### (C) G₂ Phase (Second Gap Phase)

The cell prepares for mitosis.

#### Events

- ATP synthesis.
- Protein synthesis.
- Spindle proteins are produced.
- Cell becomes ready for division.

---

# 3. Quiescent Stage (G₀ Phase)

Some cells temporarily or permanently leave the cell cycle and enter the **G₀ phase**.

### Characteristics

- Cell remains metabolically active.
- Cell does not divide.
- Seen in neurons and mature muscle cells.

---

# 4. M Phase (Mitotic Phase)

The M phase includes:

- Karyokinesis (division of nucleus)
- Cytokinesis (division of cytoplasm)

---

# 5. Mitosis

Mitosis is also called **equational division** because the chromosome number remains unchanged in the daughter cells.

Occurs in:

Why is mitosis called equational division?

**Solution**

Mitosis is called equational division because the chromosome number remains unchanged. A diploid parent cell produces two genetically identical diploid daughter cells.

### Example 2

During which phase does DNA replication occur?

**Solution**

DNA replication occurs during the **S (Synthesis) phase** of interphase.

What is the significance of crossing over?

**Solution**

Crossing over exchanges genetic material between homologous chromosomes, producing new gene combinations and increasing genetic variation among offspring.

---

Differentiate between cleavage furrow and cell plate formation.

**Solution**

In animal cells, cytokinesis occurs by the inward formation of a **cleavage furrow**. In plant cells, a **cell plate** develops at the centre and grows outward to divide the cell.

**Solution**

State two differences between mitosis and meiosis.

**Solution**

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing the **S phase** with the **M phase**; DNA replication occurs only during the **S phase**.
- Assuming chromosome number doubles during the S phase; only the **DNA content** doubles, while the chromosome number remains the same.
- Mixing up **mitosis** and **meiosis**.
- Forgetting that **crossing over** occurs during **Pachytene** of **Prophase I**.
- Confusing **synapsis** with **crossing over**; synapsis is pairing of homologous chromosomes, whereas crossing over is exchange of genetic material.
- Assuming centromeres divide during **Anaphase I**; they divide only during **Anaphase II** (and mitotic anaphase).
- Mixing up **cleavage furrow** and **cell plate** formation during cytokinesis.
- Forgetting the correct sequence of Prophase I substages: **Leptotene → Zygotene → Pachytene → Diplotene → Diakinesis**.

---

# Chapter Summary

- The **cell cycle** consists of **Interphase (G₁, S, G₂)** and the **M phase**, during which the cell grows, replicates DNA, and divides.
- During the **S phase**, DNA replication occurs, preparing the cell for division.
- Some cells enter the **G₀ phase**, where they remain metabolically active but do not divide.
- **Mitosis** is an equational division involving **Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase**, and **Cytokinesis**, producing two genetically identical diploid daughter cells.
- Cytokinesis occurs through **cleavage furrow formation** in animal cells and **cell plate formation** in plant cells.
- **Meiosis** consists of two successive divisions, producing four genetically distinct haploid cells.
- **Prophase I** is the longest stage of meiosis and includes **Leptotene, Zygotene, Pachytene, Diplotene,** and **Diakinesis**, during which **synapsis** and **crossing over** occur.
- **Crossing over** generates genetic variation and contributes to evolution.
- Cell division is essential for growth, tissue repair, reproduction, maintenance of chromosome number, and continuity of life.

# Introduction

Plants require a continuous supply of **water, minerals, and food** for growth and survival. These substances are transported through specialized tissues called **xylem** and **phloem**. Water and minerals are absorbed by the roots and transported to different parts of the plant, while food prepared during photosynthesis is distributed to all living cells.

This chapter explains the mechanisms of **diffusion, osmosis, plasmolysis, water absorption, transpiration, ascent of sap, mineral absorption, and translocation of food**.

---

# 1. Means of Transport

Plants transport substances through the following processes:

```
Transport Processes
│
├── Diffusion
├── Facilitated Diffusion
├── Osmosis
├── Active Transport
└── Bulk Flow
```

---

# 2. Diffusion

Diffusion is the movement of molecules from a region of **higher concentration** to a region of **lower concentration** until equilibrium is reached.

## Characteristics

- Passive process.
- No energy required.
- Occurs in gases, liquids, and dissolved substances.
- Continues until equilibrium.

---

## Examples

- Exchange of gases through stomata.
- Movement of oxygen and carbon dioxide.

---

# 3. Facilitated Diffusion

Facilitated diffusion is the movement of molecules across a membrane with the help of **transport proteins**.

## Characteristics

- Passive process.
- No ATP required.
- Uses carrier or channel proteins.
- Highly specific.

---

## Examples

- Movement of glucose.
- Transport of certain ions through membrane channels.

---

# 4. Osmosis

Osmosis is the movement of water through a **selectively permeable membrane** from a region of **higher water potential** to a region of **lower water potential**.

---

## Conditions Necessary

- Presence of a selectively permeable membrane.
- Difference in water potential.
- Presence of water molecules.

---

# 5. Types of Solutions

## (A) Hypotonic Solution

- Higher water potential than the cell.
- Water enters the cell.
- Cell becomes **turgid**.

---

## (B) Hypertonic Solution

- Lower water potential than the cell.
- Water leaves the cell.
- Cell undergoes **plasmolysis**.

---

## (C) Isotonic Solution

- Equal water potential inside and outside the cell.
- No net movement of water.

---

# Comparison of Solutions

| Solution   | Water Movement  | Effect on Cell |
| ---------- | --------------- | -------------- |
| Hypotonic  | Into the cell   | Turgid         |
| Hypertonic | Out of the cell | Plasmolysed    |
| Isotonic   | No net movement | Unchanged      |

---

## Deplasmolysis

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Differentiate between diffusion and osmosis.

**Solution**

Diffusion is the movement of molecules from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration and does not necessarily require a membrane. Osmosis is the movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane from higher to lower water potential.

### Example 2

Why does plasmolysis occur?

**Solution**

Plasmolysis occurs when a plant cell is placed in a hypertonic solution, causing water to move out of the cell by osmosis. The protoplast shrinks away from the cell wall.

### Example 3

State the significance of transpiration.

**Solution**

Transpiration helps in generating transpiration pull, transporting minerals, cooling the plant, and maintaining water balance.

---

### Example 4

Explain the Cohesion-Tension Theory.

**Solution**

According to Dixon and Joly, water molecules remain connected by cohesion and adhere to xylem walls. Transpiration from leaves creates a pulling force (transpiration pull), which lifts the continuous column of water upward through the xylem.

---

### Example 5

What is the Pressure Flow Hypothesis?

**Solution**

The Pressure Flow Hypothesis, proposed by Ernst Münch, explains that sugars are actively loaded into phloem at the source, creating high osmotic pressure. This drives the movement of food solution toward sink regions where sugars are unloaded.

---

### Example 6

Differentiate between transpiration and guttation.

**Solution**

Transpiration is the loss of water as vapour through stomata, whereas guttation is the loss of liquid water through hydathodes, usually during early morning when root pressure is high.

- Confusing **diffusion** with **osmosis**.
- Assuming osmosis can occur without a selectively permeable membrane.
- Mixing up **plasmolysis** and **deplasmolysis**.
- Confusing the **apoplast** and **symplast** pathways.
- Forgetting that **stomatal transpiration** accounts for the majority of water loss.
- Mixing up **transpiration** with **guttation**.
- Assuming food transport occurs through xylem; food is transported by **phloem**.
- Forgetting that the **Cohesion-Tension Theory** was proposed by **Dixon and Joly**, while the **Pressure Flow Hypothesis** was proposed by **Ernst Münch**.

# Chapter Summary

- Plants transport substances through **diffusion**, **facilitated diffusion**, **osmosis**, **active transport**, and **bulk flow**.
- **Osmosis** is the movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane from higher to lower water potential, leading to conditions such as **turgidity**, **plasmolysis**, and **deplasmolysis**.
- Water is absorbed mainly by **root hairs** and moves through the **apoplast** and **symplast** pathways before entering the xylem.
- **Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from aerial plant parts and is essential for cooling, mineral transport, and generating **transpiration pull**.
- According to the **Cohesion-Tension Theory**, water ascends through the xylem due to cohesion, adhesion, and the tension created by transpiration.
- Mineral ions are absorbed either passively or actively, depending on the concentration gradient and energy requirement.
- Food synthesized in leaves is translocated through the **phloem** according to the **Pressure Flow Hypothesis**, moving from **source** to **sink**.
- **Guttation** is the exudation of liquid water through hydathodes under conditions of high root pressure.
- Efficient transport systems ensure the distribution of water, minerals, and food necessary for plant growth, metabolism, and survival.

# Introduction

Plants require **mineral elements** in addition to water, carbon dioxide, and sunlight for proper growth and development. These minerals are absorbed from the soil through roots and participate in various physiological and biochemical processes.

Depending on the quantity required, mineral nutrients are classified into **macronutrients** and **micronutrients**. A deficiency or excess of these nutrients affects plant growth, metabolism, and productivity.

# 1. Mineral Nutrition

**Mineral nutrition** is the study of the absorption, transport, assimilation, and functions of mineral elements in plants.

- Water

# 2. Essential Mineral Elements

An element is considered **essential** if:

- It is absolutely necessary for normal growth and reproduction.
- Its deficiency prevents the plant from completing its life cycle.
- It has a direct role in plant metabolism.
- Its function cannot be replaced by another element.

These criteria were proposed by **Arnon and Stout (1939).**

A mineral element is essential if:

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **macronutrients** with **micronutrients** based on their importance rather than the quantity required.
- Assuming **iron** is a constituent of chlorophyll; it is required for chlorophyll synthesis but is **not** part of the chlorophyll molecule.
- Mixing up **mobile** and **immobile** mineral elements.
- Forgetting that **leghemoglobin** protects **nitrogenase** by maintaining a low oxygen concentration.
- Confusing **nitrification**, **ammonification**, and **denitrification**.
- Assuming plants absorb atmospheric nitrogen directly; most plants absorb nitrogen mainly as **nitrate** or **ammonium ions**.
- Forgetting the role of **Rhizobium** in symbiotic nitrogen fixation.
- Confusing **hydroponics** with **aeroponics**.

- Plants require essential **mineral elements** for normal growth, development, and completion of their life cycle.
- Essential elements are classified into **macronutrients** and **micronutrients** based on the quantity required.
- **Hydroponics** is a useful technique for studying mineral nutrition and identifying nutrient deficiencies.
- Each mineral performs specific functions, including enzyme activation, chlorophyll formation, protein synthesis, membrane stability, and energy transfer.
- Deficiency symptoms such as **chlorosis**, **necrosis**, and **stunted growth** vary depending on the nutrient and its mobility within the plant.
- The **nitrogen cycle** involves nitrogen fixation, ammonification, nitrification, assimilation, and denitrification, ensuring the continuous availability of nitrogen in ecosystems.
- **Biological nitrogen fixation** is carried out by microorganisms such as **Rhizobium**, using the enzyme **nitrogenase**, which functions under low oxygen conditions maintained by **leghemoglobin**.
- Plants absorb nitrogen mainly in the form of **nitrate (NO₃⁻)** and **ammonium (NH₄⁺)** and convert it into amino acids and proteins.
- Understanding mineral nutrition is essential for improving agricultural productivity, maintaining soil fertility, and ensuring healthy plant growth.

- Confusing the **light reaction** with the **Calvin cycle**.

- Mixing up the reaction centres of the photosystems: **P680** belongs to **Photosystem II**, while **P700** belongs to **Photosystem I**.
- Assuming the **dark reaction** occurs only in darkness; it does not require light directly but depends on ATP and NADPH produced during the light reaction.
- Confusing **RuBP** (CO₂ acceptor in C₃ plants) with **PEP** (CO₂ acceptor in C₄ plants).
- Forgetting that the first stable product of the **Calvin cycle** is **3-PGA**, whereas the first stable product of the **C₄ pathway** is **OAA**.

- **Photosynthesis** is the process by which green plants convert light energy into chemical energy, producing carbohydrates from carbon dioxide and water.
- The process occurs in **chloroplasts**, where **light reactions** take place in the **grana** and the **Calvin cycle** occurs in the **stroma**.
- **Chlorophyll a** is the primary photosynthetic pigment, while chlorophyll b, carotenoids, and xanthophylls function as accessory pigments.
- During the **light reaction**, water undergoes **photolysis**, ATP and NADPH are synthesized, and oxygen is released.
- **Photophosphorylation** may be **cyclic** or **non-cyclic**, depending on the participation of the photosystems.
- The **Calvin cycle (C₃ pathway)** fixes carbon dioxide using **RuBP**, whereas the **C₄ pathway** initially fixes carbon dioxide using **PEP**, reducing photorespiration and improving efficiency.
- **CAM plants** open their stomata at night to conserve water in dry environments.
- **Photorespiration** decreases photosynthetic efficiency and is much more pronounced in C₃ plants than in C₄ plants.
- Environmental factors such as light, carbon dioxide concentration, temperature, and water availability, along with internal plant factors, regulate the rate of photosynthesis according to **Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors**.

All living organisms require energy to perform vital life processes such as growth, reproduction, transport, biosynthesis, and movement. # Solved Examples

### Example 1

Why is glycolysis called the EMP pathway?

**Solution**

Glycolysis is called the EMP pathway because it was discovered and described by **Embden, Meyerhof, and Parnas**.

---

### Example 2

State the site of the Krebs cycle.

**Solution**

The Krebs cycle occurs in the **mitochondrial matrix** of eukaryotic cells.

---

### Example 3

Differentiate between aerobic and anaerobic respiration.

**Solution**

Aerobic respiration requires oxygen, completely oxidizes glucose, and produces a large amount of ATP. Anaerobic respiration occurs without oxygen, partially oxidizes glucose, and produces much less ATP.

---

### Example 4

Why is oxygen called the final electron acceptor?

**Solution**

In the electron transport system, oxygen accepts electrons and hydrogen ions at the end of the chain to form water, allowing electron transport and ATP synthesis to continue.

---

### Example 5

Calculate the respiratory quotient (RQ) for carbohydrate respiration.

**Solution**

For carbohydrates:

```
RQ = CO₂ Produced / O₂ Consumed = 6 / 6 = 1
```

Therefore, the respiratory quotient of carbohydrates is **1.0**.

---

### Example 6

Why is respiration considered an amphibolic pathway?

**Solution**

Respiration is amphibolic because it participates in both the breakdown of food molecules (catabolism) and the synthesis of important biomolecules such as amino acids and fatty acids (anabolism).

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **photosynthesis** with **respiration**.
- Assuming glycolysis occurs in mitochondria; it occurs in the **cytoplasm**.
- Forgetting that the **Krebs cycle** occurs in the **mitochondrial matrix**.
- Mixing up the **link reaction** with the **Krebs cycle**.
- Assuming oxygen is required for glycolysis; glycolysis itself does **not** directly require oxygen.
- Confusing **alcoholic fermentation** with **lactic acid fermentation**.
- Forgetting that **oxygen** is the **final electron acceptor** in aerobic respiration.
- Assuming every cell produces exactly 38 ATP from one glucose molecule; in eukaryotic cells, the yield is generally **about 32 ATP**.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Respiration** is the process by which living cells oxidize food molecules to release energy in the form of ATP.
- It may occur as **aerobic respiration**, requiring oxygen and producing carbon dioxide and water, or as **anaerobic respiration**, producing ethanol or lactic acid with a lower ATP yield.
- **Glycolysis (EMP pathway)** occurs in the cytoplasm and converts glucose into two molecules of pyruvate with a net gain of ATP and NADH.
- Pyruvate undergoes the **link reaction** to form acetyl CoA, which enters the **Krebs cycle** in the mitochondrial matrix for complete oxidation.
- The **electron transport system (ETS)** on the inner mitochondrial membrane transfers electrons to oxygen, the final electron acceptor, driving ATP synthesis through **oxidative phosphorylation**.
- Cellular respiration functions as an **amphibolic pathway**, participating in both energy production and biosynthesis.
- The **respiratory quotient (RQ)** varies depending on the respiratory substrate being oxidized.
- Temperature, oxygen availability, water, respiratory substrates, and enzyme activity influence the rate of respiration.
- Respiration supplies the energy required for all metabolic activities, making it essential for plant growth, maintenance, and survival.

# Introduction

Plants exhibit continuous growth throughout their life because they possess **meristematic tissues** that retain the ability to divide. Growth is influenced by both **internal factors** (genes and hormones) and **external factors** (light, water, temperature, nutrients, and oxygen).

Plant development is the sum of **growth**, **differentiation**, and **maturation**, regulated by plant growth regulators (PGRs) and environmental conditions.

---

# 1. Plant Growth

## Definition

Plant growth is a **permanent and irreversible increase** in size, volume, dry weight, and number of cells resulting from cell division and cell enlargement.

---

## Characteristics

- Permanent increase in size.
- Irreversible process.
- Requires energy.
- Influenced by internal and external factors.
- Occurs mainly in meristematic regions.

Why is ABA called the stress hormone?

In arithmetic growth, only one daughter cell continues to divide, producing a constant growth rate. In geometric growth, both daughter cells continue dividing, resulting in exponential growth.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **growth** with **development**; development includes growth, differentiation, and maturation.
- Mixing up the phases of growth: **meristematic**, **elongation**, and **maturation**.
- Assuming **auxins** promote cell division; they mainly promote **cell elongation**, while **cytokinins** primarily stimulate cell division.
- Forgetting that **ABA** is mainly a growth inhibitor and stress hormone.
- Confusing **photoperiodism** (response to day length) with **vernalization** (response to low temperature).
- Assuming **ethylene** is a liquid hormone; it is a **gaseous** plant hormone.
- Mixing up **dedifferentiation** and **redifferentiation**.
- Forgetting that **phytochrome** is the photoreceptor involved in photoperiodism.

# Chapter Summary

- Plant growth is a permanent and irreversible increase in size and dry mass, brought about by the activity of **meristematic tissues**.
- Growth occurs in three phases: **meristematic**, **elongation**, and **maturation**, and may follow **arithmetic** or **geometric** patterns, producing a characteristic **sigmoid growth curve**.
- **Differentiation**, **dedifferentiation**, and **redifferentiation** are essential processes in plant development.
- Plant growth and development are regulated by five major **plant growth regulators (PGRs)**: **auxins**, **gibberellins**, **cytokinins**, **abscisic acid (ABA)**, and **ethylene**, each performing specific physiological functions.
- **Photoperiodism**, regulated by the pigment **phytochrome**, controls flowering based on day length, while **vernalization** promotes flowering through exposure to low temperatures.
- **Seed dormancy** prevents premature germination and can be broken by treatments such as scarification, stratification, and gibberellin application.
- Plant development is influenced by genetic makeup, hormones, and environmental factors, ensuring proper growth, reproduction, and adaptation.

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Why is hydrochloric acid secreted in the stomach?

**Solution**

Hydrochloric acid provides an acidic medium for the activation of pepsinogen into pepsin, kills harmful microorganisms, and maintains the optimum pH for gastric enzymes.

---

### Example 2

Differentiate between bile and pancreatic juice.

**Solution**

Bile contains no digestive enzymes and mainly emulsifies fats, whereas pancreatic juice contains enzymes that digest carbohydrates, proteins, fats, and nucleic acids.

---

### Example 3

What is the role of villi in digestion?

**Solution**

Villi increase the surface area of the small intestine, enabling efficient absorption of digested nutrients into blood capillaries and lacteals.

---

### Example 4

How are fats digested in the small intestine?

**Solution**

Bile salts first emulsify fats into tiny droplets, increasing the surface area. Pancreatic lipase then breaks them down into fatty acids and glycerol, which are absorbed through the lacteals.

---

### Example 5

What is peristalsis?

**Solution**

Peristalsis is the wave-like contraction and relaxation of the muscles of the alimentary canal that pushes food forward through the digestive tract.

---

### Example 6

Why are lacteals important?

**Solution**

Lacteals are lymphatic vessels present inside intestinal villi. They absorb fatty acids, glycerol, and fat-soluble vitamins before transporting them into the lymphatic system.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **digestion** with **absorption**; digestion breaks complex food into simpler molecules, whereas absorption transfers these molecules into blood or lymph.
- Assuming **bile contains digestive enzymes**; bile contains bile salts and pigments but **no digestive enzymes**.
- Forgetting that **pepsinogen** is inactive and is activated by **hydrochloric acid** to form pepsin.
- Mixing up the functions of the **liver** and **pancreas**.
- Assuming digestion is completed in the stomach; most digestion and nearly all absorption occur in the **small intestine**.
- Confusing **villi** with **microvilli**.
- Forgetting that **fatty acids and glycerol** are absorbed through **lacteals**, while glucose and amino acids enter **blood capillaries**.
- Assuming the adult human dental formula represents the total number of teeth on both jaws together; **2123/2123** represents one half of the upper and lower jaws.

---

# Chapter Summary

- The **digestive system** consists of the alimentary canal and associated digestive glands, which work together to digest food, absorb nutrients, and eliminate waste.
- Digestion begins in the **mouth**, where salivary amylase initiates starch digestion, continues in the **stomach** with protein digestion by pepsin, and is completed in the **small intestine** through the action of pancreatic and intestinal enzymes.
- The **liver** produces bile, which emulsifies fats, while the **pancreas** secretes enzymes for the digestion of carbohydrates, proteins, fats, and nucleic acids.
- The **small intestine**, with its **villi** and **microvilli**, provides a large surface area for efficient absorption of nutrients.
- Glucose and amino acids are absorbed into **blood capillaries**, whereas fatty acids, glycerol, and fat-soluble vitamins enter the **lacteals**.
- Absorbed nutrients are utilized by body cells through **assimilation**, and undigested food is eliminated by **egestion**.
- Disorders such as **indigestion**, **constipation**, **vomiting**, **jaundice**, and **diarrhoea** affect the normal functioning of the digestive system.
- Efficient digestion and absorption ensure a continuous supply of energy and nutrients necessary for growth, repair, and maintenance of the human body.

# Introduction

Respiration is the process by which living organisms obtain energy from food. **Breathing** is the physical process of taking in oxygen-rich air and expelling carbon dioxide-rich air. Oxygen is essential for cellular respiration, while carbon dioxide produced during metabolism must be removed from the body.

The respiratory system consists of specialized organs that facilitate the exchange of gases between the atmosphere and body tissues.

# 8. Inspiration

Inspiration is the process of taking air into the lungs.

### Events

- Diaphragm contracts and flattens.
- External intercostal muscles contract.
- Ribs move upward and outward.
- Thoracic cavity volume increases.
- Intrapulmonary pressure decreases.
- Air enters the lungs.

Expiration is the process of expelling air from the lungs.

### Events

- Diaphragm relaxes.
- External intercostal muscles relax.
- Ribs move downward and inward.
- Thoracic cavity volume decreases.
- Intrapulmonary pressure increases.
- Air is forced out.

# 12. Exchange of Gases

Gas exchange occurs by **simple diffusion**.

---

## External Respiration

Occurs between:

- Alveoli
- Blood capillaries

### Oxygen

Moves from alveoli to blood.

### Carbon Dioxide

Moves from blood to alveoli.

---

## Internal Respiration

Occurs between:

- Blood
- Body tissues

Oxygen diffuses into cells, while carbon dioxide diffuses into blood.

---

# 13. Transport of Oxygen

About **97%** of oxygen is transported by haemoglobin in the form of **oxyhaemoglobin**.

```
Hb + O₂ ⇌ HbO₂
```

About **3%** is transported dissolved in plasma.

---

# Oxyhaemoglobin Dissociation Curve

The oxygen-haemoglobin relationship is represented by a **sigmoid (S-shaped) curve**.

Factors promoting oxygen release:

- High CO₂ concentration
- High temperature
- High H⁺ concentration (low pH)
- Low oxygen concentration

This is known as the **Bohr Effect**.

---

# 14. Transport of Carbon Dioxide

Carbon dioxide is transported in three forms.

| Form                        | Percentage   |
| --------------------------- | ------------ |
| As bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻) | About 70%    |
| As carbaminohaemoglobin     | About 20–23% |
| Dissolved in plasma         | About 7%     |

---

# 15. Chloride Shift (Hamburger Phenomenon)

When bicarbonate ions leave red blood cells, chloride ions enter to maintain electrical neutrality.

This exchange is called the **chloride shift**.

---

# 16. Regulation of Respiration

Respiration is controlled by the **respiratory centres** in the medulla oblongata and pons.

### Medullary Respiratory Centre

- Regulates the normal rhythm of breathing.

### Pneumotaxic Centre (Pons)

- Regulates the duration of inspiration.

---

## Chemoreceptors

Located in:

- Carotid bodies
- Aortic bodies

They detect:

- Increased CO₂
- Increased H⁺ concentration
- Decreased O₂ (to a lesser extent)

An increase in carbon dioxide is the strongest stimulus for breathing.

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Why are alveoli considered the functional units of the lungs?

**Solution**

Alveoli provide a very large surface area with thin walls and an extensive capillary network, allowing rapid diffusion of oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out of the blood.

---

### Example 2

Differentiate between inspiration and expiration.

**Solution**

During inspiration, the diaphragm contracts, thoracic cavity volume increases, pressure decreases, and air enters the lungs. During expiration, the diaphragm relaxes, thoracic cavity volume decreases, pressure increases, and air leaves the lungs.

---

### Example 3

Why is haemoglobin important in oxygen transport?

**Solution**

Haemoglobin reversibly combines with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin, enabling about 97% of oxygen to be transported efficiently from the lungs to body tissues.

---

### Example 4

What is the Bohr Effect?

**Solution**

The Bohr Effect refers to the increased release of oxygen from oxyhaemoglobin when carbon dioxide concentration, hydrogen ion concentration, or temperature increases, or when oxygen concentration decreases.

---

### Example 5

State the role of bicarbonate ions in carbon dioxide transport.

**Solution**

About 70% of carbon dioxide is transported in the blood as bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻), making it the major form of carbon dioxide transport.

---

### Example 6

Why does smoking increase the risk of emphysema?

**Solution**

Smoking damages the alveolar walls, reducing the respiratory surface area available for gas exchange. This decreases the efficiency of oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide removal.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **breathing** with **cellular respiration**.
- Assuming the **left lung** has three lobes; it has **two**, while the **right lung** has **three**.
- Forgetting that **alveoli** are the primary sites of gaseous exchange.
- Mixing up **tidal volume** with **vital capacity**.
- Assuming oxygen is mainly transported dissolved in plasma; about **97%** is transported by **haemoglobin**.
- Forgetting that most carbon dioxide is transported as **bicarbonate ions**, not as carbaminohaemoglobin.
- Confusing **asthma** with **emphysema**.
- Assuming oxygen concentration is the main regulator of breathing; **increased carbon dioxide concentration** is the primary stimulus under normal conditions.

---

# Chapter Summary

- The **respiratory system** consists of conducting passages and respiratory structures that facilitate the exchange of gases between the atmosphere and the body.
- Air passes through the **nostrils, nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles**, and finally reaches the **alveoli**, where gaseous exchange occurs.
- **Inspiration** and **expiration** are brought about by coordinated movements of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, producing pressure changes that move air into and out of the lungs.
- Respiratory volumes and capacities, such as **tidal volume**, **vital capacity**, and **total lung capacity**, help assess lung function.
- Oxygen is transported mainly as **oxyhaemoglobin**, while carbon dioxide is transported predominantly as **bicarbonate ions**.
- Gas exchange occurs by **simple diffusion**, driven by differences in the partial pressures of oxygen and carbon dioxide.
- The **medulla oblongata** and **pons** regulate breathing, with carbon dioxide concentration acting as the principal stimulus.
- Disorders such as **asthma**, **emphysema**, and occupational lung diseases impair respiratory efficiency and reduce the body's ability to exchange gases effectively.

What is the role of platelets in blood clotting?

Platelets adhere to the site of injury and release clotting factors that initiate the coagulation cascade, leading to the formation of a fibrin clot and preventing excessive blood loss.

---

### Example 6

Calculate the cardiac output of a person with a stroke volume of 70 mL and a heart rate of 72 beats per minute.

**Solution**

Using the formula:

```
Cardiac Output = Stroke Volume × Heart Rate
```

```
= 70 mL × 72/min
= 5040 mL/min
≈ 5.0 L/min
```

Therefore, the cardiac output is approximately **5 litres per minute**.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **plasma** with **serum**; serum is plasma without clotting factors.
- Assuming all arteries carry oxygenated blood; the **pulmonary artery** carries deoxygenated blood.
- Assuming all veins carry deoxygenated blood; the **pulmonary veins** carry oxygenated blood.
- Mixing up the **tricuspid** and **bicuspid (mitral)** valves.
- Forgetting that the **SA node** is the natural pacemaker of the heart.
- Confusing the **P wave**, **QRS complex**, and **T wave** in an ECG.
- Assuming lymph contains red blood cells; lymph normally **does not contain RBCs**.
- Mixing up **hypertension**, **coronary artery disease**, **angina**, and **heart failure**.

---

# Chapter Summary

- The **circulatory system** consists of the heart, blood, blood vessels, and lymphatic system, ensuring the transport of gases, nutrients, hormones, and wastes throughout the body.
- **Blood** is composed of **plasma** and **formed elements** (RBCs, WBCs, and platelets). RBCs transport oxygen, WBCs provide immunity, and platelets help in blood clotting.
- The **ABO** and **Rh** blood group systems are important for safe blood transfusions and pregnancy management.
- **Lymph** returns excess tissue fluid to the bloodstream, transports absorbed fats, and contributes to immune defence.
- The **human heart** has four chambers and a specialized conducting system, with the **SA node** acting as the natural pacemaker.
- The **cardiac cycle** includes atrial systole, ventricular systole, and joint diastole, producing the characteristic **lub** and **dub** heart sounds.
- The **ECG** records the electrical activity of the heart and helps assess cardiac function.
- **Arteries**, **veins**, and **capillaries** differ in structure and function, enabling efficient circulation.
- Humans exhibit **double circulation**, ensuring complete separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood and supporting efficient metabolism.
- Disorders such as **hypertension**, **coronary artery disease**, **angina pectoris**, and **heart failure** affect cardiovascular health and require timely diagnosis and management.

# Introduction

Metabolic activities in the body produce waste substances that can be harmful if allowed to accumulate. The process of removing these wastes is called **excretion**. The human excretory system maintains the internal environment by eliminating nitrogenous wastes, regulating water and electrolyte balance, and maintaining acid-base balance.

The principal excretory organs are the **kidneys**, which produce urine through millions of microscopic filtering units called **nephrons**.

---

# 1. Excretion

## Definition

Excretion is the process of removing metabolic waste products and excess substances from the body.

---

## Functions

- Removes nitrogenous wastes.
- Maintains water balance.
- Maintains electrolyte balance.
- Regulates blood pH.
- Maintains homeostasis.

The renal corpuscle consists of:

Cup-shaped structure that surrounds the glomerulus and receives the filtrate.

# 7. Parts of Renal Tubule

---

## Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT)

Functions

- Reabsorption of glucose.

- Reabsorption of water.

---

## Loop of Henle

### Descending Limb

- Permeable to water.

---

### Ascending Limb

- Impermeable to water.

---

Functions

Functions

# 10. Glomerular Filtration

Blood is filtered through the glomerulus into Bowman's capsule.

Average GFR:

```
Approximately 125 mL/min
```

Useful substances are reabsorbed into the blood.

Examples

- Glucose
- Amino acids
- Water
- Sodium
- Bicarbonate ions

Certain substances are actively secreted into the filtrate.

- Potassium ions
- Hydrogen ions
- Ammonia

- Creatinine

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Why are nephrons called the structural and functional units of the kidney?

**Solution**

Each nephron filters blood, reabsorbs useful substances, secretes unwanted substances, and produces urine. Since every kidney function is performed by nephrons, they are the structural and functional units of the kidney.

---

### Example 2

Differentiate between cortical and juxtamedullary nephrons.

**Solution**

Cortical nephrons have short loops of Henle and are more numerous, whereas juxtamedullary nephrons possess long loops of Henle that help produce highly concentrated urine.

---

### Example 3

What are the three major steps of urine formation?

**Solution**

Urine formation involves:

1. Glomerular filtration.
2. Tubular reabsorption.
3. Tubular secretion.

---

### Example 4

What is the role of ADH?

**Solution**

ADH increases the permeability of the collecting ducts to water, promoting water reabsorption and producing concentrated urine during dehydration.

---

### Example 5

Explain the function of the counter current mechanism.

**Solution**

The counter current mechanism of the loop of Henle and vasa recta maintains a high osmotic gradient in the renal medulla, allowing maximum water reabsorption and the formation of concentrated urine.

---

### Example 6

What is haemodialysis?

**Solution**

Haemodialysis is an artificial method of removing metabolic wastes, excess salts, and water from the blood using a semipermeable membrane when the kidneys fail to function properly.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **excretion** with **egestion**; excretion removes metabolic wastes, whereas egestion removes undigested food.
- Assuming all animals excrete **urea**; many aquatic animals are **ammonotelic**, while birds and reptiles are **uricotelic**.
- Mixing up the **proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)** and the **distal convoluted tubule (DCT)**.
- Forgetting that the **loop of Henle** plays a major role in concentrating urine through the counter current mechanism.
- Assuming glucose is normally present in urine; under healthy conditions, glucose is almost completely reabsorbed.
- Confusing the actions of **ADH** and **aldosterone**.
- Forgetting that the **juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA)** secretes **renin**.
- Assuming haemodialysis cures kidney disease; it only replaces some kidney functions until recovery or transplantation.

# Chapter Summary

- **Excretion** is the removal of metabolic waste products, helping maintain homeostasis.
- Nitrogenous wastes include **ammonia**, **urea**, and **uric acid**, giving rise to ammonotelic, ureotelic, and uricotelic animals.
- The human excretory system consists of the **kidneys**, **ureters**, **urinary bladder**, and **urethra**.
- The **nephron** is the structural and functional unit of the kidney and performs **glomerular filtration**, **tubular reabsorption**, and **tubular secretion** to form urine.
- The **counter current mechanism** in the loop of Henle and vasa recta enables the kidneys to produce concentrated urine and conserve water.
- Hormones such as **ADH**, **aldosterone**, **renin**, and **ANF** regulate water balance, electrolyte balance, and blood pressure.
- The kidneys play a vital role in maintaining blood pH, osmotic balance, electrolyte balance, and overall internal homeostasis.
- **Haemodialysis** temporarily replaces kidney function in patients with severe renal failure.
- Disorders such as **uremia**, **kidney stones**, **glomerulonephritis**, and **acute renal failure** impair excretory function and require appropriate medical management.
