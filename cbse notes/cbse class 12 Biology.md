> **Board:** CBSE

> **Note:** This chapter explains the structure of flowers, formation of male and female gametophytes, pollination, fertilization, seed and fruit development, and special phenomena such as apomixis and polyembryony.

# Introduction

Sexual reproduction in flowering plants (angiosperms) involves the formation of male and female gametes, their transfer through pollination, fertilization, and the development of seeds and fruits. A unique feature of angiosperms is **double fertilization**, in which one male gamete fuses with the egg to form the zygote, while the other fuses with the polar nuclei to form the endosperm.

Sexual reproduction ensures **genetic variation**, adaptation, and continuity of plant species.

---

# 1. Flower: The Reproductive Organ

## Definition

A **flower** is the reproductive structure of angiosperms that bears the male and female reproductive organs.

---

## Parts of a Typical Flower

- **Calyx (Sepals):** Protects the flower bud.
- **Corolla (Petals):** Attracts pollinators.
- **Androecium:** Male reproductive whorl.
- **Gynoecium (Pistil/Carpel):** Female reproductive whorl.

# 3. Microsporogenesis

## Definition

The process of formation of **microspores (pollen grains)** from pollen mother cells (PMCs).

---

## Steps

1. Archesporial cell differentiates.
2. Forms sporogenous tissue.
3. Sporogenous cells become pollen mother cells.
4. Each PMC undergoes meiosis.
5. Four haploid microspores are formed (microspore tetrad).

---

## Importance

- Produces haploid pollen grains.
- Introduces genetic variation through meiosis.

---

# 4. Development of Male Gametophyte

A pollen grain develops into the male gametophyte.

---

- **Exine** – Outer wall made of sporopollenin.
- **Intine** – Inner cellulose-pectin layer.

Contains:

- Vegetative (tube) cell
- Generative cell

The generative cell divides mitotically to form **two male gametes**.

# 10. Pollination

## Definition

Transfer of pollen grains from anther to stigma.

---

## Types

### A. Autogamy

Pollen reaches stigma of the **same flower**.

Requirements:

- Bisexual flower
- Anther and stigma mature simultaneously.

---

### B. Geitonogamy

Transfer between different flowers of the **same plant**.

Genetically similar but requires a pollinating agent.

---

### C. Xenogamy

Transfer between flowers of **different plants** of the same species.

Produces maximum genetic variation.

---

# Comparison of Pollination Types

| Feature                          | Autogamy | Geitonogamy | Xenogamy |
| -------------------------------- | -------- | ----------- | -------- |
| Same flower                      | ✓        | ✗           | ✗        |
| Same plant                       | ✓        | ✓           | ✗        |
| Cross-pollination agent required | No       | Yes         | Yes      |
| Genetic variation                | Very low | Low         | High     |

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

**Question:** Why is double fertilization considered unique to angiosperms?

**Solution:**

Double fertilization involves:

- One male gamete fusing with the egg to form the diploid zygote (syngamy).
- The second male gamete fusing with the two polar nuclei to form the triploid primary endosperm nucleus (triple fusion).

This phenomenon occurs only in flowering plants (angiosperms).

---

### Example 2

**Question:** Differentiate between autogamy and xenogamy.

**Solution:**

| Autogamy                           | Xenogamy                                        |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Pollination within the same flower | Pollination between flowers of different plants |
| No pollinating agent required      | Pollinating agent required                      |
| Very little genetic variation      | High genetic variation                          |

---

### Example 3

**Question:** What is the function of the tapetum?

**Solution:**

The tapetum is the innermost layer of the anther wall. It nourishes developing pollen grains and supplies materials required for pollen wall formation.

---

### Example 4

**Question:** Why are synergids important?

**Solution:**

Synergids possess a **filiform apparatus**, which guides the pollen tube into the embryo sac and facilitates the discharge of male gametes.

---

### Example 5

**Question:** State one advantage of apomixis in agriculture.

**Solution:**

Apomixis allows the production of seeds without fertilization, preserving desirable hybrid characteristics across generations without repeated hybridization.

---

### Example 6

**Question:** What develops from the following after fertilization?

- Ovule
- Ovary
- Zygote
- Integuments

**Solution:**

| Structure   | Develops Into |
| ----------- | ------------- |
| Ovule       | Seed          |
| Ovary       | Fruit         |
| Zygote      | Embryo        |
| Integuments | Seed coat     |

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **microsporogenesis** (formation of microspores in the anther) with **megasporogenesis** (formation of megaspores in the ovule).
- Assuming **autogamy** and **geitonogamy** are identical; geitonogamy occurs between different flowers of the same plant.
- Forgetting that the mature embryo sac is **7-celled and 8-nucleate**.
- Mixing up **syngamy** and **triple fusion** during double fertilization.
- Incorrectly stating that the endosperm is diploid; in most angiosperms, it is **triploid (3n)**.
- Confusing **apomixis** with **parthenocarpy**; apomixis produces seeds without fertilization, while parthenocarpy produces fruits without fertilization.
- Forgetting the functions of the **tapetum** and **synergids**, which are commonly asked in examinations.
- Assuming all pollination by insects results in xenogamy; insect pollination can also cause geitonogamy.

---

# Chapter Summary

- The **flower** is the reproductive organ of angiosperms and consists of the androecium (male) and gynoecium (female).
- **Microsporogenesis** produces pollen grains (male gametophytes), while **megasporogenesis** produces the functional megaspore that develops into the embryo sac (female gametophyte).
- The mature embryo sac is **7-celled and 8-nucleate**, containing the egg apparatus, polar nuclei, and antipodal cells.
- Pollination may be **autogamy**, **geitonogamy**, or **xenogamy**, and is carried out by abiotic or biotic agents.
- Plants possess several **outbreeding devices** to prevent self-pollination and promote genetic diversity.
- During **pollen-pistil interaction**, the pollen tube grows through the style and delivers two male gametes into the embryo sac.
- **Double fertilization**, comprising **syngamy** and **triple fusion**, is a unique characteristic of flowering plants.
- After fertilization, the **zygote develops into the embryo**, the **primary endosperm nucleus forms the endosperm**, the **ovule becomes the seed**, and the **ovary develops into the fruit**.
- **Apomixis** and **polyembryony** are important reproductive phenomena with significant agricultural and horticultural applications.

# Introduction

Human reproduction is a **sexual mode of reproduction** involving the formation and fusion of male and female gametes. Fertilization results in the formation of a diploid zygote, which develops into an embryo and later into a fetus. Human reproduction is regulated by a complex interaction of hormones produced by the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, gonads, and placenta.

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

**Question:** Why are testes located outside the abdominal cavity?

**Solution:**

The testes are present in the scrotum, where the temperature is about **2–2.5°C lower** than body temperature. This lower temperature is essential for normal spermatogenesis.

### Example 3

**Question:** Name the site of fertilization in humans.

**Solution:**

Fertilization normally occurs at the **ampullary-isthmic junction** of the fallopian tube, where the sperm fuses with the secondary oocyte to form a diploid zygote.

### Example 4

**Question:** What is the function of the corpus luteum?

**Solution:**

The corpus luteum is formed from the ruptured Graafian follicle after ovulation. It secretes **progesterone**, which maintains the uterine endometrium for implantation and early pregnancy.

### Example 5

**Question:** What is the importance of colostrum?

**Solution:**

Colostrum is the first milk secreted after childbirth. It is rich in proteins and antibodies, especially **IgA**, and provides **passive immunity** to the newborn against infections.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **seminiferous tubules** with **Leydig cells**; seminiferous tubules produce sperms, while Leydig cells produce testosterone.
- Assuming spermatogenesis and oogenesis begin at the same stage of life; oogenesis begins before birth, whereas spermatogenesis starts at puberty.
- Forgetting that fertilization occurs in the **ampullary-isthmic junction**, not in the uterus.
- Confusing **morula** with **blastocyst**; the blastocyst contains a trophoblast and an inner cell mass.
- Mixing up the functions of **FSH** and **LH** during gametogenesis.
- Incorrectly stating that prolactin causes milk ejection; **prolactin** stimulates milk production, while **oxytocin** causes milk ejection.
- Forgetting that **colostrum** provides passive immunity due to its high antibody content.
- Assuming implantation occurs immediately after fertilization; it generally occurs about **6–7 days** later.

---

# Chapter Summary

- The human reproductive system consists of specialized organs that produce gametes and facilitate fertilization and development.
- The **male reproductive system** includes the testes, accessory ducts, glands, and penis, while the **female reproductive system** includes the ovaries, fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, and vagina.
- **Spermatogenesis** produces four functional sperms and begins at puberty, whereas **oogenesis** begins before birth and produces one functional ovum.
- The **menstrual cycle** consists of menstrual, follicular, ovulatory, and luteal phases regulated by **GnRH, FSH, LH, estrogen, and progesterone**.
- Fertilization occurs in the **ampullary-isthmic junction** of the fallopian tube, followed by cleavage, blastocyst formation, and implantation.
- The **placenta** supports fetal growth by facilitating nutrient exchange, gas exchange, waste removal, and hormone secretion.
- **Parturition** is regulated by oxytocin and other hormones, while **lactation** provides nutrition and passive immunity to the newborn through colostrum.
- Human reproduction is a coordinated process involving hormonal regulation, successful fertilization, embryonic development, pregnancy, childbirth, and postnatal nourishment.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **contraceptive methods** with **infertility treatments**; contraceptives prevent pregnancy, whereas ART techniques help achieve pregnancy.
- Assuming all IUDs work in the same way; non-medicated, copper-releasing, and hormone-releasing IUDs have different mechanisms.

- Confusing **vasectomy** (male sterilization) with **tubectomy** (female sterilization).
- Assuming **IVF**, **ZIFT**, and **GIFT** are identical; they differ mainly in the site of fertilization and embryo or gamete transfer.
- Misunderstanding **amniocentesis** as a sex determination test; its legitimate purpose is prenatal diagnosis of genetic disorders, while sex determination is legally prohibited.
- Assuming all STDs show immediate symptoms; many infections remain asymptomatic during the early stages.
- Forgetting that **Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP)** should be carried out only under qualified medical supervision and in accordance with legal provisions.

# Introduction

**Inheritance** is the transmission of genetic information from parents to offspring, while **variation** refers to differences among individuals of the same species. The study of heredity began with the experiments of **Gregor Johann Mendel**, who is known as the **Father of Genetics**.

Modern genetics explains inheritance through genes located on chromosomes, which are transmitted during reproduction according to specific principles.

When two contrasting alleles are present together, only one is expressed.

Genes controlling different characters assort independently during gamete formation.

Applicable only to genes located on different chromosomes or far apart on the same chromosome.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **genotype** with **phenotype**.
- Assuming **dominance** and **co-dominance** are the same; in co-dominance, both alleles are expressed equally.
- Mixing up **incomplete dominance** with **co-dominance**; incomplete dominance produces an intermediate phenotype.
- Forgetting that the **Law of Independent Assortment** applies mainly to genes located on different chromosomes or far apart on the same chromosome.
- Confusing **linkage** with **crossing over**; linkage keeps genes together, whereas crossing over exchanges genetic material.
- Incorrectly stating that the mother determines the sex of the child in humans; the **father** determines the sex through X- or Y-bearing sperm.
- Mixing up the chromosomal constitutions of **Turner's syndrome (XO)** and **Klinefelter syndrome (XXY)**.
- Assuming all genetic disorders are inherited; **Down syndrome** usually results from chromosomal nondisjunction rather than inheritance.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Inheritance** is the transmission of genetic traits from parents to offspring, while **variation** introduces differences among individuals.
- **Gregor Mendel** established the fundamental laws of inheritance through experiments on pea plants, leading to the **Law of Dominance**, **Law of Segregation**, and **Law of Independent Assortment**.
- A **monohybrid cross** produces a **3:1 phenotypic ratio**, while a **dihybrid cross** produces a **9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio** under Mendelian inheritance.
- **Incomplete dominance**, **co-dominance**, and **multiple alleles** are important deviations from simple Mendelian inheritance.
- The **Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance** explains that genes are located on chromosomes, which segregate during meiosis.
- **Linkage** and **crossing over** influence the inheritance of genes and contribute to genetic variation and gene mapping.
- Sex determination differs among organisms: **XX-XY** in humans, **ZZ-ZW** in birds, and **haplodiploidy** in honey bees.
- Human genetic disorders such as **thalassemia**, **Down syndrome**, **Turner's syndrome**, and **Klinefelter syndrome** arise due to gene mutations or chromosomal abnormalities.
- Understanding inheritance patterns is essential for genetics, medicine, breeding programmes, and genetic counseling.

- Forgetting that the **Hardy-Weinberg principle** applies only under ideal conditions with no evolutionary forces acting.
- Mixing up **gene flow** and **genetic drift**; gene flow results from migration, while genetic drift is due to random chance.
- Assuming all mutations are harmful; many are neutral, and some may be beneficial.
- Confusing **adaptive radiation** with **convergent evolution**.
- Believing evolution occurs in individuals; **evolution occurs in populations over generations**.
- Memorizing the sequence of human ancestors without understanding the major evolutionary trends, such as increased brain size and bipedalism.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Evolution** explains the gradual change in populations over time, leading to biological diversity.
- The **chemical evolution theory** proposed by Oparin and Haldane, supported by the **Miller-Urey experiment**, provides a scientific explanation for the origin of life.
- Fossils, homologous and analogous organs, embryology, and molecular studies provide strong evidence for evolution.
- **Darwin's theory of natural selection** states that individuals with favorable heritable variations are more likely to survive and reproduce.
- The **modern synthetic theory** integrates natural selection with genetics and recognizes mutation, recombination, gene flow, and genetic drift as sources of variation.
- The **Hardy-Weinberg principle** describes genetic equilibrium in populations and identifies factors responsible for evolutionary change.
- **Adaptive radiation** and **natural selection** explain how organisms diversify and adapt to different environments.
- **Industrial melanism** and **antibiotic resistance** are important examples of evolution occurring in observable time scales.
- Human evolution involved progressive changes such as increased cranial capacity, upright posture, and advanced cognitive abilities, culminating in modern **Homo sapiens**.

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

**Question:** Why does malaria cause recurring fever?

**Solution:**

Malaria causes recurring fever because Plasmodium multiplies inside red blood cells. When infected RBCs rupture, parasites and toxins are released, producing periodic fever and chills.

---

### Example 2

**Question:** Differentiate between innate and acquired immunity.

**Solution:**

Innate immunity is present from birth and provides non-specific defense without memory. Acquired immunity develops after exposure to antigens and provides specific responses with immunological memory.

---

### Example 3

**Question:** Why are antibiotics ineffective against viruses?

**Solution:**

Antibiotics target bacterial structures and processes such as cell walls and bacterial metabolism. Viruses lack these structures and reproduce using host cells, so antibiotics do not affect them.

---

### Example 4

**Question:** Name the immune cells targeted by HIV.

**Solution:**

HIV primarily infects and destroys **CD4+ T-helper lymphocytes**, weakening the immune system and leading to AIDS.

---

### Example 5

**Question:** What is the difference between benign and malignant tumours?

**Solution:**

Benign tumours remain localized and usually do not invade other tissues, whereas malignant tumours spread to other parts of the body through metastasis.

---

### Example 6

**Question:** Why is vaccination effective?

**Solution:**

Vaccination introduces harmless forms of antigens that stimulate the immune system to produce antibodies and memory cells. On future exposure, the immune response becomes faster and stronger.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **infection** with **disease**; infection refers to entry and multiplication of pathogens, while disease involves symptoms and damage.
- Assuming all microorganisms are harmful; many microbes are beneficial.
- Mixing up **innate immunity** and **acquired immunity**.
- Forgetting that **B-cells produce antibodies**, while **T-cells are responsible for cell-mediated immunity**.
- Assuming antibiotics can treat viral infections; they work mainly against bacteria.
- Confusing HIV infection with AIDS; HIV is the virus, while AIDS is the advanced disease condition.
- Forgetting that cancer results from uncontrolled cell division and may involve mutations.
- Assuming vaccines provide immediate protection; immune memory develops after activation of the immune system.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing restriction endonucleases with exonucleases; endonucleases cut within DNA, while exonucleases remove nucleotides from ends.
- Forgetting that sticky ends are produced by specific restriction enzymes and help DNA fragments join.
- Confusing PCR with natural DNA replication; PCR occurs artificially in vitro.
- Assuming all vectors are plasmids; bacteriophages and artificial chromosomes can also act as vectors.
- Forgetting that **Taq polymerase** comes from _Thermus aquaticus_.
- Mixing up the steps of PCR; the correct order is **Denaturation → Annealing → Extension**.
- Confusing transformation with translation; transformation is DNA uptake by cells, while translation is protein synthesis.
- Ignoring the importance of downstream processing in commercial biotechnology.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing population attributes with individual characteristics; birth rate and sex ratio apply to populations, not individuals.
- Forgetting that logistic growth includes carrying capacity (**K**).
- Mixing up mutualism and commensalism; mutualism benefits both organisms, while commensalism benefits only one.
- Confusing predation and parasitism; predators usually kill prey immediately, whereas parasites depend on hosts for longer periods.
- Assuming competition occurs only between different species; it can also occur within the same species.
- Forgetting that evolution occurs through population changes, not changes in individual organisms.
- Mixing up immigration and emigration; immigration adds individuals, while emigration removes individuals.
- Assuming all adaptations occur during an individual's lifetime; adaptations are inherited traits developed over generations.

- Adaptations help organisms survive under specific environmental conditions.
- Populations are characterized by birth rate, death rate, sex ratio, age structure, and population density.
- Population growth may be exponential (unlimited resources) or logistic (limited resources with carrying capacity).
- Organisms interact through mutualism, commensalism, predation, parasitism, competition, and amensalism.
- The **Hardy-Weinberg principle** and evolutionary concepts explain population-level changes over time.
- Understanding organism–environment relationships helps explain biodiversity, ecological balance, and survival strategies.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **GPP** with **NPP**; NPP is always lower because respiration losses are subtracted.
- Forgetting that energy flow in ecosystems is **unidirectional**.
- Assuming all ecological pyramids are upright; biomass pyramids can be inverted in aquatic ecosystems.
- Confusing food chains with food webs; food webs contain multiple interconnected food chains.
- Forgetting that decomposers play a major role in nutrient recycling.
- Mixing up primary and secondary succession.
- Assuming energy is recycled; energy flows one way, while nutrients are recycled.
- Forgetting the order of decomposition steps.

# Chapter Summary

- An ecosystem consists of abiotic and biotic components interacting as a functional unit.
- Producers capture solar energy, consumers transfer energy through trophic levels, and decomposers recycle nutrients.
- Food chains and food webs describe energy transfer pathways in ecosystems.
- Productivity is measured as **GPP** and **NPP**, with NPP representing the energy available to consumers.
- Decomposition involves fragmentation, leaching, catabolism, humification, and mineralization.
- Energy flow is unidirectional and follows the **10% law**, with only a small fraction transferred between trophic levels.
- Ecological pyramids represent trophic relationships, and the pyramid of energy is always upright.
- Nutrient cycles such as carbon and phosphorus cycles maintain ecosystem balance.
- Ecosystem functioning depends on continuous energy flow and recycling of essential nutrients.

- Biodiversity provides ecological, economic, ethical, and aesthetic benefits.
- Major causes of biodiversity loss are habitat destruction, over-exploitation, invasive species, and co-extinction.
- Conservation strategies include **in-situ conservation** (national parks, sanctuaries, biosphere reserves) and **ex-situ conservation** (zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, cryopreservation).
- Biodiversity hotspots and protected areas play an important role in preventing extinction.
- Sustainable use and global cooperation are essential for conserving Earth's biological diversity.
