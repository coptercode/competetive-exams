# Introduction

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more components.

Examples:

- Salt solution
- Sugar solution
- Air
- Alloys

A solution consists of:

1. **Solvent** – Component present in larger amount.
2. **Solute** – Component present in smaller amount.

---

# 1. Types of Solutions

Solutions can exist in different physical states.

| Solute | Solvent | Example          |
| ------ | ------- | ---------------- |
| Gas    | Gas     | Air              |
| Gas    | Liquid  | CO₂ in water     |
| Liquid | Liquid  | Alcohol in water |
| Solid  | Liquid  | Salt in water    |
| Solid  | Solid   | Brass alloy      |

---

# 2. Concentration of Solutions

The amount of solute present in a given amount of solution is called concentration.

---

# A. Mass Percentage

## Formula

\[
\text{Mass \%} =
\frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}}
\times100
\]

where:

\[
\text{Mass of solution}
=

\text{Mass of solute}+\text{Mass of solvent}
\]

---

## Example

10 g salt is dissolved in 90 g water.

Mass %:

\[
=\frac{10}{10+90}\times100
\]

\[
=10\%
\]

---

# B. Volume Percentage

## Formula

\[
\text{Volume \%}
=

\frac{\text{Volume of solute}}
{\text{Volume of solution}}
\times100
\]

---

# C. Mass by Volume Percentage

Used mainly in medical solutions.

\[
\text{Mass/Volume \%}
=

\frac{\text{Mass of solute(g)}}
{\text{Volume of solution(mL)}}
\times100
\]

---

# D. Parts Per Million (ppm)

Used for very dilute solutions.

\[
ppm=
\frac{\text{Number of parts of solute}}
{\text{Number of parts of solution}}
\times10^6
\]

---

## Applications

- Pollution measurement
- Trace impurities in water

---

# E. Mole Fraction

## Definition

Ratio of moles of one component to total moles of all components.

For component A:

\[
X_A=\frac{n_A}{n_A+n_B}
\]

---

## Properties

\[
X_A+X_B=1
\]

---

# F. Molarity (M)

## Definition

Number of moles of solute dissolved per litre of solution.

---

## Formula

\[
M=
\frac{\text{Moles of solute}}
{\text{Volume of solution in litres}}
\]

---

## Unit

\[
mol\,L^{-1}
\]

---

## Example

0.5 mol NaCl in 2 L solution:

\[
M=\frac{0.5}{2}
\]

\[
=0.25M
\]

---

# G. Molality (m)

## Definition

Number of moles of solute present in 1 kg of solvent.

---

## Formula

\[
m=
\frac{\text{Moles of solute}}
{\text{Mass of solvent(kg)}}
\]

---

## Unit

\[
mol\,kg^{-1}
\]

---

# Molarity vs Molality

| Molarity                 | Molality                   |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Based on solution volume | Based on solvent mass      |
| Changes with temperature | Independent of temperature |
| Unit: mol/L              | Unit: mol/kg               |

---

# 3. Solubility

## Definition

Maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a specific temperature.

---

# Factors Affecting Solubility

## A. Nature of Solute and Solvent

"Like dissolves like"

- Polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents.
- Non-polar solutes dissolve in non-polar solvents.

---

## B. Temperature

For solids:

- Solubility usually increases with temperature.

For gases:

- Solubility decreases with temperature.

---

## C. Pressure

Important mainly for gases.

---

# 4. Solubility of Gases in Liquids

## Henry's Law

Given by:

```
William Henry
```

---

## Statement

At constant temperature, solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas.

---

## Equation

\[
p=k_Hx
\]

where:

- p = partial pressure of gas
- x = mole fraction of dissolved gas
- kH = Henry's constant

---

# Applications of Henry's Law

## 1. Carbonated Drinks

CO₂ is dissolved under high pressure.

---

## 2. Scuba Diving

High pressure increases nitrogen solubility in blood.

Rapid release causes:

```
Decompression sickness
```

---

## 3. High Altitude

Low oxygen pressure causes reduced oxygen availability.

---

# 5. Vapour Pressure of Liquid Solutions

## Vapour Pressure

Pressure exerted by vapour in equilibrium with its liquid.

---

# Raoult's Law

For a volatile component:

\[
p_A=p_A^0X_A
\]

where:

- pA = partial vapour pressure
- pA⁰ = vapour pressure of pure component
- XA = mole fraction

---

# Binary Solution

For two volatile liquids:

\[
P=p_A+p_B
\]

---

# Ideal Solutions

A solution that obeys Raoult's law throughout concentration range.

---

## Characteristics

- Similar intermolecular forces.
- No heat absorption/release.
- No volume change.

Examples:

- Benzene + Toluene

---

# Non-Ideal Solutions

Do not obey Raoult's law.

---

# Positive Deviation

## Cause

A-B attraction is weaker.

Effects:

- Higher vapour pressure.
- Lower boiling point.

Example:

Ethanol + Acetone

---

# Negative Deviation

## Cause

A-B attraction is stronger.

Effects:

- Lower vapour pressure.
- Higher boiling point.

Example:

Chloroform + Acetone

---

# 6. Colligative Properties

## Definition

Properties that depend only on the number of solute particles and not their nature.

---

# Four Colligative Properties

1. Relative lowering of vapour pressure
2. Elevation of boiling point
3. Depression of freezing point
4. Osmotic pressure

---

# A. Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure

For non-volatile solute:

\[
\frac{P^0-P}{P^0}=X_B
\]

where:

- P⁰ = vapour pressure of pure solvent
- P = vapour pressure of solution
- XB = mole fraction of solute

---

# B. Elevation in Boiling Point

Adding solute increases boiling point.

\[
\Delta T_b=T_b-T_b^0
\]

---

## Formula

\[
\Delta T_b=K_bm
\]

where:

- Kb = molal elevation constant
- m = molality

---

# C. Depression in Freezing Point

\[
\Delta T_f=T_f^0-T_f
\]

---

## Formula

\[
\Delta T_f=K_fm
\]

where:

- Kf = molal depression constant

---

# D. Osmotic Pressure

## Definition

Pressure required to stop osmosis.

---

## Formula

\[
\pi=CRT
\]

or

\[
\pi=\frac{nRT}{V}
\]

---

where:

- C = molar concentration
- R = gas constant
- T = temperature

---

# 7. Osmosis

## Definition

Movement of solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane from dilute solution to concentrated solution.

---

## Diagram

```
Dilute Solution

      ↓
-------------
Membrane
-------------
      ↑

Concentrated Solution
```

---

# Types of Solutions

## Hypotonic

Lower solute concentration.

---

## Hypertonic

Higher solute concentration.

---

## Isotonic

Equal concentration.

---

# 8. Reverse Osmosis

## Definition

Process where solvent is forced from concentrated solution to pure solvent side by applying pressure greater than osmotic pressure.

---

## Application

- Water purification
- Desalination

---

# 9. Abnormal Molar Mass

Sometimes experimentally calculated molar mass differs from actual value due to:

- Association
- Dissociation

---

# van't Hoff Factor (i)

## Definition

Ratio of observed colligative property to calculated colligative property.

\[
i=
\frac{\text{Observed colligative property}}
{\text{Calculated colligative property}}
\]

---

# Effect of Dissociation

Number of particles increases.

\[
i>1
\]

Example:

\[
KCl\rightarrow K^+ + Cl^-
\]

---

# Effect of Association

Number of particles decreases.

\[
i<1
\]

Example:

Acetic acid dimerization:

\[
2CH_3COOH
\rightarrow
(CH_3COOH)_2
\]

---

# Modified Colligative Equations

## Elevation of boiling point

\[
\Delta T_b=iK_bm
\]

---

## Depression of freezing point

\[
\Delta T_f=iK_fm
\]

---

## Osmotic pressure

\[
\pi=iCRT
\]

---

| ------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Obeys Raoult's law | Deviates from Raoult's law |

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** Calculate molarity of a solution containing 2 mol solute in 500 mL solution.

### Solution

Volume:

\[
500mL=0.5L
\]

\[
M=\frac{2}{0.5}
\]

\[
\boxed{M=4M}
\]

---

## Example 2

**Question:** Find mole fraction of ethanol in a mixture containing 1 mole ethanol and 3 moles water.

### Solution

\[
X_{ethanol}
=

\frac{1}{1+3}
\]

\[
\boxed{X=0.25}
\]

---

## Example 3

**Question:** Why does adding salt increase boiling point of water?

### Solution

Salt is a non-volatile solute. It lowers vapour pressure of water. Therefore, higher temperature is required for vapour pressure to become equal to atmospheric pressure, increasing the boiling point.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** Why does KCl have van't Hoff factor greater than 1?

### Solution

KCl dissociates into two ions:

\[
KCl\rightarrow K^+ + Cl^-
\]

Number of particles increases, therefore:

\[
i>1
\]

---

## Example 5

**Question:** Explain reverse osmosis.

### Solution

In reverse osmosis, pressure greater than osmotic pressure is applied on the concentrated solution side, forcing solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane. It is used for purification of water.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing molarity with molality; molarity depends on solution volume, while molality depends on solvent mass.
- Forgetting that molarity changes with temperature but molality does not.
- Using Raoult's law for non-ideal solutions without considering deviation.
- Assuming gases become more soluble at higher temperatures; gas solubility usually decreases with temperature.
- Forgetting that colligative properties depend on the number of particles, not their chemical identity.
- Ignoring van't Hoff factor in electrolyte solutions.
- Mixing up positive and negative deviations from Raoult's law.
- Forgetting units while calculating molarity and molality.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Solutions are homogeneous mixtures of solute and solvent.
- Concentration can be expressed using molarity, molality, mole fraction, mass percentage, and ppm.
- Gas solubility in liquids is governed by **Henry's Law**.
- **Raoult's Law** describes vapour pressure behaviour of ideal solutions.
- Non-ideal solutions show positive or negative deviations due to differences in intermolecular forces.
- Colligative properties depend on the number of solute particles and include boiling point elevation, freezing point depression, vapour pressure lowering, and osmotic pressure.
- Osmosis and reverse osmosis have important biological and industrial applications.
- Abnormal molar masses arise due to association or dissociation of solute particles and are explained using the **van't Hoff factor**.
- Understanding solutions is essential for chemistry, biology, medicine, and industrial applications.

# Introduction

**Electrochemistry** is the branch of chemistry that deals with the relationship between chemical reactions and electrical energy.

It involves:

- Production of electricity from chemical reactions.
- Use of electricity to drive chemical reactions.

---

# 1. Electrochemical Cells

## Definition

An electrochemical cell is a device where chemical energy is converted into electrical energy or electrical energy is converted into chemical energy.

---

# Types of Electrochemical Cells

```
Electrochemical Cells

       |
       |

-----------------

Galvanic Cell

Electrolytic Cell
```

---

# A. Galvanic Cell

## Definition

A cell in which a spontaneous chemical reaction produces electrical energy.

Example:

```
Daniell Cell
```

---

## Daniell Cell

Reaction:

\[
Zn + Cu^{2+}\rightarrow Zn^{2+}+Cu
\]

---

## Components

### Anode

- Zinc electrode
- Oxidation occurs

\[
Zn\rightarrow Zn^{2+}+2e^-
\]

---

### Cathode

- Copper electrode
- Reduction occurs

\[
Cu^{2+}+2e^-\rightarrow Cu
\]

---

# Cell Representation

\[
Zn|Zn^{2+}||Cu^{2+}|Cu
\]

---

# Salt Bridge

## Functions

1. Completes electrical circuit.
2. Maintains electrical neutrality.
3. Prevents direct mixing of solutions.
4. Minimizes liquid junction potential.

---

# 2. Electrode Potential

## Definition

The tendency of an electrode to lose or gain electrons when placed in a solution.

---

# Standard Electrode Potential (E°)

Measured under:

- 1 M concentration
- 1 atm pressure
- 298 K temperature

---

## Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

Reference electrode:

\[
2H^+ +2e^- \rightarrow H_2
\]

Standard potential:

\[
E^\circ=0V
\]

---

# 3. Cell Potential

## Formula

\[
E^\circ_{cell}=E^\circ_{cathode}-E^\circ_{anode}
\]

---

## Interpretation

### Positive E°cell

Reaction is spontaneous.

### Negative E°cell

Reaction is non-spontaneous.

---

# 4. Gibbs Energy and Cell Potential

Relationship:

\[
\Delta G^\circ=-nFE^\circ_{cell}
\]

where:

- n = number of electrons
- F = Faraday constant

---

## Meaning

If:

\[
E^\circ_{cell}>0
\]

then:

\[
\Delta G^\circ<0
\]

Reaction is spontaneous.

---

# 5. Nernst Equation

## Definition

Equation used to calculate cell potential under non-standard conditions.

---

## General Equation

\[
E=E^\circ-\frac{0.0591}{n}\log Q
\]

(at 298 K)

---

where:

- E = cell potential
- E° = standard potential
- n = electrons transferred
- Q = reaction quotient

---

# For Daniell Cell

Reaction:

\[
Zn+Cu^{2+}\rightarrow Zn^{2+}+Cu
\]

\[
Q=\frac{[Zn^{2+}]}{[Cu^{2+}]}
\]

---

# 6. Electrolytic Conductance

## Definition

Ability of a solution to conduct electricity.

Depends on:

- Number of ions
- Charge of ions
- Mobility of ions

---

# Resistance

\[
R=\rho\frac{l}{A}
\]

where:

- ρ = resistivity
- l = length
- A = area

---

# Conductance

\[
G=\frac{1}{R}
\]

Unit:

\[
Siemens(S)
\]

---

# 7. Conductivity (Specific Conductance)

## Definition

Conductance of a solution placed between electrodes of unit length and unit area.

---

## Formula

\[
\kappa=G\frac{l}{A}
\]

---

## Unit

\[
S\,cm^{-1}
\]

---

# 8. Molar Conductivity

## Definition

Conductance of all ions produced by one mole of electrolyte placed between electrodes.

---

## Formula

\[
\Lambda_m=
\frac{\kappa\times1000}{C}
\]

where:

- C = concentration in mol/L

---

## Unit

\[
S\,cm^2 mol^{-1}
\]

---

# 9. Variation of Conductivity

## Strong Electrolytes

Examples:

- HCl
- NaCl

Conductivity increases sharply on dilution.

---

## Weak Electrolytes

Examples:

- CH₃COOH

Conductivity increases greatly on dilution due to increased ionization.

---

# 10. Kohlrausch's Law

## Statement

At infinite dilution, each ion contributes independently to the total molar conductivity.

---

## Formula

\[
\Lambda_m^0=
\lambda^0_+ + \lambda^0_-
\]

---

# Applications

## 1. Finding limiting molar conductivity.

## 2. Calculating degree of dissociation.

## 3. Finding dissociation constants of weak electrolytes.

# 12. Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

---

# First Law

## Statement

The mass of substance deposited is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed.

\[

\]

\[
Q=It
\]

\[

\]

- Z = electrochemical equivalent

---

## Statement

When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes, masses deposited are proportional to their equivalent masses.

---

## Formula

\[
m\propto \frac{M}{n}
\]

---

# Faraday Constant

One mole of electrons:

\[
F=96500C
\]

# Introduction

**Chemical kinetics** is the branch of chemistry that deals with the **rate of chemical reactions** and the factors affecting them.

It helps us understand:

- How fast a reaction occurs.
- Why reaction rates differ.
- How reaction rates can be controlled.

---

# 1. Rate of a Chemical Reaction

## Definition

The change in concentration of reactants or products per unit time is called the rate of reaction.

---

For a reaction:

\[
A \rightarrow B
\]

Rate:

\[
Rate=-\frac{\Delta[A]}{\Delta t}
\]

or

\[
Rate=\frac{\Delta[B]}{\Delta t}
\]

---

## Unit of Rate

\[
mol\,L^{-1}s^{-1}
\]

---

# 2. Average Rate of Reaction

## Definition

Rate measured over a finite time interval.

---

Formula:

\[
Average\ Rate=
-\frac{\Delta R}{\Delta t}
\]

where:

- ΔR = change in concentration of reactant
- Δt = time interval

---

# Example

If concentration decreases from 0.8 M to 0.2 M in 10 seconds:

\[
Rate=
-\frac{0.2-0.8}{10}
\]

\[
=\frac{0.6}{10}
\]

\[
=0.06\,mol\,L^{-1}s^{-1}
\]

---

# 3. Instantaneous Rate

## Definition

Rate of reaction at a particular instant.

---

Mathematically:

\[
Rate=-\frac{d[R]}{dt}
\]

---

# 4. Factors Affecting Rate of Reaction

---

## A. Concentration of Reactants

Higher concentration:

- More collisions.
- Faster reaction.

---

## B. Temperature

Increase in temperature:

- Increases kinetic energy.
- Increases effective collisions.

---

## C. Catalyst

A catalyst:

- Increases reaction rate.
- Lowers activation energy.
- Remains chemically unchanged.

---

## D. Surface Area

Powdered solids react faster than large pieces.

---

## E. Nature of Reactants

Ionic reactions are generally faster than covalent reactions.

---

# 5. Rate Law

## Definition

The mathematical relationship between reaction rate and reactant concentration.

---

For:

\[
aA+bB\rightarrow Products
\]

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k[A]^m[B]^n
\]

where:

- k = rate constant
- m,n = experimentally determined powers

---

# 6. Rate Constant (k)

## Definition

The rate of reaction when concentration of all reactants is unity.

---

## Factors Affecting k

Depends on:

- Temperature
- Catalyst
- Nature of reactants

Does not depend on concentration.

---

# 7. Order of Reaction

## Definition

Sum of powers of concentration terms in rate law.

---

For:

\[
Rate=k[A]^m[B]^n

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing order with molecularity; they are not always the same.
- Forgetting that order is determined experimentally.
- Using the first-order half-life equation for zero-order reactions.
- Ignoring units of rate constant for different orders.
- Assuming catalysts increase equilibrium constant; catalysts only change reaction rate.
- Forgetting that effective collisions require both sufficient energy and proper orientation.
- Mixing up activation energy with heat of reaction.
- Forgetting that half-life of a first-order reaction is independent of initial concentration.

- Zero-order and first-order reactions have specific integrated rate equations and half-life expressions.
- The Arrhenius equation explains the temperature dependence of reaction rates.
- Activation energy is the minimum energy required for successful reaction.
- Collision theory explains reactions through effective molecular collisions.
- Catalysts increase reaction rate by lowering activation energy without being consumed.

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** Why are aldehydes more reactive than ketones?

### Solution

Aldehydes have less steric hindrance and only one alkyl group donating electrons. Ketones have two alkyl groups, reducing electrophilicity of carbonyl carbon.

---

## Example 2

**Question:** Give Tollens' test for aldehydes.

### Solution

Aldehydes reduce ammoniacal silver nitrate to metallic silver, producing a silver mirror.

---

## Example 3

**Question:** What is aldol condensation?

### Solution

Aldehydes or ketones containing alpha hydrogen react in presence of dilute base to form β-hydroxy aldehydes or ketones.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** Why are carboxylic acids acidic?

### Solution

Loss of H⁺ produces carboxylate ion, which is stabilized by resonance.

---

## Example 5

**Question:** What is iodoform test?

### Solution

Compounds containing methyl carbonyl group produce yellow precipitate of iodoform (CHI₃) on reaction with iodine and alkali.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing aldehydes and ketones.
- Forgetting that aldehydes give Tollens' and Fehling's tests.
- Mixing up Rosenmund, Stephen, and Etard reactions.
- Forgetting the role of alpha hydrogen in aldol condensation.
- Applying Cannizzaro reaction to aldehydes having alpha hydrogen.
- Confusing carbonyl carbon with alpha carbon.
- Forgetting that carboxylic acids form hydrogen-bonded dimers.
- Assuming ketones oxidise easily like aldehydes.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids contain carbonyl-related functional groups.
- Aldehydes are generally more reactive than ketones due to lower steric hindrance.
- Carbonyl compounds undergo nucleophilic addition reactions.
- Important tests include Tollens' test, Fehling's test, and iodoform test.
- Aldol condensation occurs due to alpha hydrogen, while Cannizzaro reaction occurs in aldehydes without alpha hydrogen.
- Carboxylic acids are acidic due to resonance stabilization of carboxylate ions.
- Important preparation methods include Rosenmund reduction, Stephen reaction, Etard reaction, and HVZ reaction.
- These compounds have major applications in industry, biology, and organic synthesis.

# Introduction

Amines are organic compounds derived from ammonia (**NH₃**) by replacing one or more hydrogen atoms with alkyl or aryl groups.

\[
NH_3 \rightarrow RNH_2,\ R_2NH,\ R_3N

Amines are classified based on the number of carbon groups attached to nitrogen.

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** Why is aniline less basic than methylamine?

### Solution

In aniline, the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring through resonance. This reduces availability of the lone pair for proton acceptance, making it less basic than methylamine.

---

## Example 2

**Question:** What is Gabriel phthalimide synthesis?

### Solution

It is a method for preparing primary amines by reacting potassium phthalimide with alkyl halides followed by hydrolysis.

---

## Example 3

**Question:** Which amines give carbylamine test?

### Solution

Only primary amines (both aliphatic and aromatic) give the carbylamine test.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** What is diazotisation?

### Solution

Conversion of primary aromatic amines into diazonium salts using sodium nitrite and HCl at 273–278 K is called diazotisation.

---

## Example 5

**Question:** Arrange amines in basic strength order in aqueous solution.

### Solution:

\[
2^\circ>1^\circ>3^\circ>NH_3
\]

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing primary, secondary, and tertiary amines with primary, secondary, and tertiary carbons.
- Forgetting that aromatic amines are less basic due to resonance.
- Applying carbylamine test to secondary or tertiary amines.
- Forgetting the low temperature requirement in diazotisation.
- Mixing Sandmeyer and Gattermann reactions.
- Forgetting Gabriel synthesis gives only primary amines.
- Confusing diazonium salts with ordinary ammonium salts.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Amines are derivatives of ammonia where hydrogen atoms are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups.
- They are classified as primary, secondary, and tertiary amines.
- Amines are basic due to the lone pair on nitrogen.
- Basicity depends on electronic effects and resonance.
- Important preparation methods include nitro reduction, Gabriel synthesis, and Hoffmann bromamide reaction.
- Primary aromatic amines form diazonium salts through diazotisation.
- Diazonium salts are useful in Sandmeyer, Gattermann, hydrolysis, and azo coupling reactions.
- Identification tests such as carbylamine and Hinsberg tests distinguish different types of amines.

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** What are carbohydrates?

### Solution

Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds that produce them on hydrolysis.

---

## Example 2

**Question:** Why is sucrose a non-reducing sugar?

### Solution

Sucrose has both anomeric carbons involved in glycosidic bond formation, so it cannot open to form free aldehyde or ketone groups.

---

## Example 3

**Question:** What is denaturation of proteins?

### Solution

Denaturation is the loss of biological activity of proteins due to disruption of secondary and tertiary structures without breaking peptide bonds.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** Differentiate DNA and RNA.

### Solution

DNA contains deoxyribose sugar and thymine, while RNA contains ribose sugar and uracil.

---

## Example 5

**Question:** What is a peptide bond?

### Solution

A peptide bond is the amide linkage:

\[
-CO-NH-
\]

formed between two amino acids by removal of water.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing glucose and fructose; glucose is an aldose while fructose is a ketose.
- Forgetting that sucrose is non-reducing.
- Mixing up starch, glycogen, and cellulose.
- Forgetting that proteins are polymers of amino acids.
- Confusing peptide bonds with hydrogen bonds.
- Forgetting the difference between nucleoside and nucleotide.
- Mixing DNA bases with RNA bases.
- Forgetting vitamin deficiency diseases.
- Assuming denaturation breaks peptide bonds; it mainly affects higher structures.
