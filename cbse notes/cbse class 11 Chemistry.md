# Introduction

Chemistry is the branch of science that deals with the **composition, structure, properties, and transformations of matter**. Since atoms and molecules are extremely small, chemists use measurable quantities such as the **mole** to represent large numbers of particles.

Understanding chemical reactions requires knowledge of **chemical formulae**, **balanced equations**, and quantitative relationships between reactants and products, collectively known as **stoichiometry**.

---

# 1. Matter

## Definition

Matter is anything that:

- Has mass.
- Occupies space.

Examples:

- Water
- Air
- Iron
- Wood

---

# 2. Physical and Chemical Properties

## Physical Properties

Properties that can be observed without changing the chemical composition.

Examples

- Colour
- Density
- Melting point
- Boiling point

---

## Chemical Properties

Properties observed when a substance undergoes a chemical change.

Examples

- Combustibility
- Rusting
- Reactivity with acids

---

# 3. Physical and Chemical Changes

## Physical Change

A change in which no new substance is formed.

Examples

- Melting of ice
- Boiling of water
- Breaking glass

---

## Chemical Change

A change in which new substances are formed.

Examples

- Burning of magnesium
- Rusting of iron
- Digestion of food

# 4. Laws of Chemical Combination

---

## (A) Law of Conservation of Mass

### Proposed by

**Antoine Lavoisier**

### Statement

Mass is neither created nor destroyed during a chemical reaction.

```
Total Mass of Reactants
=
Total Mass of Products
```

### Example

```
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

4 g + 32 g = 36 g
```

---

## (B) Law of Constant Proportions

### Proposed by

**Joseph Proust**

### Statement

A chemical compound always contains the same elements in the same fixed proportion by mass.

Example

Water always contains:

```
Hydrogen : Oxygen

1 : 8
```

---

## (C) Law of Multiple Proportions

### Proposed by

**John Dalton**

### Statement

When two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in simple whole-number ratios.

Example

```
CO

12 g C + 16 g O

CO₂

12 g C + 32 g O

Ratio = 16 : 32 = 1 : 2
```

---

# 5. Dalton's Atomic Theory

Main postulates:

- Matter consists of tiny particles called atoms.
- Atoms are indivisible (modified later).
- Atoms of the same element are identical.
- Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios.
- Chemical reactions involve rearrangement of atoms.

---

# 6. Atomic Mass

Atomic mass is the mass of an atom relative to **1/12th of the mass of a carbon-12 atom**.

Unit:

```
Atomic Mass Unit (u)
```

Example

| Element | Atomic Mass (u) |
| ------- | --------------- |
| H       | 1               |
| C       | 12              |
| O       | 16              |
| Na      | 23              |

---

# 7. Molecular Mass

The molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms present in a molecule.

### Formula

```
Molecular Mass

= Σ Atomic Masses
```

### Example

For H₂O

```
= (2 × 1) + 16

= 18 u
```

---

Used for ionic compounds.

A chemical equation represents a chemical reaction using symbols and formulae.


# 16. Balancing Chemical Equations

Rules

- Number of atoms must be equal on both sides.
- Use coefficients only.
- Never change subscripts.

Example

```
Unbalanced

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Balanced

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
```

# 17. Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry deals with the quantitative relationship between reactants and products in a chemical reaction.

Example

```
N₂ + 3H₂

↓

2NH₃
```

Meaning

```
1 mol N₂

+

3 mol H₂

↓

2 mol NH₃
```

---

# 18. Limiting Reagent

The limiting reagent is the reactant that is completely consumed first and limits the amount of product formed.

Example

```
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Available

2 mol H₂

2 mol O₂

Required ratio

2 : 1

Therefore,

Hydrogen is limiting.
```

---

# 19. Excess Reagent

The reactant left after completion of the reaction is called the excess reagent.

---

# 20. Stoichiometric Calculations

General steps

1. Write balanced equation.
2. Convert mass into moles.
3. Compare mole ratio.
4. Identify limiting reagent.
5. Calculate product.
6. Convert moles into required unit.

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Calculate the number of moles in **36 g of water (H₂O).**

**Solution**

Molar mass of H₂O = 18 g mol⁻¹

```
Number of moles

= Mass / Molar Mass

= 36 / 18

= 2 mol
```

---

### Example 2

Calculate the number of molecules present in **2 mol of oxygen gas.**

**Solution**

```
Number of molecules

= 2 × 6.022 × 10²³

= 1.2044 × 10²⁴ molecules
```

---

### Example 3

Find the molecular mass of carbon dioxide (CO₂).

**Solution**

```
C = 12

O = 16

CO₂

= 12 + (2 ×16)

= 44 u
```

---

### Example 4

A compound contains **40% carbon, 6.67% hydrogen, and 53.33% oxygen**. Determine its empirical formula.

**Solution**

Assume 100 g of compound.

```
C = 40/12 = 3.33

H = 6.67/1 = 6.67

O = 53.33/16 = 3.33
```

Divide by the smallest value (3.33):

```
C : H : O

= 1 : 2 : 1
```

Empirical formula:

```
CH₂O
```

---

### Example 5

Calculate the percentage of oxygen in water.

**Solution**

```
Mass of oxygen = 16

Molar mass of water = 18

Percentage

= (16/18) ×100

= 88.89%
```

---

### Example 6

In the reaction:

```
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
```

If **4 mol H₂** reacts with **1 mol O₂**, identify the limiting reagent.

**Solution**

Required ratio:

```
2 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂
```

Available:

```
4 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂
```

Only 2 mol H₂ are required for 1 mol O₂.

Therefore,

- **Oxygen is the limiting reagent.**
- Hydrogen is present in excess.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **atomic mass** with **molar mass**; atomic mass is expressed in **u**, while molar mass is expressed in **g mol⁻¹**.
- Using an **unbalanced chemical equation** for stoichiometric calculations.
- Changing **subscripts** instead of coefficients while balancing equations.
- Forgetting to convert **mass into moles** before using mole ratios.
- Mixing up **empirical formula** and **molecular formula**.
- Ignoring the **limiting reagent**, leading to incorrect product calculations.
- Using **22.4 L** for gases under conditions other than STP without checking the given conditions.
- Confusing **molarity** (depends on solution volume) with **molality** (depends on solvent mass).

---

# Chapter Summary

- Matter undergoes **physical** and **chemical** changes, and chemical reactions obey the **laws of chemical combination**: conservation of mass, constant proportions, and multiple proportions.
- **Dalton's atomic theory** explains matter in terms of atoms combining in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
- **Atomic mass**, **molecular mass**, and **formula mass** help determine the masses of atoms, molecules, and ionic compounds.
- The **mole concept** provides a bridge between microscopic particles and measurable laboratory quantities using **Avogadro's constant (6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹)**.
- Quantitative relationships between **mass**, **moles**, **particles**, and **gas volume** form the basis of chemical calculations.
- **Percentage composition**, **empirical formula**, and **molecular formula** are used to determine the composition and identity of compounds.
- A correctly **balanced chemical equation** is essential for all stoichiometric calculations.
- **Stoichiometry** enables the calculation of reactant and product amounts, while the **limiting reagent** determines the maximum amount of product that can be formed.
- Concentration terms such as **mass percentage**, **ppm**, **mole fraction**, **molarity**, and **molality** are widely used to express the composition of solutions.

# Introduction

The atom is the fundamental unit of matter. Early scientists believed atoms were indivisible, but experiments by several scientists revealed the existence of **electrons, protons, and neutrons**. Modern atomic theory explains the arrangement of electrons around the nucleus using **quantum mechanics**, which forms the basis of chemical bonding and periodic properties.

---

# 1. Discovery of Subatomic Particles

## Electron

### Discovered by

**J. J. Thomson (1897)**

### Experiment

Cathode Ray Discharge Tube Experiment

### Properties

- Negatively charged particle.
- Charge = **−1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C**
- Mass = **9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg**

---

## Proton

### Discovered by

**Eugen Goldstein** (Canal Rays)

The proton was later identified as the positively charged particle present in the nucleus.

### Properties

- Positive charge.
- Charge = **+1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C**
- Mass = **1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg**

---

## Neutron

### Discovered by

**James Chadwick (1932)**

### Properties

- Electrically neutral.
- Mass = **1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg**

---

# 2. Thomson's Atomic Model

Also called:

```
Plum Pudding Model
```

### Main Features

- Atom is a positively charged sphere.
- Electrons are embedded throughout the sphere.
- Overall atom is electrically neutral.

### Limitations

- Could not explain Rutherford's experiment.
- Failed to explain atomic stability.

---

# 3. Rutherford's Atomic Model

### Experiment

Gold Foil (α-particle Scattering) Experiment

### Observations

- Most α-particles passed straight through.
- Some were deflected.
- Very few rebounded.

---

## Conclusions

- Atom is mostly empty space.
- Positive charge is concentrated in the nucleus.
- Electrons revolve around the nucleus.

---

## Limitations

- Could not explain atomic stability.
- Could not explain line spectra of atoms.

---

# 4. Bohr's Atomic Model

### Proposed by

**Niels Bohr (1913)**

---

## Postulates

1. Electrons revolve only in certain permitted circular orbits.
2. These orbits have fixed energies.
3. Electrons do not radiate energy while moving in these stationary orbits.
4. Energy is emitted or absorbed only when electrons jump between energy levels.

---

## Bohr's Radius

For hydrogen atom,

```
rₙ = (n²a₀) / Z
```

where

- n = principal quantum number
- Z = atomic number
- a₀ = 0.529 Å

---

## Velocity of Electron

```
vₙ = (2.18 × 10⁶ Z) / n  m s⁻¹
```

---

## Energy of Electron

```
Eₙ = -13.6 Z² / n² eV
```

---

## Frequency of Radiation

```
ΔE = hν
```

where

- h = Planck's constant
- ν = frequency

---

## Merits

- Explained hydrogen spectrum.
- Explained atomic stability.
- Introduced quantized energy levels.

---

## Limitations

- Applicable only to hydrogen-like atoms.
- Failed to explain fine spectra.
- Could not explain Zeeman and Stark effects.
- Ignored wave nature of electrons.

---

# 7. Photoelectric Effect

### Explained by

**Albert Einstein**

### Observation

# 8. Dual Nature of Matter

### Proposed by

**Louis de Broglie**

Matter exhibits both:

- Particle nature.
- Wave nature.

# 9. Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

### Proposed by

**Werner Heisenberg**

### Statement

It is impossible to simultaneously determine the exact position and momentum of an electron.

---

## Mathematical Expression

```
Δx × Δp ≥ h / 4π
```

---

## Significance

- Electrons cannot have fixed paths.
- Led to quantum mechanical model.

# 10. Quantum Mechanical Model

Developed by:

**Erwin Schrödinger**

According to this model:

- Electrons behave as waves.
- Exact path cannot be predicted.
- Electrons are found in regions of high probability called **orbitals**.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **orbit** with **orbital**; orbits are fixed paths in Bohr's model, whereas orbitals are probability regions in quantum mechanics.
- Assuming Bohr's model applies to all atoms; it is mainly valid for **hydrogen-like species**.
- Forgetting the **(n + l) rule** while writing electronic configurations.
- Mixing up the meanings of the four **quantum numbers**.
- Violating **Pauli's exclusion principle** by placing more than two electrons in an orbital.
- Ignoring **Hund's rule** and pairing electrons before singly occupying degenerate orbitals.
- Forgetting the exceptional configurations of **chromium** and **copper**.
- Confusing **atomic orbitals** with **subshells** and **shells**.

---

# Chapter Summary

- The discovery of **electrons**, **protons**, and **neutrons** established that atoms are composed of subatomic particles.
- **Thomson**, **Rutherford**, and **Bohr** proposed successive atomic models, with Bohr successfully explaining the hydrogen spectrum using quantized energy levels.
- **Electromagnetic radiation** exhibits wave properties, while **Planck's quantum theory** and **Einstein's photoelectric effect** demonstrated its particle nature.
- **de Broglie** proposed the wave nature of matter, and **Heisenberg's uncertainty principle** showed that the exact position and momentum of an electron cannot be known simultaneously.
- The **quantum mechanical model** describes electrons in terms of **orbitals**, which are regions of high probability around the nucleus.
- The four **quantum numbers** (n, l, mₗ, mₛ) completely describe the state of an electron in an atom.
- The **Aufbau principle**, **Pauli exclusion principle**, and **Hund's rule** govern the arrangement of electrons in atomic orbitals.
- **Half-filled** and **fully-filled** subshells possess extra stability, explaining the exceptional electronic configurations of elements such as **chromium** and **copper**.

# Introduction

As the number of known elements increased, scientists needed a systematic method to organize them based on their properties. This led to the development of the **Periodic Table**, where elements are arranged so that similar elements occur together.

The **Modern Periodic Table** is based on the **Modern Periodic Law**, which relates the properties of elements to their **atomic numbers** rather than their atomic masses.

---

# 1. Need for Classification of Elements

Classification helps to:

- Study a large number of elements systematically.
- Predict the properties of unknown elements.
- Understand similarities and differences among elements.
- Correlate electronic configuration with chemical properties.

---

# 2. Early Attempts at Classification

---

## (A) Döbereiner's Triads

### Proposed by

**Johann Wolfgang Döbereiner (1829)**

### Features

- Elements were grouped in threes.
- Middle element had an atomic mass approximately equal to the average of the other two.
- Members showed similar properties.

### Example

```
Li      Na      K

7       23      39

(7 + 39)/2 = 23
```

### Limitations

- Applicable to only a few elements.
- Most known elements could not be grouped.

---

## (B) Newlands' Law of Octaves

### Proposed by

**John Newlands (1865)**

### Statement

When elements are arranged in increasing order of atomic mass, every eighth element has properties similar to the first.

```
1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8

↓

Similar properties
```

### Merits

- First systematic attempt.
- Showed periodic repetition of properties.

### Limitations

- Valid only up to calcium.
- Left no gaps for undiscovered elements.
- Failed for heavier elements.

---

## (C) Mendeleev's Periodic Table

### Proposed by

**Dmitri Mendeleev (1869)**

### Basis

- Atomic mass.
- Similar chemical properties.

---

## Mendeleev's Periodic Law

"The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic masses."

---

## Merits

- Left gaps for undiscovered elements.
- Predicted properties of unknown elements.
- Corrected atomic masses of several elements.

---

## Limitations

- Position of hydrogen uncertain.
- Isotopes could not be accommodated.
- Anomalous pairs (Ar-K, Co-Ni, Te-I).

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Arrange the following in increasing order of atomic radius:

```
Na, Mg, Al
```

**Solution**

All three elements belong to Period 3.

Across a period, atomic radius decreases due to increasing effective nuclear charge.

Therefore,

```
Al < Mg < Na
```

---

### Example 2

Which has a higher ionization enthalpy: Be or B?

**Solution**

Although ionization enthalpy generally increases across a period, **beryllium has a completely filled 2s² subshell**, making it more stable than boron (2s²2p¹).

Therefore,

```
Be > B
```

---

### Example 3

Arrange the following isoelectronic species in decreasing order of ionic radius:

```
O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺
```

**Solution**

All have 10 electrons.

Greater nuclear charge leads to a smaller ionic radius.

Hence,

```
O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺
```

---

### Example 4

Why is fluorine more electronegative than chlorine?

**Solution**

Fluorine has a smaller atomic size and higher effective nuclear charge, allowing it to attract the shared electron pair more strongly than chlorine.

---

### Example 5

State the trend of metallic character across a period.

**Solution**

Metallic character decreases from left to right because atoms hold their valence electrons more strongly and are less likely to lose them.

---

### Example 6

Why does chlorine have a more negative electron gain enthalpy than fluorine?

**Solution**

Although fluorine is more electronegative, its very small atomic size causes greater electron-electron repulsion when an additional electron enters the compact 2p orbital. In chlorine, the larger 3p orbital accommodates the incoming electron more easily, making its electron gain enthalpy more negative.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **atomic radius** with **ionic radius**.
- Assuming all periodic trends have no exceptions; **Be–B** and **N–O** are important anomalies in ionization enthalpy.
- Mixing up **electron gain enthalpy** with **electronegativity**; the former is an energy change, whereas the latter is a relative tendency in a chemical bond.
- Forgetting that **fluorine** does not have the most negative electron gain enthalpy; **chlorine** does.
- Assuming **hydrogen** fits perfectly into one group; its position remains unique due to its dual characteristics.
- Ignoring the role of **effective nuclear charge** and **shielding effect** in explaining periodic trends.
- Confusing **periods** with **groups**.
- Assuming all oxides are either acidic or basic; some, such as **Al₂O₃**, are **amphoteric**.

---

# Chapter Summary

- The classification of elements evolved through **Döbereiner's Triads**, **Newlands' Law of Octaves**, **Mendeleev's Periodic Table**, and finally the **Modern Periodic Table**.
- The **Modern Periodic Law**, proposed by **Henry Moseley**, states that the properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.
- The modern periodic table consists of **7 periods**, **18 groups**, and four blocks: **s**, **p**, **d**, and **f**.
- **Atomic radius** decreases across a period and increases down a group due to changes in effective nuclear charge and shielding.
- **Ionization enthalpy** generally increases across a period and decreases down a group, with important anomalies for **Be/B** and **N/O**.
- **Electron gain enthalpy** generally becomes more negative across a period, while **electronegativity** increases from left to right and decreases down a group.
- **Metallic character** decreases across a period and increases down a group, whereas **non-metallic character** shows the opposite trend.
- **Diagonal relationships** exist between certain second- and third-period elements because of similarities in size, charge density, and electronegativity.
- Periodic trends can be explained using **effective nuclear charge**, **shielding effect**, **atomic size**, and **electronic configuration**, enabling the prediction of the chemical behaviour of elements.

# Common Mistakes

- Assuming every molecule follows the **octet rule**; compounds like **BF₃**, **PCl₅**, and **SF₆** are important exceptions.
- Confusing **ionic bonds** with **coordinate covalent bonds**.
- Mixing up **bond length** and **bond enthalpy**.
- Forgetting that **σ bonds are stronger** than **π bonds**.
- Assuming all molecules containing polar bonds are polar; molecular geometry must also be considered (e.g., CO₂ is non-polar despite polar C=O bonds).
- Using the incorrect molecular orbital energy order for **B₂–N₂** and **O₂–F₂**.
- Forgetting that **O₂ is paramagnetic**, a key success of Molecular Orbital Theory.
- Confusing **hybridization** with **molecular geometry**; for example, NH₃ is **sp³ hybridized** but has a **trigonal pyramidal** shape due to one lone pair.

# Chapter Summary

- Atoms form **chemical bonds** to attain greater stability, usually by achieving noble gas electronic configurations.
- The **octet rule** explains the formation of many ionic and covalent compounds but has important exceptions involving incomplete, expanded, and odd-electron octets.
- **Ionic bonds** involve electron transfer, while **covalent bonds** involve electron sharing. **Coordinate bonds** are formed when both shared electrons are donated by the same atom.
- **Lewis structures** and **formal charges** help represent and compare molecular structures.
- Bond parameters such as **bond length**, **bond angle**, **bond enthalpy**, and **bond order** determine molecular stability and properties.
- **VSEPR theory** predicts molecular shapes based on electron pair repulsions around the central atom.
- **Valence Bond Theory** explains covalent bond formation through orbital overlap and introduces **hybridization** to account for observed molecular geometries.
- **Molecular Orbital Theory** describes the formation of bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals, successfully explaining **bond order**, **bond stability**, and the **paramagnetic nature of O₂**.
- **Hydrogen bonding** and other intermolecular forces significantly influence physical properties such as boiling point, melting point, viscosity, and solubility.

# Introduction

Thermodynamics is the branch of chemistry that deals with the **relationship between heat, work, and energy** during physical and chemical changes.

It helps answer questions such as:

# 20. Relationship between Gibbs Energy and Equilibrium

At equilibrium,


- T = Temperature (K)
- K = Equilibrium constant

- **K > 1** → ΔG° is negative (products favored).
- **K < 1** → ΔG° is positive (reactants favored).
- **K = 1** → ΔG° = 0.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **heat (q)** with **temperature**; heat is energy in transit, whereas temperature measures the average kinetic energy of particles.
- Mixing up **internal energy (U)** and **enthalpy (H)**.
- Using the wrong **sign convention** for heat and work.
- Forgetting that **heat** and **work** are **path functions**, while **U**, **H**, **S**, and **G** are **state functions**.
- Assuming every exothermic reaction is spontaneous; **entropy** must also be considered.
- Forgetting the relationship **ΔG = ΔH − TΔS** while predicting spontaneity.
- Using **°C** instead of **Kelvin** in thermodynamic equations involving temperature.
- Ignoring the sign of **Δn(g)** when applying the equation **ΔH = ΔU + Δn(g)RT**.

- **Thermodynamics** studies the interconversion of heat and other forms of energy during physical and chemical processes.
- A **system** may be open, closed, or isolated, depending on whether it exchanges matter and/or energy with its surroundings.
- The **First Law of Thermodynamics** expresses the conservation of energy through the equation **ΔU = q + w**.
- **Internal energy (U)** and **enthalpy (H)** are state functions used to describe the energy content of a system.
- Various standard enthalpy changes, including **formation**, **combustion**, **atomization**, **neutralization**, and **solution**, help quantify heat changes in chemical reactions.
- **Hess's Law** enables the calculation of reaction enthalpies by combining known thermochemical equations.
- The **Second Law of Thermodynamics** introduces **entropy (S)** as a measure of disorder and explains the direction of spontaneous processes.
- **Gibbs free energy (ΔG)** combines enthalpy and entropy to predict spontaneity, with **ΔG < 0** indicating a spontaneous process.
- The relationship **ΔG° = −RT ln K** connects thermodynamics with chemical equilibrium, linking free energy changes to the equilibrium constant.
- **Calorimetry** and the equation **q = mcΔT** are widely used to measure heat changes experimentally.

# Introduction

Many chemical reactions do not proceed to completion. Instead, they reach a state where the **forward** and **backward** reactions occur at equal rates. This state is called **chemical equilibrium**.

Equilibrium also exists in aqueous solutions involving acids, bases, and salts, known as **ionic equilibrium**.

Understanding equilibrium is essential for predicting reaction direction, product yield, pH, and solubility.

---

# 1. Equilibrium

## Definition

Equilibrium is the state at which the **rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the backward reaction**.

At equilibrium:

- Concentrations remain constant.
- Reactions continue in both directions.
- It is a **dynamic** state.

---

## Dynamic Nature

```
N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃

Forward Rate

=

Backward Rate
```

Although no visible change occurs, molecules continue reacting.

---

# 2. Types of Equilibrium

## (A) Physical Equilibrium

Occurs during physical processes.

Examples

- Melting of ice
- Evaporation of water
- Dissolution of sugar

---

## (B) Chemical Equilibrium

Occurs during reversible chemical reactions.

Example

```
H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI
```

---

# 3. Characteristics of Chemical Equilibrium

- Dynamic in nature.
- Established only in a closed system.
- Forward and backward reaction rates are equal.
- Concentrations remain constant.
- Can be approached from either direction.

---

# 4. Law of Mass Action

### Proposed by

**Guldberg and Waage**

### Statement

At constant temperature, the rate of a chemical reaction is proportional to the product of the active masses (concentrations) of the reactants.

---

# 5. Equilibrium Constant (K)

For the reaction:

```
aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD
```

The equilibrium constant is

```
Kc

=

[C]^c [D]^d

──────────────

[A]^a [B]^b
```

where concentrations are taken at equilibrium.

---

# 6. Equilibrium Constant (Kc)

## Characteristics

- Constant at a given temperature.
- Independent of initial concentration.
- Independent of catalyst.
- Indicates the extent of reaction.

---

## Interpretation of Kc

| Value of Kc | Interpretation              |
| ----------- | --------------------------- |
| Kc >> 1     | Products predominate        |
| Kc << 1     | Reactants predominate       |
| Kc ≈ 1      | Appreciable amounts of both |

---

# 7. Equilibrium Constant (Kp)

For gaseous reactions,

```
Kp

=

(Pc)^c (Pd)^d

──────────────

(Pa)^a (Pb)^b
```

where P represents equilibrium partial pressures.

---

## Relationship Between Kp and Kc

```

```

where

```
Δn

=

Moles of gaseous products

−

Moles of gaseous reactants
```

| --------- | -------------------------- |

| Q > K     | Reaction proceeds backward |

# 9. Factors Affecting Equilibrium

---

## (A) Concentration

Increasing concentration of reactants shifts equilibrium toward products.

Removing products also shifts equilibrium toward products.

---

## (B) Pressure

Applicable only to gaseous reactions.

Increase in pressure shifts equilibrium toward the side having fewer gaseous moles.

---

## (C) Temperature

For exothermic reactions,

```
Heat + Products
```

Increasing temperature shifts equilibrium backward.

For endothermic reactions,

Increasing temperature shifts equilibrium forward.

---

## (D) Catalyst

A catalyst:

- Does not change K.
- Does not shift equilibrium.
- Helps equilibrium to be attained faster.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **equilibrium** with the completion of a reaction; at equilibrium, both forward and backward reactions continue at equal rates.
- Assuming a **catalyst** changes the equilibrium constant or equilibrium position; it only speeds up the attainment of equilibrium.
- Forgetting to include only **gaseous species** while calculating **Δn** in the equation **Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn**.
- Mixing up **Ka** and **Kb** or assuming a larger value always means a weaker acid or base.
- Using **concentrations instead of equilibrium concentrations** in the expression for Kc.
- Forgetting the relation **pH + pOH = 14** at **25°C**.
- Confusing **common ion effect** with **buffer action**; the former suppresses ionization, whereas the latter resists changes in pH.
- Incorrectly predicting the nature of salt solutions without considering the strengths of the parent acid and base.

# Chapter Summary

- **Chemical equilibrium** is a dynamic state where the rates of the forward and backward reactions are equal.
- The **Law of Mass Action** leads to the expressions for the equilibrium constants **Kc** and **Kp**, which measure the extent of a reaction.
- The **reaction quotient (Q)** helps predict the direction in which a reaction will proceed to attain equilibrium.
- **Le Chatelier's Principle** explains how equilibrium shifts in response to changes in concentration, pressure, and temperature.
- The **Arrhenius**, **Brønsted-Lowry**, and **Lewis** theories provide progressively broader definitions of acids and bases.
- The strength of weak acids and bases is measured using **Ka** and **Kb**, while the **ionic product of water (Kw)** forms the basis of the **pH scale**.
- **Buffer solutions** resist changes in pH and play an important role in biological and industrial systems.
- The **solubility product (Ksp)** describes the solubility of sparingly soluble salts, and the **common ion effect** reduces the ionization or solubility of weak electrolytes.
- **Salt hydrolysis** explains why solutions of certain salts are acidic, basic, or neutral depending on the strengths of the parent acid and base.

Identify the oxidizing and reducing agents in the reaction

**Solution**

### Example 4

State whether the following is oxidation or reduction:

```
Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻
```

**Solution**

The oxidation number of iron increases from +2 to +3 and an electron is lost.

Therefore, it is **oxidation**.

---

### Example 5

Why is hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) considered a disproportionation reagent in the reaction

```
2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂
```

**Solution**

In H₂O₂, the oxidation number of oxygen is −1.

During the reaction:

- Oxygen changes from −1 to −2 in H₂O (reduction).
- Oxygen changes from −1 to 0 in O₂ (oxidation).

Thus, the same element undergoes both oxidation and reduction, making it a **disproportionation reaction**.

### Example 6

Balance the following reaction in acidic medium using the ion-electron method:


Half-reactions:

```
Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻
```

Reduction:

```
MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O
```

Multiply the oxidation half-reaction by 5 and add:

```
5Fe²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺

↓

5Fe³⁺ + Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O
```

This is the balanced redox equation.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **oxidation** with the addition of oxygen only; oxidation also includes **loss of electrons** or **increase in oxidation number**.
- Assigning incorrect oxidation numbers in compounds such as **peroxides**, **superoxides**, and **OF₂**.
- Forgetting that **fluorine always has an oxidation number of −1** in its compounds.
- Mixing up the **oxidizing agent** and the **reducing agent**; the oxidizing agent is itself reduced, while the reducing agent is itself oxidized.
- Ignoring the balancing of **charge** when using the **ion-electron method**.
- Forgetting to add **H₂O** and **H⁺** while balancing redox reactions in acidic medium.
- Confusing **disproportionation** with **comproportionation** reactions.
- Using oxidation numbers instead of actual ionic charges in half-reactions.

# Chapter Summary

- **Redox reactions** involve the simultaneous occurrence of **oxidation** and **reduction**, accompanied by electron transfer.
- **Oxidation** is the loss of electrons or increase in oxidation number, whereas **reduction** is the gain of electrons or decrease in oxidation number.
- The **oxidizing agent** accepts electrons and undergoes reduction, while the **reducing agent** donates electrons and undergoes oxidation.
- **Oxidation numbers** are assigned using a set of standard rules and are essential for identifying redox changes.
- Redox reactions can be classified as **combination**, **decomposition**, **displacement**, **disproportionation**, and **comproportionation** reactions.
- Redox equations can be balanced using the **oxidation number method** or the **ion-electron (half-reaction) method**, especially in acidic or basic media.
- Redox processes are fundamental to **corrosion**, **combustion**, **respiration**, **photosynthesis**, **electrochemistry**, and many industrial processes.
- Understanding oxidation numbers, electron transfer, and balancing methods provides the foundation for studying electrochemistry and oxidation–reduction processes in higher chemistry.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **functional groups** with substituents.
- Incorrectly selecting the **longest carbon chain** during IUPAC naming.
- Forgetting to give the **lowest possible number** to the principal functional group.
- Mixing up the **inductive effect** (permanent σ-electron displacement) with the **electromeric effect** (temporary π-electron displacement).
- Confusing **resonance** with **hyperconjugation**.
- Assuming **homolytic cleavage** produces ions; it produces **free radicals**.
- Reversing the roles of **electrophiles** and **nucleophiles**.
- Forgetting that **Kjeldahl's method** cannot estimate nitrogen in **nitro**, **azo**, and some other nitrogen-containing compounds.

# Chapter Summary

- Organic chemistry deals primarily with the chemistry of **carbon compounds**, whose diversity arises from **tetravalency** and **catenation**.
- Organic compounds are classified as **acyclic**, **cyclic**, **alicyclic**, **aromatic**, and **heterocyclic**, and are identified by their **functional groups**.
- The **IUPAC system** provides a systematic method for naming organic compounds.
- **Electronic displacement effects**—including the **inductive effect**, **electromeric effect**, **resonance**, and **hyperconjugation**—help explain the stability and reactivity of organic molecules.
- Organic reactions proceed through intermediates such as **carbocations**, **carbanions**, and **free radicals**, which are formed by **heterolytic** or **homolytic** bond cleavage.
- Chemical reactions involve the attack of **electrophiles** and **nucleophiles**, leading to **substitution**, **addition**, **elimination**, or **rearrangement** reactions.
- Organic compounds are purified using methods such as **crystallization**, **distillation**, **sublimation**, **chromatography**, and **extraction**.
- The elemental composition of organic compounds is determined using qualitative tests such as **Lassaigne's test** and quantitative methods including the **Kjeldahl**, **Dumas**, and **Carius** methods.

### Example 5

Why are terminal alkynes acidic?

**Solution**

In terminal alkynes, the acidic hydrogen is attached to an **sp-hybridized carbon atom**, which has greater s-character and higher electronegativity. This stabilizes the conjugate base after the proton is removed, making terminal alkynes weakly acidic.

### Example 6

Why does benzene undergo substitution rather than addition reactions?

**Solution**

Addition reactions would destroy the delocalized π-electron cloud responsible for benzene's aromatic stability. Substitution reactions replace a hydrogen atom while preserving aromaticity, making them energetically more favorable.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **alkanes**, **alkenes**, and **alkynes** along with their general formulae.
- Mixing up **Markovnikov's rule** with the **Anti-Markovnikov rule**.
- Forgetting that the **peroxide effect occurs only with HBr** and not with HCl or HI.
- Assuming all alkynes are acidic; only **terminal alkynes** possess acidic hydrogen atoms.
- Confusing **staggered** and **eclipsed** conformations in Newman projections.
- Drawing benzene as a molecule with alternating single and double bonds instead of recognizing its **resonance hybrid**.
- Assuming benzene undergoes addition reactions under ordinary conditions; it predominantly undergoes **electrophilic substitution**.
- Forgetting that **ozonolysis** is used to determine the **position of the double bond** in alkenes.

# Chapter Summary

- **Hydrocarbons** are organic compounds composed only of carbon and hydrogen and are classified into **aliphatic** (alkanes, alkenes, alkynes) and **aromatic** hydrocarbons.
- **Alkanes** are saturated hydrocarbons that mainly undergo **free radical substitution reactions**, such as halogenation.
- The **staggered conformation** of ethane is more stable than the **eclipsed conformation** because it has lower torsional strain.
- **Alkenes** are unsaturated hydrocarbons that undergo **electrophilic addition reactions**, following **Markovnikov's rule** or the **Anti-Markovnikov rule** in the presence of peroxides.
- **Ozonolysis** is an important reaction used to identify the position of carbon-carbon double bonds.
- **Alkynes** contain carbon-carbon triple bonds, undergo addition reactions, and **terminal alkynes** exhibit weak acidic character due to the presence of an **sp-hybridized carbon atom**.
- **Benzene** is an aromatic compound with a planar hexagonal structure, delocalized π-electrons, and enhanced stability due to **resonance**.
- Aromatic hydrocarbons undergo **electrophilic substitution reactions**, including **nitration**, **halogenation**, **sulphonation**, and **Friedel-Crafts alkylation/acylation**, while retaining aromatic stability.
- The concepts learned in this chapter provide the foundation for understanding the chemistry of more complex organic compounds in higher classes.
