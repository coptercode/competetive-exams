# Introduction

Everything around us occupies space and has mass. The chair we sit on, the air we breathe, the water we drink, and even the perfume we smell are all examples of **matter**.

Matter exists in different forms called **states of matter**. The arrangement and movement of particles determine whether matter exists as a solid, liquid, or gas. Matter can also change from one state to another due to changes in temperature or pressure.

This chapter explains the nature of matter, the characteristics of particles, different states of matter, interconversion between states, evaporation, and its practical applications.

---

# 1. Matter

**Matter** is anything that has **mass** and **occupies space**.

### Examples

- Air
- Water
- Wood
- Iron
- Milk
- Oxygen
- Carbon dioxide

### Things That Are Not Matter

- Light
- Heat
- Sound
- Electricity

These are forms of **energy** and do not occupy space.

---

# 2. Physical Nature of Matter

Matter is made up of extremely tiny particles called **particles of matter**.

These particles are so small that they cannot be seen with the naked eye.

### Activity

Dissolve a small amount of potassium permanganate crystals in water.

Even after repeated dilution, the solution remains coloured, showing that one crystal contains millions of tiny particles.

---

# 3. Characteristics of Particles of Matter

## Particles Have Spaces Between Them

There are empty spaces between particles.

Example:

When sugar is dissolved in water, the sugar particles occupy the spaces between water particles without increasing the water level significantly.

---

## Particles Are Continuously Moving

Particles are always in motion.

This movement increases with temperature.

Example:

The smell of perfume spreads throughout a room because perfume particles move continuously in air.

---

## Particles Attract Each Other

Particles of matter attract one another.

The force of attraction varies in different substances.

- Strongest in solids
- Moderate in liquids
- Weakest in gases

---

# 4. States of Matter

Matter mainly exists in three states:

- Solid
- Liquid
- Gas

---

## Solids

A **solid** has a definite shape and definite volume.

### Properties

- Fixed shape
- Fixed volume
- High density
- Strong intermolecular force
- Very little compressibility
- Particles vibrate about fixed positions

### Examples

- Stone
- Ice
- Wood
- Iron

---

## Liquids

A **liquid** has a definite volume but no definite shape.

### Properties

- Definite volume
- No fixed shape
- Flows easily
- Moderate intermolecular force
- Slightly compressible

### Examples

- Water
- Milk
- Oil
- Petrol

---

## Gases

A **gas** has neither a definite shape nor a definite volume.

### Properties

- No fixed shape
- No fixed volume
- Highly compressible
- Very weak intermolecular force
- Particles move freely in all directions

### Examples

- Oxygen
- Nitrogen
- Carbon dioxide
- Hydrogen

---

# 5. Comparison Between Solids, Liquids and Gases

| Property            | Solids     | Liquids     | Gases       |
| ------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| Shape               | Fixed      | Not fixed   | Not fixed   |
| Volume              | Fixed      | Fixed       | Not fixed   |
| Compressibility     | Negligible | Very little | Very high   |
| Particle Motion     | Vibrate    | Slide       | Move freely |
| Force of Attraction | Strong     | Moderate    | Weak        |
| Diffusion           | Very slow  | Moderate    | Fast        |

---

# 6. Diffusion

**Diffusion** is the movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

### Examples

- Smell of perfume spreading in a room.
- Aroma of food reaching another room.
- Ink spreading in water.

### Factors Affecting Diffusion

- Temperature
- Density of the medium
- Size of particles

Diffusion is fastest in gases and slowest in solids.

---

# 7. Change of State

Matter changes from one state to another by changing **temperature** or **pressure**.

---

## Melting

The conversion of a solid into a liquid on heating.

Example:

```
Ice → Water
```

### Melting Point

The temperature at which a solid changes into a liquid.

Example:

Ice melts at

```
0°C
```

or

```
273 K
```

---

## Freezing

The conversion of a liquid into a solid on cooling.

Example:

```
Water → Ice
```

---

## Boiling

The conversion of a liquid into vapour at a fixed temperature.

Example:

```
Water → Steam
```

### Boiling Point

The temperature at which a liquid changes into vapour.

For water,

```
100°C
```

or

```
373 K
```

---

## Condensation

The conversion of a gas into a liquid.

Example:

```
Steam → Water
```

---

## Sublimation

The direct conversion of a solid into vapour without passing through the liquid state.

Examples

- Camphor
- Naphthalene balls
- Dry ice
- Ammonium chloride

The reverse process is called **deposition**.

---

# 8. Effect of Temperature on Matter

Heating increases the kinetic energy of particles.

As particles move faster:

- Intermolecular attraction decreases.
- Particles move farther apart.
- Change of state occurs.

---

# 9. Latent Heat

**Latent heat** is the heat absorbed or released during a change of state without any change in temperature.

### Latent Heat of Fusion

Heat required to convert **1 kg of solid into liquid** at its melting point without changing the temperature.

Example:

Ice at **0°C** changes into water at **0°C** by absorbing latent heat.

---

### Latent Heat of Vaporisation

Heat required to convert **1 kg of liquid into vapour** at its boiling point without changing the temperature.

Water at **100°C** changes into steam at **100°C** by absorbing latent heat.

---

# 10. Effect of Pressure on Matter

Increasing pressure brings gas particles closer together.

As pressure increases and temperature decreases, gases can be liquefied.

Examples

- Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG)
- Compressed Natural Gas (CNG)
- Liquid oxygen used in hospitals

---

# 11. Evaporation

**Evaporation** is the slow conversion of a liquid into vapour from its surface at any temperature below its boiling point.

Unlike boiling, evaporation occurs only at the surface of the liquid.

---

# 12. Factors Affecting Evaporation

## Surface Area

Greater surface area leads to faster evaporation.

Example:

Water spread on a plate evaporates faster than water in a glass.

---

## Temperature

Higher temperature increases the rate of evaporation.

---

## Humidity

Humidity is the amount of water vapour present in air.

Higher humidity decreases evaporation because the air already contains a large amount of water vapour.

---

## Wind Speed

Higher wind speed increases evaporation by removing water vapour from the surface.

---

# 13. Cooling Effect of Evaporation

During evaporation, the particles with higher kinetic energy escape from the liquid.

The remaining particles lose energy, causing the liquid to cool.

### Everyday Examples

- Sweating cools the human body.
- Water kept in an earthen pot becomes cool.
- Acetone on the skin produces a cooling sensation.
- Desert coolers work by evaporation.

---

# 14. Kelvin Scale

The SI unit of temperature is **Kelvin (K)**.

Conversion:

```
K = °C + 273
```

Example

```
25°C = 298 K
```

---

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **boiling** with **evaporation**.
- Assuming evaporation occurs only at the boiling point.
- Forgetting that diffusion occurs in solids as well, though very slowly.
- Mixing up melting point with boiling point.
- Writing the Kelvin conversion incorrectly.
- Believing gases have no mass because they are invisible.
- Confusing latent heat of fusion with latent heat of vaporisation.

---

# Key Definitions

| Term          | Definition                                                                    |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Matter        | Anything that has mass and occupies space                                     |
| Diffusion     | Movement of particles from higher to lower concentration                      |
| Melting       | Solid changing into liquid                                                    |
| Boiling       | Liquid changing into gas at a fixed temperature                               |
| Evaporation   | Slow conversion of a liquid into vapour from its surface                      |
| Condensation  | Gas changing into liquid                                                      |
| Sublimation   | Direct conversion of solid into gas                                           |
| Latent Heat   | Heat absorbed or released during a change of state without temperature change |

| Boiling Point | Temperature at which a liquid boils                                           |

---

# Chapter Summary

- Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.
- Matter is made up of tiny particles that have spaces between them, are continuously moving, and attract one another.
- Matter exists mainly in three states: **solid, liquid, and gas**.
- Solids have fixed shape and volume, liquids have fixed volume but no fixed shape, while gases have neither fixed shape nor fixed volume.
- Diffusion is fastest in gases and slowest in solids.
- Matter changes its state due to changes in temperature and pressure.
- Important changes of state include melting, freezing, boiling, condensation, and sublimation.
- Latent heat is absorbed or released during a change of state without any change in temperature.
- Evaporation occurs at all temperatures and is affected by surface area, temperature, humidity, and wind speed.
- Evaporation causes cooling because high-energy particles escape from the liquid.
- The SI unit of temperature is **Kelvin (K)**, and the conversion formula is **K = °C + 273**.

# Introduction

In everyday life, we come across different kinds of substances such as milk, air, sugar, salt, water, and soil. Some of these substances consist of only one kind of particle, while others contain two or more different substances mixed together.

Based on their composition, substances are classified as **pure substances** and **mixtures**. Pure substances have a fixed composition and definite properties, whereas mixtures contain two or more substances physically combined in varying proportions.

This chapter explains the difference between pure substances and mixtures, various types of mixtures, methods of separating their components, and the distinction between physical and chemical changes.

---

# 1. Pure Substance

A **pure substance** is a material made up of only one type of particle and has a fixed composition.

Pure substances have:

- Uniform composition.
- Definite melting and boiling points.
- Characteristic physical and chemical properties.

Examples:

- Gold (Au)
- Oxygen (O₂)
- Distilled water (H₂O)
- Sodium chloride (NaCl)

Pure substances are classified into:

- Elements
- Compounds

---

# 2. Element

An **element** is a pure substance that consists of only one kind of atom.

An element cannot be broken down into simpler substances by ordinary chemical methods.

### Types of Elements

#### Metals

Properties:

- Good conductors of heat and electricity.
- Lustrous.
- Malleable.
- Ductile.

Examples:

- Iron
- Copper
- Aluminium
- Gold

---

#### Non-metals

Properties:

- Poor conductors of heat and electricity.
- Brittle.
- Generally dull.

Examples:

- Oxygen
- Sulphur
- Carbon
- Nitrogen

---

#### Metalloids

Metalloids possess properties of both metals and non-metals.

Examples:

- Silicon
- Germanium
- Boron

---

# 3. Compound

A **compound** is a pure substance formed when two or more elements combine chemically in a fixed ratio.

### Characteristics

- Fixed composition.
- New properties different from constituent elements.
- Components cannot be separated by physical methods.
- Can be decomposed only by chemical methods.

Examples:

- Water (H₂O)
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
- Sodium chloride (NaCl)

---

# 4. Mixture

A **mixture** is a physical combination of two or more substances in any proportion.

### Characteristics

- Variable composition.
- Components retain their individual properties.
- Can be separated by physical methods.
- No new substance is formed.

Examples:

- Air
- Milk
- Soil
- Salt solution

---

# 5. Types of Mixtures

## Homogeneous Mixture

A **homogeneous mixture** has a uniform composition throughout.

The different components cannot be seen separately.

Examples:

- Salt solution
- Sugar solution
- Air
- Vinegar

---

## Heterogeneous Mixture

A **heterogeneous mixture** has a non-uniform composition.

The different components can often be distinguished.

Examples:

- Sand and water
- Oil and water
- Soil
- Granite

---

# 6. Solution

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances.

### Components of a Solution

#### Solute

The substance that dissolves.

Example:

Salt in salt water.

---

#### Solvent

The substance in which the solute dissolves.

Example:

Water in salt solution.

---

### Properties of a Solution

- Homogeneous.
- Transparent.
- Stable.
- Solute particles cannot be seen.
- Do not scatter light.

---

# 7. Concentration of a Solution

The **concentration** of a solution indicates the amount of solute present in a given quantity of solution or solvent.

### Mass by Mass Percentage

```
Mass % = (Mass of Solute / Mass of Solution) × 100
```

---

### Mass by Volume Percentage

```
Mass % = (Mass of Solute / Volume of Solution) × 100
```

---

### Volume by Volume Percentage

```
Volume % = (Volume of Solute / Volume of Solution) × 100
```

---

# 8. Suspension

A **suspension** is a heterogeneous mixture in which insoluble particles are dispersed throughout a liquid.

### Properties

- Heterogeneous.
- Particles are visible.
- Particles settle on standing.
- Can be separated by filtration.
- Scatter light.

Examples:

- Muddy water.
- Chalk powder in water.

---

# 9. Colloid

A **colloid** is a heterogeneous mixture in which very small particles remain uniformly dispersed.

### Properties

- Particles do not settle.
- Cannot be separated by ordinary filtration.
- Scatter light.
- Stable.

Examples:

- Milk.
- Fog.
- Smoke.
- Jelly.
- Butter.

---

# 10. Tyndall Effect

The **Tyndall Effect** is the scattering of light by colloidal particles.

### Conditions

- Observed in colloids.
- Not observed in true solutions.

### Examples

- Sunlight passing through fog.
- Light entering a dusty room.
- Car headlights in mist.

---

# 11. Difference Between Solution, Suspension, and Colloid

| Property       | Solution     | Suspension    | Colloid                    |
| -------------- | ------------ | ------------- | -------------------------- |
| Nature         | Homogeneous  | Heterogeneous | Heterogeneous              |
| Particle Size  | Very small   | Large         | Intermediate               |
| Visibility     | Not visible  | Visible       | Not visible to naked eye   |
| Settling       | No           | Yes           | No                         |
| Filtration     | Not possible | Possible      | Not by ordinary filtration |
| Tyndall Effect | No           | Yes           | Yes                        |

---

# 12. Physical Change

A **physical change** is a change in which no new substance is formed.

### Characteristics

- Usually reversible.
- Physical properties may change.
- Chemical composition remains unchanged.

Examples:

- Melting of ice.
- Boiling of water.
- Cutting paper.
- Breaking glass.

---

# 13. Chemical Change

A **chemical change** is a change in which one or more new substances are formed.

### Characteristics

- Usually irreversible.
- New substances are produced.
- Chemical composition changes.
- Energy may be released or absorbed.

Examples:

- Rusting of iron.
- Burning paper.
- Digestion of food.
- Cooking food.

---

# 14. Separation Techniques

## Handpicking

Used to separate large impurities by hand.

Example:

Removing stones from rice.

---

## Winnowing

Used to separate lighter particles from heavier ones using wind.

Example:

Separating husk from grains.

---

## Sieving

Used to separate particles based on size.

Example:

Separating bran from flour.

---

## Sedimentation and Decantation

Heavier particles settle at the bottom during sedimentation.

The clear liquid is carefully poured off during decantation.

Example:

Separating sand from water.

---

## Filtration

Used to separate insoluble solids from liquids.

Example:

Separating chalk powder from water.

---

## Evaporation

Used to recover dissolved solids from a solution.

Example:

Obtaining salt from seawater.

---

## Centrifugation

Used to separate particles by spinning at high speed.

Example:

Separating cream from milk.

---

## Sublimation

Used to separate substances that sublime.

Examples:

- Camphor.
- Ammonium chloride.

---

## Chromatography

Used to separate coloured substances based on their movement through a medium.

Examples:

- Separating colours in ink.
- Identifying pigments.

---

## Distillation

Used to separate a solvent from a solution by boiling and condensation.

Example:

Obtaining distilled water from salt solution.

---

## Fractional Distillation

Used to separate miscible liquids with different boiling points.

Examples:

- Separation of oxygen and nitrogen from liquid air.
- Separation of petroleum products.

# Key Definitions

| Term            | Definition                                                |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pure Substance  | Substance containing only one type of particle            |
| Element         | Pure substance made of one kind of atom                   |
| Compound        | Pure substance formed by chemical combination of elements |
| Mixture         | Physical combination of two or more substances            |
| Solution        | Homogeneous mixture of solute and solvent                 |
| Suspension      | Heterogeneous mixture with visible particles that settle  |
| Colloid         | Heterogeneous mixture with particles that do not settle   |

# Chapter Summary

- Matter can be classified as **pure substances** and **mixtures**.
- Pure substances include **elements** and **compounds**.
- Mixtures may be **homogeneous** or **heterogeneous**.
- A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture consisting of a **solute** and a **solvent**.
- **Suspensions** have large particles that settle on standing, while **colloids** have intermediate-sized particles that remain dispersed.
- The **Tyndall Effect** is shown by colloids and suspensions but not by true solutions.
- **Physical changes** do not produce new substances, whereas **chemical changes** do.
- Different separation techniques are used depending on the nature of the mixture.
- Understanding the composition and separation of matter is essential in chemistry and many industrial processes.

# Introduction

Everything around us is made up of tiny particles called **atoms** and **molecules**. These particles are so small that they cannot be seen with the naked eye. The concept of atoms was first proposed by ancient Indian and Greek philosophers, but it was **John Dalton** who provided the first scientific theory explaining the nature of atoms.

This chapter introduces the fundamental laws of chemical combination, Dalton's Atomic Theory, the concepts of atoms, molecules, ions, valency, chemical formulae, molecular mass, and formula unit mass.

---

# 1. Laws of Chemical Combination

Chemical reactions follow certain fundamental laws that explain how substances combine.

---

## Law of Conservation of Mass

**Statement**

The **Law of Conservation of Mass** states that:

> **Mass can neither be created nor destroyed during a chemical reaction.**

The total mass of the reactants is always equal to the total mass of the products.

### Example

```
Carbon + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide
```

If

- Carbon = **12 g**
- Oxygen = **32 g**

Then,

- Carbon dioxide formed = **44 g**

```
12 g + 32 g = 44 g
```

---

## Law of Constant Proportions

**Statement**

The **Law of Constant Proportions** states that:

> **A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements combined in the same fixed proportion by mass.**

### Example

Water (**H₂O**) always contains:

- Hydrogen = **1 part by mass**
- Oxygen = **8 parts by mass**

The ratio remains the same regardless of the source of water.

---

# 2. Dalton's Atomic Theory

John Dalton proposed the Atomic Theory in **1808**.

### Postulates of Dalton's Atomic Theory

- All matter is made up of tiny particles called **atoms**.
- Atoms are indivisible and cannot be created or destroyed in chemical reactions.
- All atoms of the same element are identical in mass and properties.
- Atoms of different elements have different masses and properties.
- Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
- In a given compound, the relative number and kinds of atoms remain fixed.

### Limitations

Modern discoveries showed that:

- Atoms are divisible into subatomic particles.
- Atoms of the same element may have different masses (isotopes).
- Atoms can be converted into energy during nuclear reactions.

---

# 3. Atom

An **atom** is the smallest particle of an element that retains its chemical properties.

Atoms are extremely small.

Examples:

- Hydrogen atom
- Oxygen atom
- Sodium atom

Some elements exist as individual atoms.

Examples:

- Helium
- Neon
- Argon

These are called **monoatomic elements**.

---

# 4. Molecule

A **molecule** is formed when two or more atoms combine chemically.

It is the smallest particle of a substance that can exist independently.

### Molecules of Elements

Examples:

- Hydrogen (H₂)
- Oxygen (O₂)
- Nitrogen (N₂)
- Ozone (O₃)

---

### Molecules of Compounds

Examples:

- Water (H₂O)
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
- Ammonia (NH₃)
- Methane (CH₄)

---

# 5. Atomicity

**Atomicity** is the number of atoms present in one molecule of an element.

### Types of Atomicity

| Type         | Example    |
| ------------ | ---------- |
| Monoatomic   | He, Ne     |
| Diatomic     | H₂, O₂, N₂ |
| Triatomic    | O₃         |
| Tetra-atomic | P₄         |
| Octa-atomic  | S₈         |

---

# 6. Ion

An **ion** is an atom or a group of atoms carrying an electric charge.

Ions are formed when atoms lose or gain electrons.

---

## Cation

A **cation** is a positively charged ion formed by the loss of electrons.

Examples:

- Na⁺
- K⁺
- Ca²⁺
- Mg²⁺

---

## Anion

An **anion** is a negatively charged ion formed by the gain of electrons.

Examples:

- Cl⁻
- O²⁻
- SO₄²⁻
- NO₃⁻

---

# 7. Valency

**Valency** is the combining capacity of an atom.

It indicates the number of electrons lost, gained, or shared during chemical combination.

### Examples

| Element   | Valency |
| --------- | ------- |
| Hydrogen  | 1       |
| Oxygen    | 2       |
| Nitrogen  | 3       |
| Carbon    | 4       |
| Sodium    | 1       |
| Calcium   | 2       |
| Aluminium | 3       |

---

# 8. Writing Chemical Formulae

Chemical formulae are written by combining the symbols of elements according to their valencies.

### Steps

1. Write the symbols.
2. Write the valencies.
3. Interchange the valencies.
4. Reduce to the simplest ratio if required.

### Example

Sodium Chloride

```
Na → 1
Cl → 1
```

Formula

```
NaCl
```

---

### Example

Magnesium Chloride

```
Mg → 2
Cl → 1
```

Formula

```
MgCl₂
```

---

### Example

Aluminium Oxide

```
Al → 3
O → 2
```

Formula

```
Al₂O₃
```

---

# 9. Molecular Mass

The **molecular mass** is the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms present in one molecule.

### Formula

```
Molecular Mass = Sum of Atomic Masses
```

### Example

Water

```
H₂O
```

Atomic masses:

- H = 1 u
- O = 16 u

```
Molecular Mass
= (2 × 1) + 16
= 18 u
```

---

### Example

Carbon Dioxide

```
CO₂
```

```
= 12 + (2 × 16)
= 44 u
```

---

# 10. Formula Unit Mass

The **formula unit mass** is the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms present in the formula unit of an ionic compound.

### Example

Sodium Chloride

```
NaCl
```

```
Na = 23 u
Cl = 35.5 u
```

```
Formula Unit Mass
= 23 + 35.5
= 58.5 u
```

---

# 11. Significance of Chemical Formulae

A chemical formula tells us:

- Elements present in a compound.
- Number of atoms of each element.
- Ratio of atoms.
- Molecular or formula unit mass.

Example:

```
H₂SO₄
```

Contains:

- 2 Hydrogen atoms
- 1 Sulphur atom
- 4 Oxygen atoms

# Introduction

Atoms are the basic building blocks of matter. Earlier, atoms were believed to be indivisible, but later scientific discoveries revealed that they are made up of smaller particles called **subatomic particles**. The study of these particles led to the development of different atomic models, helping scientists understand the structure of the atom.

This chapter discusses the discovery of electrons, protons, and neutrons, various atomic models proposed by scientists, electronic configuration, valency, atomic number, mass number, isotopes, and isobars.

---

# 1. Discovery of Subatomic Particles

Atoms are made up of three fundamental particles:

- Electron
- Proton
- Neutron

| Particle | Symbol | Charge | Relative Mass | Location            |
| -------- | ------ | ------ | ------------- | ------------------- |
| Electron | e⁻     | -1     | 1/1836 u      | Outside the nucleus |
| Proton   | p⁺     | +1     | 1 u           | Inside the nucleus  |
| Neutron  | n⁰     | 0      | 1 u           | Inside the nucleus  |

---

## Electron

The **electron** was discovered by **J. J. Thomson** in **1897** through the **cathode ray experiment**.

### Characteristics

- Negatively charged.
- Present outside the nucleus.
- Very small mass compared to protons and neutrons.

---

## Proton

The **proton** was discovered by **Eugen Goldstein** through **canal ray experiments**.

### Characteristics

- Positively charged.
- Present inside the nucleus.
- Mass approximately equal to 1 u.

---

## Neutron

The **neutron** was discovered by **James Chadwick** in **1932**.

### Characteristics

- Electrically neutral.
- Located inside the nucleus.
- Mass nearly equal to that of a proton.

---

# 2. Thomson's Model of the Atom

J. J. Thomson proposed the **Plum Pudding Model**.

### Main Features

- The atom is a positively charged sphere.
- Electrons are embedded throughout the sphere.
- The total positive and negative charges are equal, making the atom electrically neutral.

### Limitation

This model could not explain the results of Rutherford's experiment.

---

# 3. Rutherford's Atomic Model

Ernest Rutherford proposed his atomic model based on the **alpha-particle scattering experiment**.

### Observations

- Most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil.
- Some were deflected through small angles.
- A very few were reflected back.

### Conclusions

- Most of the atom is empty space.
- A tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus is present at the centre.
- Electrons revolve around the nucleus.

### Limitation

According to classical physics, revolving electrons should continuously lose energy and eventually fall into the nucleus. Thus, the model could not explain the stability of atoms.

---

# 4. Bohr's Model of the Atom

Niels Bohr improved Rutherford's model by introducing fixed energy levels.

### Main Features

- Electrons revolve around the nucleus in fixed circular paths called **shells** or **orbits**.
- These shells have definite energy.
- Electrons do not lose energy while moving in these shells.
- Energy is emitted or absorbed only when an electron jumps from one shell to another.

The shells are named:

- K
- L
- M
- N

---

# 5. Electronic Configuration

The arrangement of electrons in different shells of an atom is called its **electronic configuration**.

### Maximum Number of Electrons in a Shell

The maximum number of electrons in a shell is given by:

```
2n²
```

where **n** is the shell number.

| Shell | Value of n | Maximum Electrons |
| ----- | ---------- | ----------------- |
| K     | 1          | 2                 |
| L     | 2          | 8                 |
| M     | 3          | 18                |
| N     | 4          | 32                |

For the first twenty elements:

- K shell can have a maximum of **2 electrons**.
- L shell can have a maximum of **8 electrons**.
- M shell is filled up to **8 electrons** before filling the next shell.

### Examples

#### Oxygen (Atomic Number = 8)

Electronic configuration:

```
2, 6
```

---

#### Sodium (Atomic Number = 11)

Electronic configuration:

```
2, 8, 1
```

---

#### Calcium (Atomic Number = 20)

Electronic configuration:

```
2, 8, 8, 2
```

---

# 6. Valency

**Valency** is the combining capacity of an atom.

It depends on the number of electrons present in the outermost shell (valence shell).

### Rules

- If the outermost shell has **1 to 4 electrons**, valency equals the number of electrons lost.
- If it has **5 to 7 electrons**, valency equals **8 − number of valence electrons**.
- Noble gases have valency **0** because their outermost shell is completely filled.

### Examples

| Element  | Electronic Configuration | Valency |
| -------- | ------------------------ | ------- |
| Hydrogen | 1                        | 1       |
| Carbon   | 2, 4                     | 4       |
| Oxygen   | 2, 6                     | 2       |
| Sodium   | 2, 8, 1                  | 1       |
| Chlorine | 2, 8, 7                  | 1       |
| Neon     | 2, 8                     | 0       |

---

# 7. Atomic Number

The **atomic number (Z)** is the number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom.

In a neutral atom:

```
Atomic Number = Number of Protons = Number of Electrons
```

### Example

Carbon has:

- Protons = 6
- Electrons = 6

Therefore,

```
Atomic Number = 6
```

---

# 8. Mass Number

The **mass number (A)** is the total number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus.

### Formula

```
Mass Number = Number of Protons + Number of Neutrons
```

or

```
A = Z + N
```

where:

- **A** = Mass number
- **Z** = Atomic number
- **N** = Number of neutrons

### Example

Sodium

- Protons = 11
- Neutrons = 12

```
Mass Number = 11 + 12 = 23
```

---

# 9. Isotopes

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element having:

- Same atomic number.
- Different mass numbers.

This is because they have different numbers of neutrons.

### Examples

Hydrogen:

- Protium (¹H)
- Deuterium (²H)
- Tritium (³H)

Chlorine:

- Cl-35
- Cl-37

### Applications of Isotopes

- Cobalt-60 is used in cancer treatment.
- Iodine-131 is used to diagnose thyroid disorders.
- Uranium-235 is used as a fuel in nuclear reactors.
- Carbon-14 is used in carbon dating.

---

# 10. Isobars

**Isobars** are atoms of different elements having:

- Same mass number.
- Different atomic numbers.

### Examples

- Calcium-40 (²⁰Ca⁴⁰)
- Argon-40 (¹⁸Ar⁴⁰)

---

# 11. Difference Between Isotopes and Isobars

| Isotopes                        | Isobars                           |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Same atomic number              | Same mass number                  |
| Different mass numbers          | Different atomic numbers          |
| Same element                    | Different elements                |
| Chemical properties are similar | Chemical properties are different |

- **Isotopes** have the same atomic number but different mass numbers, whereas **isobars** have the same mass number but different atomic numbers.
- The modern understanding of atomic structure forms the basis of chemistry and explains the properties of elements and compounds.

# Introduction

All living organisms are made up of tiny structural and functional units called **cells**. Just as bricks are the building blocks of a house, cells are the building blocks of life. Some organisms consist of a single cell, while others are made up of millions of cells working together.

The study of cells is called **Cytology**. This chapter explains the discovery of cells, Cell Theory, the structure and functions of cell organelles, and the differences between plant and animal cells.

---

# 1. Discovery of the Cell

The cell was first discovered by **Robert Hooke** in **1665** while observing a thin slice of cork under a microscope.

Later discoveries:

- **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** observed living cells for the first time.
- **Robert Brown** discovered the nucleus.
- **Matthias Schleiden** stated that all plants are made of cells.
- **Theodor Schwann** stated that all animals are made of cells.
- **Rudolf Virchow** proposed that all cells arise from pre-existing cells.

---

# 2. Cell Theory

The **Cell Theory** was proposed by **Schleiden** and **Schwann** and later modified by **Virchow**.

### Main Postulates

- All living organisms are made up of one or more cells.
- The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
- All cells arise from pre-existing cells.

---

# 3. Cell

A **cell** is the smallest structural and functional unit of life capable of carrying out all life processes.

### Types of Organisms

#### Unicellular Organisms

Made up of a single cell.

Examples:

- Amoeba
- Paramecium
- Euglena
- Bacteria

#### Multicellular Organisms

Made up of many cells.

Examples:

- Humans
- Mango tree
- Dog
- Frog

---

# 4. Shape and Size of Cells

Cells vary greatly in shape and size depending on their function.

### Examples

| Cell                 | Shape             |
| -------------------- | ----------------- |
| Red Blood Cell (RBC) | Biconcave disc    |
| Nerve Cell           | Long and branched |
| Muscle Cell          | Spindle-shaped    |
| Amoeba               | Irregular         |
| Plant Cell           | Rectangular       |

The **ostrich egg** is one of the largest cells, while **Mycoplasma** is among the smallest living cells.

---

# 5. Plasma Membrane (Cell Membrane)

The **plasma membrane** is the thin, flexible outer covering of the cell.

It separates the cell from its surroundings.

### Functions

- Protects the cell.
- Gives shape to the cell.
- Regulates the movement of substances into and out of the cell.
- Maintains the internal environment of the cell.

The plasma membrane is **selectively permeable**, allowing only certain substances to pass through.

---

# 6. Diffusion

**Diffusion** is the movement of molecules from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

### Examples

- Exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide in the lungs.
- Movement of gases in plant leaves.

---

# 7. Osmosis

**Osmosis** is the movement of water molecules through a selectively permeable membrane from a region of higher water concentration to a region of lower water concentration.

### Types of Solutions

#### Hypotonic Solution

- Lower concentration of solute.
- Water enters the cell.
- Cell swells.

---

#### Hypertonic Solution

- Higher concentration of solute.
- Water leaves the cell.
- Cell shrinks.

---

#### Isotonic Solution

- Equal concentration inside and outside the cell.
- No net movement of water.

---

# 8. Cell Wall

The **cell wall** is a rigid outer covering found only in plant cells.

It is mainly composed of **cellulose**.

### Functions

- Provides strength.
- Gives shape to the cell.
- Protects the cell.
- Prevents bursting due to water absorption.

Animal cells do not possess a cell wall.

---

# 9. Nucleus

The **nucleus** is the control centre of the cell.

### Structure

The nucleus consists of:

- Nuclear membrane
- Nucleoplasm
- Nucleolus
- Chromosomes

### Functions

- Controls all cellular activities.
- Stores hereditary information.
- Regulates cell division.
- Directs protein synthesis.

---

# 10. Chromosomes

Chromosomes are thread-like structures present inside the nucleus.

They contain **DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid)** and **genes**, which carry hereditary information from parents to offspring.

---

# 11. Cytoplasm

The **cytoplasm** is the jelly-like substance present between the nucleus and the plasma membrane.

### Functions

- Supports cell organelles.
- Site of many metabolic reactions.
- Helps in the transport of materials within the cell.

---

# 12. Cell Organelles

Cell organelles are specialized structures that perform specific functions.

---

## Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

The **Endoplasmic Reticulum** is a network of membrane-bound tubes.

### Types

### Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (RER)

- Ribosomes are present.
- Synthesizes proteins.

### Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (SER)

- Ribosomes are absent.
- Synthesizes lipids.
- Detoxifies harmful substances.

---

## Ribosomes

Ribosomes are tiny, non-membranous structures attached to the RER or found freely in the cytoplasm.

### Function

- Site of protein synthesis.

---

## Golgi Apparatus

The **Golgi Apparatus** consists of flattened membrane-bound sacs.

### Functions

- Modifies proteins and lipids.
- Packages materials for secretion.
- Forms lysosomes.

---

## Lysosomes

Lysosomes are membrane-bound sacs containing digestive enzymes.

### Functions

- Digest worn-out organelles.
- Destroy foreign particles.
- Break down waste materials.

They are often called the **"suicide bags"** of the cell because they may digest the cell during severe damage.

---

## Mitochondria

The **mitochondria** are known as the **powerhouses of the cell**.

### Functions

- Site of cellular respiration.
- Produce ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate), the energy currency of the cell.

Mitochondria have their own DNA and ribosomes.

---

## Plastids

Plastids are found only in plant cells.

### Types

#### Chloroplast

Contains chlorophyll and performs photosynthesis.

#### Chromoplast

Contains coloured pigments.

#### Leucoplast

Stores food such as starch, oils, and proteins.

---

## Vacuoles

Vacuoles are membrane-bound sacs filled with cell sap.

### Functions

- Store food and water.
- Maintain turgidity in plant cells.
- Store waste products.

Plant cells have one large central vacuole, whereas animal cells have small temporary vacuoles.

---

# 13. Difference Between Plant Cell and Animal Cell

| Plant Cell                    | Animal Cell                |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Cell wall present             | Cell wall absent           |
| Chloroplast present           | Chloroplast absent         |
| Large central vacuole         | Small vacuoles             |
| Usually rectangular           | Usually irregular or round |
| Centrioles absent (generally) | Centrioles present         |

# Chapter Summary

- The **cell** is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
- **Robert Hooke** discovered cells, while **Schleiden**, **Schwann**, and **Virchow** developed the **Cell Theory**.
- Cells may be **unicellular** or **multicellular** and vary in shape and size according to their functions.
- The **plasma membrane** controls the movement of substances, while the **cell wall** provides support and protection in plant cells.

- Plant cells differ from animal cells mainly in the presence of a **cell wall, chloroplasts,** and a **large central vacuole**.
- The coordinated functioning of cell organelles enables cells to carry out all life processes efficiently.

# Introduction

In multicellular organisms, similar cells group together to perform a specific function. Such a group of similar cells, along with their intercellular material, is called a **tissue**.

The study of tissues is known as **Histology**.

Plants and animals have different types of tissues because their body structures and functions differ. Plant tissues mainly provide growth, support, and transport, whereas animal tissues are specialized for movement, protection, communication, and coordination.

---

# 1. Tissue

A **tissue** is a group of similar cells having a common origin that work together to perform a specific function.

### Advantages of Tissues

- Division of labour.
- Increased efficiency.
- Better coordination.
- Growth and repair.
- Specialized functions.

---

# 2. Plant Tissues

Plant tissues are broadly classified into:

- **Meristematic Tissue**
- **Permanent Tissue**

---

# 3. Meristematic Tissue

**Meristematic tissue** consists of actively dividing cells responsible for plant growth.

### Characteristics

- Cells divide continuously.
- Thin cell walls.
- Dense cytoplasm.
- Prominent nucleus.
- Vacuoles absent or very small.
- Cells are compactly arranged.

### Types of Meristematic Tissue

---

## Apical Meristem

Located at the tips of roots and shoots.

### Function

- Increases the length of the plant.
- Responsible for primary growth.

---

## Intercalary Meristem

Located at the base of leaves or internodes.

### Function

- Helps in the growth of grasses.
- Regenerates damaged parts.

---

## Lateral Meristem

Located along the sides of stems and roots.

### Function

- Increases the girth (thickness) of the plant.
- Responsible for secondary growth.

---

# 4. Permanent Tissue

Permanent tissues are formed from meristematic tissues after cell division stops.

### Characteristics

- Cells lose the ability to divide.
- Cells become specialized.
- Perform specific functions.

Permanent tissues are classified into:

- Simple Permanent Tissue
- Complex Permanent Tissue

---

# 5. Simple Permanent Tissue

Simple permanent tissues are made up of only one type of cells.

They include:

- Parenchyma
- Collenchyma
- Sclerenchyma

---

## Parenchyma

Parenchyma consists of living, thin-walled cells.

### Functions

- Storage of food and water.
- Photosynthesis (when chloroplasts are present).
- Healing and repair.
- Provides support through turgidity.

### Special Types

#### Chlorenchyma

Contains chlorophyll and performs photosynthesis.

#### Aerenchyma

Contains large air spaces and provides buoyancy in aquatic plants.

---

## Collenchyma

Collenchyma consists of living cells with unevenly thickened corners.

### Functions

- Provides flexibility.
- Gives mechanical support.
- Allows bending without breaking.

Found in:

- Leaf stalks
- Young stems

---

## Sclerenchyma

Sclerenchyma consists of dead cells with thick, lignified walls.

### Functions

- Provides strength.
- Gives rigidity.
- Protects plant parts.

Examples:

- Coconut husk.
- Hard seed coats.

---

# 6. Complex Permanent Tissue

Complex tissues consist of more than one type of cell working together.

They include:

- Xylem
- Phloem

---

## Xylem

Xylem transports water and minerals from roots to other parts of the plant.

### Components

- Tracheids
- Vessels
- Xylem fibres
- Xylem parenchyma

### Functions

- Conducts water.
- Provides mechanical support.

---

## Phloem

Phloem transports prepared food from leaves to all parts of the plant.

### Components

- Sieve tubes
- Companion cells
- Phloem parenchyma
- Phloem fibres

### Functions

- Conducts food.
- Stores food.

Unlike xylem, phloem transport can occur in both upward and downward directions.

---

# 7. Difference Between Xylem and Phloem

| Xylem                              | Phloem                             |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Transports water and minerals      | Transports food                    |
| Mostly dead cells                  | Mostly living cells                |
| Movement is mainly upward          | Movement occurs in both directions |
| Provides strong mechanical support | Provides limited support           |

---

# 8. Animal Tissues

Animal tissues are classified into:

- Epithelial Tissue
- Connective Tissue
- Muscular Tissue
- Nervous Tissue

---

# 9. Epithelial Tissue

Epithelial tissue forms the outer covering of the body and lines internal organs.

### Functions

- Protection.
- Absorption.
- Secretion.
- Excretion.
- Exchange of materials.

### Types

---

## Squamous Epithelium

Thin and flat cells.

### Function

Allows rapid diffusion.

Examples:

- Air sacs of lungs.
- Blood capillaries.

---

## Cuboidal Epithelium

Cube-shaped cells.

### Function

- Secretion.
- Absorption.

Found in:

- Kidney tubules.
- Glands.

---

## Columnar Epithelium

Tall, pillar-like cells.

### Function

- Absorption.
- Secretion.

Found in:

- Stomach.
- Intestine.

---

## Ciliated Epithelium

Columnar cells with hair-like cilia.

### Function

Moves mucus or other substances.

Found in:

- Respiratory tract.
- Oviduct.

---

## Glandular Epithelium

Modified epithelial tissue specialized for secretion.

Examples:

- Sweat glands.
- Salivary glands.

---

# 10. Connective Tissue

Connective tissue connects, supports, and binds different body parts.

### Functions

- Support.
- Protection.
- Transport.
- Storage.

### Types

---

## Areolar Tissue

Found beneath the skin.

### Function

- Connects skin to muscles.
- Fills spaces between organs.

---

## Adipose Tissue

Stores fat.

### Functions

- Energy storage.
- Heat insulation.
- Protection of organs.

---

## Tendons

Connect muscles to bones.

Characteristics:

- Strong.
- Less elastic.

---

## Ligaments

Connect bones to bones.

Characteristics:

- Strong.
- Elastic.

## Cartilage

Flexible connective tissue.

Found in:

- Nose.

## Bone

Hard connective tissue rich in calcium salts.

### Functions

- Provides support.
- Protects organs.
- Helps in movement.

## Blood

Blood is a fluid connective tissue.

### Components

- Plasma

### Functions

- Transport of oxygen and nutrients.
- Protection against diseases.
- Blood clotting.

# 11. Muscular Tissue

Muscular tissue enables movement by contraction and relaxation.

### Types

---

# 12. Nervous Tissue

Nervous tissue consists of specialized cells called **neurons**.

### Structure of a Neuron

A neuron has:

- Cell body
- Dendrites

# Introduction

Motion is one of the most common phenomena observed in our daily lives. A car moving on a road, a bird flying in the sky, a falling stone, and the rotation of the Earth are all examples of motion.

To describe motion scientifically, we use quantities such as **distance, displacement, speed, velocity,** and **acceleration**. Motion can also be represented graphically using **distance-time** and **velocity-time graphs**. For objects moving with uniform acceleration, the relationship between velocity, acceleration, time, and displacement is described by the **equations of motion**.

---

# 1. Motion

An object is said to be **in motion** if its position changes with time with respect to a reference point.

An object whose position does not change with time is said to be **at rest**.

### Examples of Motion

- A moving car.
- A flying bird.
- A rotating fan.
- A train moving on a track.
- The Earth revolving around the Sun.

---

# 2. Types of Motion

### Rectilinear Motion

Motion along a straight line.

**Example:** A train moving on a straight track.

---

### Circular Motion

Motion along a circular path.

**Example:** Hands of a clock.

---

### Rotational Motion

Motion about a fixed axis.

**Example:** Ceiling fan.

---

### Periodic Motion

Motion that repeats itself at regular intervals of time.

**Example:** Pendulum of a clock.

---

# 3. Distance

**Distance** is the total length of the actual path travelled by an object.

### Characteristics

- Scalar quantity.
- Always positive.
- Depends on the path followed.

### SI Unit

```
metre (m)
```

---

# 4. Displacement

**Displacement** is the shortest distance between the initial and final positions of an object in a particular direction.

### Characteristics

- Vector quantity.
- Has both magnitude and direction.
- Can be zero even when distance is not zero.

### SI Unit

```
metre (m)
```

---

# 5. Difference Between Distance and Displacement

| Distance             | Displacement                                |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Total path travelled | Shortest straight-line distance             |
| Scalar quantity      | Vector quantity                             |
| Depends on the path  | Depends only on initial and final positions |
| Always positive      | Can be positive, negative, or zero          |

---

# 6. Speed

**Speed** is the distance travelled per unit time.

### Formula

```
Speed = Distance / Time
```

### SI Unit

```
m/s
```

Other unit:

```
km/h
```

### Conversion

```
1 m/s = 3.6 km/h
```

```
1 km/h = 5/18 m/s
```

---

# 7. Types of Speed

### Uniform Speed

An object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time.

---

### Non-uniform Speed

An object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.

---

### Average Speed

```
Average Speed
=
Total Distance Travelled
------------------------
Total Time Taken
```

---

# 8. Velocity

**Velocity** is the displacement per unit time in a specified direction.

### Formula

```
Velocity = Displacement / Time
```

### SI Unit

```
m/s
```

Velocity is a **vector quantity** because it has both magnitude and direction.

---

# 9. Difference Between Speed and Velocity

| Speed               | Velocity                           |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Scalar quantity     | Vector quantity                    |
| Depends on distance | Depends on displacement            |
| No direction        | Has direction                      |
| Always positive     | Can be positive, negative, or zero |

# 10. Acceleration

**Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity with time.

### Formula

```
Acceleration
=
(Final Velocity − Initial Velocity)
-----------------------------------
Time
```

or

```
a = (v − u) / t
```

where:

- **u** = Initial velocity
- **v** = Final velocity
- **t** = Time

### SI Unit

```
m/s²
```

---

# 11. Uniform Acceleration

If the velocity changes by equal amounts in equal intervals of time, the motion is said to have **uniform acceleration**.

---

# 12. Retardation (Negative Acceleration)

When the velocity of an object decreases with time, it is said to have **retardation** or **negative acceleration**.

Example:

A car slowing down before a traffic signal.

---

# 13. Distance-Time Graph

A **distance-time graph** shows how distance changes with time.

### Characteristics

A **velocity-time graph** shows how velocity changes with time.

### Characteristics

- Time on X-axis.
- Velocity on Y-axis.

### Interpretation

- Horizontal line → Uniform velocity.
- Straight sloping line → Uniform acceleration.
- Downward slope → Retardation.

### Important Facts

- **Slope** of the graph gives **acceleration**.
- **Area under the graph** gives **displacement**.

When an object moves along a circular path with constant speed, its motion is called **uniform circular motion**.

- Stone tied to a string and rotated.

- The slope of a velocity-time graph gives **acceleration**, and the area under it gives **displacement**.
- The **three equations of motion** are applicable only for objects moving with **uniform acceleration**.
- In **uniform circular motion**, the speed remains constant but the velocity changes continuously due to the changing direction of motion.

# Introduction

Objects around us are either at rest or in motion. Their state can change when a **force** acts on them. Force can make an object move, stop, change its speed, or change its direction of motion.

The behavior of moving objects was explained by **Sir Isaac Newton** through his **three laws of motion**, which form the foundation of classical mechanics. This chapter also introduces the concepts of **momentum** and the **Law of Conservation of Momentum**.

---

# 1. Force

A **force** is a push or a pull acting on an object.

### Effects of Force

- Changes the state of rest or motion.
- Changes the speed of an object.
- Changes the direction of motion.
- Changes the shape or size of an object.

### SI Unit

```
newton (N)
```

---

# 2. Types of Force

## Contact Forces

These forces act only when objects are in physical contact.

### Examples

- Muscular force
- Frictional force

---

## Non-contact Forces

These forces act without physical contact.

### Examples

- Gravitational force
- Magnetic force
- Electrostatic force

---

# 3. Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

## Balanced Forces

Balanced forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

### Characteristics

- Resultant force is zero.
- Do not change the state of motion.
- May change the shape of an object.

### Example

Two people pulling a rope with equal force in opposite directions.

---

## Unbalanced Forces

Unbalanced forces do not cancel each other.

### Characteristics

- Resultant force is not zero.
- Cause a change in motion.
- Can change speed or direction.

### Example

Kicking a stationary football.

---

# 4. Newton's First Law of Motion

### Statement

> **An object remains at rest or continues to move with uniform velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by an unbalanced external force.**

This law is also called the **Law of Inertia**.

---

# 5. Inertia

**Inertia** is the property of an object by which it resists any change in its state of rest or motion.

The greater the mass of an object, the greater is its inertia.

### Types of Inertia

#### Inertia of Rest

A stationary object tends to remain at rest.

**Example:**

Passengers fall backward when a bus suddenly starts.

---

#### Inertia of Motion

A moving object tends to continue moving.

**Example:**

Passengers fall forward when a moving bus stops suddenly.

---

#### Inertia of Direction

An object resists changes in its direction of motion.

**Example:**

Passengers are pushed sideways when a vehicle takes a sharp turn.

---

# 6. Momentum

**Momentum** is the quantity of motion possessed by an object.

It depends on both the mass and velocity of the object.

### Formula

```
Momentum (p) = Mass × Velocity
```

```
p = mv
```

### SI Unit

```
kg m/s
```

---

# 7. Newton's Second Law of Motion

### Statement

> **The rate of change of momentum of an object is directly proportional to the applied force and takes place in the direction of the force.**

From this law, the mathematical relation is obtained:

```
F = ma
```

where:

- **F** = Force
- **m** = Mass
- **a** = Acceleration

### SI Unit of Force

```
1 Newton
```

One newton is the force required to produce an acceleration of **1 m/s²** in a body of mass **1 kg**.

---

# 8. Newton's Third Law of Motion

### Statement

> **For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.**

### Characteristics

- Action and reaction forces are equal in magnitude.
- They act in opposite directions.
- They act on different objects.
- They occur simultaneously.

### Examples

- Recoil of a gun.
- Rocket propulsion.
- Walking on the ground.
- Swimming.
- Rowing a boat.

---

# 9. Law of Conservation of Momentum

### Statement

> **When no external force acts on a system, the total momentum before collision is equal to the total momentum after collision.**

### Formula

```
Initial Momentum = Final Momentum
```

For two objects,

```
m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂
```

where:

- **m₁, m₂** = Masses of the objects
- **u₁, u₂** = Initial velocities
- **v₁, v₂** = Final velocities

This law is applicable during collisions and explosions when external forces are negligible.

---

# 10. Applications of Newton's Laws

### Newton's First Law

- Seat belts in cars.
- Dust removed by beating carpets.
- Shaking branches to remove fruits.

---

### Newton's Second Law

- Kicking a football.
- Pushing a loaded cart.
- Catching a ball by moving hands backward.

---

### Newton's Third Law

- Flying of birds.
- Rocket launch.
- Walking.
- Swimming.

- A **force** is a push or pull that can change the state of motion or shape of an object.

# Introduction

Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force called **gravitational force**. This force keeps the planets revolving around the Sun, the Moon revolving around the Earth, and causes objects to fall towards the Earth.

The study of gravitation explains the **Universal Law of Gravitation**, **acceleration due to gravity**, **mass and weight**, **free fall**, **thrust**, **pressure**, **buoyancy**, and **Archimedes' Principle**.

---

# 1. Gravitation

**Gravitation** is the force of attraction between any two objects having mass.

### Examples

- Fruits falling from trees.
- Planets revolving around the Sun.
- Moon revolving around the Earth.
- Ocean tides caused by the Moon.

---

# 2. Universal Law of Gravitation

Sir **Isaac Newton** proposed the **Universal Law of Gravitation**.

### Statement

> **Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres.**

### Formula

```
        Gm₁m₂
F = ------------
         r²
```

where:

- **F** = Gravitational force
- **G** = Universal gravitational constant
- **m₁, m₂** = Masses of the two objects
- **r** = Distance between their centres

### SI Unit of Force

```
newton (N)
```

---

# 3. Universal Gravitational Constant (G)

The **universal gravitational constant** is represented by **G**.

### Value

```
G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg²
```

### Characteristics

- Constant throughout the universe.
- Independent of the nature of objects.

---

# 4. Importance of Gravitation

Gravitation is responsible for:

- Falling of objects towards the Earth.
- Keeping planets in their orbits.
- Keeping satellites in orbit.
- Ocean tides.
- Formation of stars and galaxies.

---

# 5. Free Fall

When an object falls towards the Earth under the influence of gravity alone, it is said to be in **free fall**.

In free fall:

- Air resistance is neglected.
- Acceleration remains constant.

### Symbol

### With Altitude

As altitude increases,

```

```

---

### With Depth

As depth below the Earth's surface increases,

```
g decreases.
```

---

### At the Equator and Poles

- Maximum at the poles.
- Minimum at the equator.

This is due to the Earth's rotation and its slightly flattened shape.

- Assuming mass changes from place to place.
- Ignoring the direction of gravity in upward motion problems.
- Mixing up **density** and **relative density**.

# Chapter Summary

- **Gravitation** is the force of attraction between any two masses.
- Newton's **Universal Law of Gravitation** explains the gravitational attraction between objects.
- The **universal gravitational constant (G)** has a fixed value throughout the universe.
- A freely falling object experiences a constant **acceleration due to gravity (g)**, approximately **9.8 m/s²** near the Earth's surface.
- The value of **g** decreases with increasing altitude and depth and is slightly greater at the poles than at the equator.
- **Mass** is the quantity of matter in an object, while **weight** is the gravitational force acting on it.
- **Thrust** is the force acting perpendicular to a surface, and **pressure** is thrust per unit area.
- **Buoyancy** is the upward force exerted by a fluid on an immersed object.
- **Archimedes' Principle** states that the buoyant force on an object is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by it.
- Gravitation explains many natural phenomena, including planetary motion, tides, free fall, floating of objects, and satellite motion.

# Introduction

In everyday language, any physical or mental effort is called work. However, in science, **work** has a specific meaning. Work is said to be done only when a force acting on an object causes it to move in the direction of the force.

The ability to do work is called **energy**. Energy exists in many forms, such as kinetic energy, potential energy, heat energy, electrical energy, and light energy. This chapter explains the concepts of work, energy, power, the **Law of Conservation of Energy**, and commercial units of energy.

---

# 1. Work

**Work** is said to be done when a force acting on an object produces displacement in the direction of the force.

### Conditions for Work to be Done

- A force must act on the object.
- The object must undergo displacement.
- The displacement should have a component in the direction of the applied force.

### Formula

```
Work = Force × Displacement
```

```
W = F × s
```

where:

- **W** = Work
- **F** = Force
- **s** = Displacement

### SI Unit

```
joule (J)
```

One joule is the work done when a force of **1 N** moves an object through **1 m** in the direction of the force.

---

# 2. Positive, Negative, and Zero Work

## Positive Work

When the force and displacement are in the same direction.

### Examples

- Pulling a cart.
- A falling stone.

---

## Negative Work

When the force acts opposite to the direction of displacement.

### Examples

- Friction acting on a moving object.
- Applying brakes to a bicycle.

---

## Zero Work

Work is zero when:

- There is no displacement.
- The force is perpendicular to the displacement.

### Examples

- Pushing a wall that does not move.
- Carrying a load on a horizontal road at constant height.

---

# 3. Energy

**Energy** is the capacity of an object to do work.

### SI Unit

```
joule (J)
```

### Forms of Energy

- Mechanical energy
- Heat energy
- Light energy
- Sound energy
- Electrical energy
- Chemical energy
- Nuclear energy

Mechanical energy consists of:

- Kinetic energy
- Potential energy

---

# 4. Kinetic Energy

**Kinetic energy** is the energy possessed by an object due to its motion.

### Formula

```
KE = ½mv²
```

where:

- **m** = Mass
- **v** = Velocity

### SI Unit

```
joule (J)
```

### Examples

- Moving car.
- Running athlete.
- Rolling ball.

---

# 5. Potential Energy

**Potential energy** is the energy possessed by an object due to its position or configuration.

### Gravitational Potential Energy

The potential energy of an object raised above the ground is given by:

```
PE = mgh
```

where:

- **m** = Mass
- **g** = Acceleration due to gravity
- **h** = Height above the ground

### SI Unit

```
joule (J)
```

### Examples

- Water stored in a dam.
- A stretched rubber band.
- A book placed on a shelf.

---

# 6. Mechanical Energy

**Mechanical energy** is the sum of kinetic energy and potential energy.

### Formula

```
Mechanical Energy = KE + PE
```

---

# 7. Law of Conservation of Energy

### Statement

> **Energy can neither be created nor destroyed. It can only be transformed from one form to another.**

Thus, the total energy of an isolated system always remains constant.

### Example

A freely falling object:

- At the highest point, potential energy is maximum and kinetic energy is zero.
- As it falls, potential energy decreases while kinetic energy increases.
- Just before reaching the ground, kinetic energy becomes maximum and potential energy becomes minimum.

The total mechanical energy remains constant (neglecting air resistance).

---

# 8. Power

**Power** is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

### Formula

```
Power = Work / Time
```

```
P = W / t
```

where:

- **P** = Power
- **W** = Work done
- **t** = Time taken

### SI Unit

```
watt (W)
```

One watt is the power when **1 joule** of work is done in **1 second**.

---

## Larger Units of Power

```
1 kilowatt (kW)
= 1000 W
```

```
1 megawatt (MW)
= 1000 kW
```

---

# 9. Commercial Unit of Energy

Electrical energy consumed in homes is measured in **kilowatt-hour (kWh)**.

### Definition

One kilowatt-hour is the energy consumed by a **1000 W** appliance operating for **1 hour**.

### Conversion

```
1 kWh
= 3.6 × 10⁶ J
```

This unit is commonly referred to as **one unit of electricity**.

---

# 10. Difference Between Work, Energy, and Power

| Work                              | Energy                             | Power                    |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Product of force and displacement | Capacity to do work                | Rate of doing work       |
| SI Unit: Joule (J)                | SI Unit: Joule (J)                 | SI Unit: Watt (W)        |
| Depends on force and displacement | Depends on the state of the object | Depends on work and time |

# Chapter 11: Sound

# Introduction

Sound is a form of energy that produces the sensation of hearing. It is produced by **vibrating objects** and travels through a **material medium** such as solids, liquids, or gases. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum because it requires particles to transmit vibrations.

This chapter explains how sound is produced and propagated, the characteristics of sound waves, reflection of sound, echoes, reverberation, the range of hearing, and the working of SONAR.

---

# 1. Sound

**Sound** is a form of energy produced by vibrating objects.

### Examples

- Ringing of a bell.
- Vibrating guitar strings.
- Human voice.
- Beating of a drum.

---

# 2. Production of Sound

Sound is produced when an object **vibrates**.

The vibrating object transfers energy to the surrounding particles, causing them to vibrate and produce sound waves.

### Examples

- Tuning fork.
- Guitar string.
- Drum membrane.
- Vocal cords.

---

# 3. Propagation of Sound

Sound travels through a medium in the form of **longitudinal waves**.

In these waves, particles of the medium vibrate **parallel** to the direction of wave propagation.

Sound requires a **material medium** and cannot travel through a vacuum.

### Speed of Sound

The speed of sound depends on the nature of the medium.

- Fastest in solids.
- Slower in liquids.
- Slowest in gases.

At room temperature, the speed of sound in air is approximately:

```
343 m/s
```

---

# 4. Longitudinal Waves

A **longitudinal wave** is a wave in which particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction of wave propagation.

### Components

#### Compression

A region where particles are close together.

- High pressure.
- High density.

---

#### Rarefaction

A region where particles are far apart.

- Low pressure.
- Low density.

---

# 5. Characteristics of a Sound Wave

---

## Amplitude

**Amplitude** is the maximum displacement of particles from their mean position.

### Effect

Greater amplitude produces a **louder sound**.

### SI Unit

```
metre (m)
```

---

## Time Period

The **time period** is the time taken to complete one vibration.

### Symbol

```
T
```

### SI Unit

```
second (s)
```

---

## Frequency

**Frequency** is the number of vibrations produced in one second.

### Formula

```
Frequency = 1 / Time Period
```

```
f = 1/T
```

### SI Unit

```
hertz (Hz)
```

### Effect

Higher frequency produces a **higher pitch**.

---

## Wavelength

**Wavelength** is the distance between two consecutive compressions or two consecutive rarefactions.

### Symbol

```
λ
```

### SI Unit

```
metre (m)
```

---

## Wave Speed

The speed of a sound wave is given by:

```
Speed = Frequency × Wavelength
```

```
v = fλ
```

### SI Unit

```
m/s
```

---

# 6. Loudness and Pitch

## Loudness

Loudness depends on the **amplitude** of the sound wave.

Greater amplitude → Greater loudness.

It is measured in **decibel (dB)**.

---

## Pitch

Pitch depends on the **frequency** of the sound wave.

- Higher frequency → Higher pitch.
- Lower frequency → Lower pitch.

Example:

A child's voice generally has a higher pitch than an adult's voice.

---

# 7. Reflection of Sound

Sound waves obey the laws of reflection just like light waves.

### Laws of Reflection

- The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.
- The incident wave, reflected wave, and the normal lie in the same plane.

### Applications

- Megaphones.
- Hearing aids.
- Stethoscopes.
- Soundboards in auditoriums.

---

# 8. Echo

An **echo** is the repetition of a sound due to its reflection from a distant surface.

For an echo to be heard separately, the reflected sound should reach the listener after at least:

```
0.1 second
```

At room temperature, the reflecting surface should be at least about:

```
17.2 metres
```

away from the listener.

### Applications

- Measuring distance.
- Detecting obstacles.
- SONAR.

---

# 9. Reverberation

**Reverberation** is the persistence of sound in an enclosed space due to multiple reflections.

### Effects

- Makes speech unclear.
- Reduces sound quality.

### Methods to Reduce Reverberation

- Curtains.
- Carpets.
- Cushioned seats.
- Acoustic panels.
- False ceilings.

---

# 10. Range of Hearing

The human ear can hear sounds with frequencies between:

```
20 Hz and 20,000 Hz
```

---

## Infrasonic Sound

Frequency below:

```
20 Hz
```

### Examples

- Earthquakes.
- Elephants.
- Whales.

---

## Audible Sound

Frequency between:

```
20 Hz and 20,000 Hz
```

This is the normal hearing range for humans.

---

## Ultrasonic Sound

Frequency above:

```
20,000 Hz
```

### Uses

- Medical imaging (Ultrasonography).
- Cleaning delicate instruments.
- Detecting cracks in metals.
- SONAR.

Animals such as **bats** and **dolphins** use ultrasonic sounds for navigation.

---

# 11. SONAR

**SONAR** stands for:

```
Sound Navigation And Ranging
```

It uses ultrasonic waves to detect underwater objects and measure distances.

### Working Principle

- Ultrasonic waves are transmitted into water.
- They are reflected from underwater objects.
- The reflected waves (echoes) are received.
- The time taken is used to calculate the distance.

### Formula

```
Distance
=
Speed × Time
--------------
2
```

Division by **2** is necessary because the sound travels to the object and back.

### Applications

- Measuring sea depth.
- Detecting submarines.
- Locating underwater rocks.
- Finding schools of fish.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Sound** is a form of energy produced by vibrating objects.
- Sound travels through solids, liquids, and gases as **longitudinal waves** but cannot travel through a vacuum.
- Longitudinal waves consist of **compressions** and **rarefactions**.
- The main characteristics of sound are **amplitude, frequency, time period, wavelength,** and **wave speed**.

- The normal hearing range of humans is **20 Hz to 20,000 Hz**.
- **Ultrasonic waves** have frequencies above **20,000 Hz** and are widely used in medicine, industry, and navigation.
- **SONAR** uses ultrasonic waves and the principle of echo to locate underwater objects and measure distances.
- Understanding sound helps explain communication, music, medical imaging, underwater exploration, and many everyday phenomena.

- **Improvement in food resources** aims to increase food production and quality using scientific farming methods.
- **Crop variety improvement** develops high-yielding, disease-resistant, and climate-tolerant crop varieties.
- Proper **nutrient management** involves the balanced use of **manure** and **fertilizers**.
- Efficient **irrigation systems**, such as **sprinkler** and **drip irrigation**, help conserve water and improve crop growth.
- **Cropping patterns** like mixed cropping, intercropping, and crop rotation increase productivity and maintain soil fertility.
- **Crop protection management** reduces losses caused by weeds, pests, and diseases.
- Scientific **storage methods** protect harvested grains from moisture, insects, fungi, and rodents.
- **Animal husbandry** includes cattle farming, poultry farming, fish production, and bee-keeping to improve the production of milk, meat, eggs, fish, honey, and other useful products.
- Sustainable agricultural practices ensure food security while protecting natural resources and the environment.
