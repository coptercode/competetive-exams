# Introduction

Physics is an experimental science. Every physical quantity must be measured using a **standard unit**. Accurate measurements and proper representation of measured values are essential for obtaining reliable experimental results.

This chapter provides the foundation for all numerical calculations in physics by introducing **units, dimensions, measurement errors, and significant figures**.

---

# 1. Physical Quantities

## Definition

A **physical quantity** is a quantity that can be measured and expressed as a numerical value along with an appropriate unit.

Examples

- Length
- Mass
- Time
- Force
- Energy
- Velocity

---

# 2. Measurement

Measurement is the process of comparing an unknown physical quantity with a standard quantity of the same kind.

Example

```
Length of a rod = 2 m
```

where

- 2 = Numerical value
- m = Unit

---

# 3. Units

## Definition

A **unit** is a fixed standard used for measuring a physical quantity.

Example

```
Length → metre (m)

Mass → kilogram (kg)

Time → second (s)
```

---

# 4. Characteristics of a Good Unit

A good unit should be:

- Well defined.
- Easily reproducible.
- Invariable with time and place.
- Easily accessible.
- Of convenient magnitude.
- Internationally accepted.

---

# 12. Principle of Dimensional Homogeneity

A valid physical equation must have the **same dimensions on both sides**.

Example

```
s = ut + ½at²
```

Dimensions of each term:

```
[L]

=

[L]

+

[L]
```

Hence, the equation is dimensionally correct.

```
[L T⁻¹]
```

# 14. Limitations of Dimensional Analysis

- Cannot determine numerical constants (2, π, etc.).
- Cannot derive trigonometric, logarithmic, or exponential relations.
- Cannot distinguish between quantities having the same dimensions.

Example

```

and

↓
```

# 15. Accuracy and Precision

## Accuracy

Closeness of a measured value to the true value.

---

## Precision

Closeness of repeated measurements to one another.

---

## Difference

| Accuracy                    | Precision               |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Near true value             | Reproducible values     |

# 16. Errors in Measurement

## Definition

The difference between the measured value and the true value is called **error**.

---

## Types of Errors

### (A) Systematic Errors

Occur due to definite causes.

Examples

- Instrumental errors.
- Personal errors.
- Environmental errors.
- Imperfect experimental techniques.

---

### (B) Random Errors

Occur due to unpredictable variations.

Examples

- Observer variation.
- Fluctuations in conditions.

---

### (C) Gross Errors

Occur due to human mistakes.

Examples

- Wrong reading.
- Incorrect calculation.
- Recording errors.

# 20. Significant Figures

## Definition

The meaningful digits in a measured quantity, including the first uncertain digit.

---

## Rules for Counting Significant Figures

### Rule 1

All non-zero digits are significant.

Example

```
248

↓

3 Significant Figures
```

---

### Rule 2

Zeros between non-zero digits are significant.

Example

```
2007

↓

4 Significant Figures
```

---

### Rule 3

Leading zeros are not significant.

Example

```
0.0052

↓

2 Significant Figures
```

---

### Rule 4

Trailing zeros after the decimal point are significant.

Example

```
2.500

↓

4 Significant Figures
```

---

### Rule 5

Trailing zeros in a whole number without a decimal point may not always be significant.

Example

```
2500
```

The number of significant figures depends on the context or notation used.

---

# 21. Rounding Off Rules

- If the digit to be dropped is less than 5, leave the previous digit unchanged.
- If greater than 5, increase the previous digit by 1.
- If exactly 5 followed by non-zero digits, increase the previous digit by 1.
- If exactly 5 followed only by zeros or nothing:
  - Increase the previous digit if it is odd.
  - Leave it unchanged if it is even.

---

# 22. Rules for Arithmetic Operations

## Addition and Subtraction

The result should have the same number of decimal places as the quantity with the fewest decimal places.

---

## Multiplication and Division

The result should have the same number of significant figures as the quantity with the least number of significant figures.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **units** with **dimensions**.
- Assuming two quantities with the same dimensions represent the same physical quantity (e.g., **work** and **torque** have the same dimensions but different meanings).
- Ignoring **systematic errors** while analyzing experimental results.
- Counting leading zeros as significant figures.
- Forgetting the correct rounding-off rules, especially when the dropped digit is exactly **5**.
- Using dimensional analysis to derive equations containing numerical constants like **π** or trigonometric functions, which is not possible.
- Mixing **accuracy** with **precision**.
- Expressing calculated answers with more significant figures than justified by the measurements.

---

# Chapter Summary

- A **physical quantity** is expressed as a numerical value along with a standard **unit**.
- The **SI system** is the internationally accepted system of units and consists of **seven base quantities** and their corresponding units.
- Physical quantities are classified as **fundamental** or **derived**, and their dependence on base quantities is represented by **dimensional formulas**.
- **Dimensional analysis** is useful for checking equations, deriving relations, and converting units, although it has important limitations.
- Experimental measurements are affected by **systematic**, **random**, and **gross errors**, which influence the reliability of results.
- **Absolute**, **relative**, and **percentage errors** quantify measurement uncertainty.
- **Accuracy** indicates closeness to the true value, whereas **precision** indicates the consistency of repeated measurements.
- **Significant figures** and **rounding-off rules** ensure that calculated results reflect the actual precision of measurements.
- **Scientific notation** provides a convenient way to represent very large and very small numbers.
- These concepts form the basis of all experimental measurements and calculations throughout physics.

# Introduction

Motion is one of the most fundamental concepts in physics. A body is said to be in motion if its position changes with time relative to a chosen reference point.

When an object moves along a **straight line**, its motion is called **one-dimensional motion** or **motion in a straight line**. Such motion can be completely described using position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration.

---

# 1. Frame of Reference

## Definition

A **frame of reference** is a coordinate system with respect to which the position and motion of an object are described.

Example

- A person standing on the ground observes a moving train.
- A passenger inside the train observes another passenger at rest.

---

## Types of Frames

### Inertial Frame

A frame in which Newton's laws of motion are valid.

Example

- Earth (approximately)
- A stationary laboratory

---

### Non-Inertial Frame

A frame accelerating with respect to an inertial frame.

Example

- Accelerating car
- Rotating merry-go-round

---

# 2. Position

The position of a particle is specified by its distance from the origin along a chosen axis.

# 3. Distance

## Definition

Distance is the **total length of the actual path travelled** by a body.

### Characteristics

- Scalar quantity.
- Always positive.

Unit

# 4. Displacement

## Definition

Displacement is the **shortest distance** between the initial and final positions of a body with a specified direction.

Formula

- Vector quantity.
- Can be positive, negative, or zero.
- Independent of the actual path.

# 10. Equations of Uniformly Accelerated Motion

Applicable only when acceleration is constant.

---

## First Equation

```
v = u + at
```

where

- u = Initial velocity
- v = Final velocity
- a = Acceleration
- t = Time

---

### Derivation

From the definition of acceleration,

```
a = (v − u)/t
```

Rearranging,

```
v = u + at
```

---

## Second Equation

```
s = ut + ½at²
```

where

- s = Displacement

---

### Derivation

Average velocity

```
= (u + v)/2
```

Substituting

```
v = u + at
```

into

```
s = Average Velocity × Time
```

gives

```
s = ut + ½at²
```

---

## Third Equation

```
v² = u² + 2as
```

---

### Derivation

Eliminating time from the first two equations,

```
v² = u² + 2as
```

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **distance** with **displacement**.
- Assuming **speed** and **velocity** are always numerically equal; they differ whenever the direction changes or displacement differs from distance.
- Applying the equations of motion when **acceleration is not constant**.
- Forgetting that the **slope of an x–t graph gives velocity**, while the **slope of a v–t graph gives acceleration**.
- Mistaking the **area under a v–t graph** as velocity; it actually represents **displacement**.
- Using **g = +9.8 m s⁻²** or **−9.8 m s⁻²** without first choosing a sign convention.
- Ignoring the direction while calculating **relative velocity**.
- Mixing SI units (m, s) with non-SI units (km, h) without proper conversion.

# Chapter Summary

- Motion in a straight line is described with respect to a **frame of reference** using quantities such as **position**, **displacement**, **velocity**, and **acceleration**.
- **Distance** is a scalar quantity representing the actual path travelled, whereas **displacement** is a vector representing the shortest path between two positions.
- **Speed** is based on distance travelled, while **velocity** is based on displacement and includes direction.
- **Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity and may be **positive**, **negative (retardation)**, **uniform**, or **non-uniform**.
- The three equations of uniformly accelerated motion—**v = u + at**, **s = ut + ½at²**, and **v² = u² + 2as**—are applicable only for **constant acceleration**.
- Motion can be effectively analyzed using **position-time**, **velocity-time**, and **acceleration-time** graphs, where slopes and areas provide important physical information.
- For freely falling bodies, acceleration is replaced by **g**, the acceleration due to gravity.
- **Relative velocity** describes the motion of one object with respect to another and depends on whether the objects move in the same or opposite directions.
- The concepts developed in this chapter form the basis for studying motion in two dimensions, Newton's laws of motion, and other advanced topics in mechanics.

# Common Mistakes

- Treating **vectors** as ordinary algebraic quantities without considering direction.
- Confusing the **dot product** (scalar result) with the **cross product** (vector result).
- Interchanging **sinθ** and **cosθ** while resolving vectors into components.
- Forgetting that the **trajectory of a projectile is parabolic**, not circular.
- Assuming the **horizontal velocity of a projectile changes**; it remains constant when air resistance is neglected.
- Using **θ** instead of **2θ** in the formula for horizontal range.
- Assuming that an object in uniform circular motion has **zero acceleration** because its speed is constant; its **velocity changes continuously**, so it has **centripetal acceleration**.
- Confusing **centripetal force** with a new type of force; it is the **net inward force** provided by existing forces such as tension, friction, or gravity.

# Common Mistakes

- Assuming work is done whenever a force acts; **displacement** must also occur.
- Forgetting the **cosθ** factor in the work formula.
- Confusing **kinetic energy** with **potential energy**.
- Applying the **conservation of mechanical energy** when non-conservative forces such as friction are present.
- Assuming **momentum** and **kinetic energy** are both conserved in every collision; only **momentum** is always conserved in isolated systems.
- Ignoring the sign of work done by **gravity** or **friction**.
- Using **P = W/t** for situations involving changing force without considering **instantaneous power (P = F·v)**.
- Forgetting that **work done equals the area under the force-displacement graph** for variable forces.

# Chapter Summary

- **Work** is done when a force causes displacement and is given by **W = Fs cosθ** for a constant force.
- **Energy** is the capacity to do work and exists in many forms, with **kinetic** and **potential energy** being the primary forms of mechanical energy.
- The **Work-Energy Theorem** states that the net work done on a body equals the change in its kinetic energy.
- **Gravitational potential energy** is given by **mgh**, while the energy stored in a stretched or compressed spring is **½kx²**.
- **Conservative forces** conserve mechanical energy and perform zero work over a closed path, whereas **non-conservative forces** depend on the path and dissipate mechanical energy.
- In the absence of non-conservative forces, the total **mechanical energy (KE + PE)** of a system remains constant.
- **Power** is the rate of doing work and is measured in **watts**, with instantaneous power given by **P = F·v**.
- During **collisions**, the total momentum of an isolated system is always conserved, while kinetic energy is conserved only in **elastic collisions**.
- The principles of work, energy, and power provide powerful tools for solving mechanical problems without directly applying Newton's laws of motion.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing the **centre of mass** with the geometric centre; they coincide only for bodies with **uniform mass distribution**.

- During **rolling motion**, both translational and rotational kinetic energies contribute to the total kinetic energy.
- The principles of rotational motion are essential for understanding the motion of machines, vehicles, satellites, planets, and many engineering systems.

# Introduction

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing the **gravitational constant (G)** with the **acceleration due to gravity (g)**.
- Assuming **g** is the same everywhere on Earth; it varies with **altitude**, **depth**, and **latitude**.
- Forgetting the **negative sign** in the general formula for gravitational potential energy.
- Using **mgh** for large distances from Earth; the expression **PE = -GMm/r** should be used instead.
- Mixing up **orbital velocity** and **escape velocity**.
- Assuming the **mass of the satellite** affects its orbital or escape velocity; both are **independent of the satellite's mass**.
- Confusing **geostationary satellites** with **polar satellites**.
- Forgetting that **Kepler's Third Law** relates the **square of the time period** to the **cube of the orbital radius**.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **stress** with **pressure**; stress is an internal restoring force per unit area, while pressure is an external force per unit area.
- Treating **strain** as having units; strain is a **dimensionless ratio**.
- Applying **Hooke's law** beyond the **elastic limit**, where it is no longer valid.
- Mixing up **Young's modulus**, **Bulk modulus**, and **Shear modulus**.
- Forgetting to convert **millimetres** to **metres** before calculations.
- Assuming all materials obey Hooke's law under all conditions; it is valid only within the proportional limit.

- The **stress-strain curve** provides important information about the elastic limit, yield point, ultimate tensile strength, and breaking point of a material.

- The principles of elasticity are widely applied in engineering structures such as bridges, buildings, cranes, railway tracks, springs, and measuring instruments.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **density** with **relative density**; relative density has **no unit**.
- Assuming pressure depends on the **shape of the container**; it depends only on **depth, density, and gravity**.
- Forgetting that **Pascal's law** applies only to **confined fluids**.
- Mixing up **buoyant force** with the **weight of the body**.
- Using **Bernoulli's theorem** for viscous or turbulent flow, where its ideal assumptions are not satisfied.
- Confusing **streamline flow** with **turbulent flow**.
- Assuming **viscosity** and **surface tension** are the same property; viscosity acts throughout the fluid, while surface tension acts only at the surface.
- Ignoring the angle of contact while solving **capillarity** problems.

# Chapter Summary

- **Fluids** are substances that can flow and include both liquids and gases.
- **Pressure** is the normal force per unit area and increases with the depth of a liquid.
- **Pascal's law** states that pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted equally in all directions, forming the basis of hydraulic machines.
- **Archimedes' principle** explains buoyancy and the floating of bodies by relating the buoyant force to the weight of the displaced fluid.
- The **continuity equation** expresses the conservation of mass during fluid flow.
- **Bernoulli's theorem** relates pressure, velocity, and height in the streamline flow of an ideal fluid and has numerous engineering applications.
- **Viscosity** is the resistance offered by a fluid to the relative motion of its layers, while **Stokes' law** describes the viscous drag on a sphere moving through a fluid.
- **Terminal velocity** is the constant maximum speed attained by an object falling through a viscous medium.
- **Surface tension** arises due to cohesive forces between liquid molecules and is responsible for phenomena such as droplets, capillarity, and wetting.
- The mechanical properties of fluids have wide applications in hydraulic systems, aviation, medicine, meteorology, transportation, and everyday life.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **heat** with **internal energy**; heat is energy in transit, while internal energy is a property of the system.
- Using the wrong **sign convention** for heat and work in the first law of thermodynamics.
- Assuming **ΔU = 0** for all processes; it is true only for an **ideal gas** undergoing an **isothermal process**.
- Forgetting that **work done is zero** in an **isochoric process** because the volume remains constant.
- Mixing up **isothermal** and **adiabatic** processes.
- Using temperatures in **°C** instead of **Kelvin** while calculating Carnot efficiency.
- Assuming a heat engine can have **100% efficiency**, which violates the second law of thermodynamics.
- Confusing **efficiency** of a heat engine with the **coefficient of performance (COP)** of a refrigerator.

# Chapter Summary

- **Thermodynamics** deals with the relationship between heat, work, and internal energy.
- A thermodynamic system may be **open**, **closed**, or **isolated**, depending on the exchange of matter and energy.
- The **Zeroth Law** defines thermal equilibrium and forms the basis of temperature measurement.
- The **First Law of Thermodynamics** is a statement of the conservation of energy: **Q = ΔU + W**.
- The four important thermodynamic processes are **isothermal**, **adiabatic**, **isobaric**, and **isochoric**, each having characteristic equations and properties.
- The **specific heat capacities** at constant pressure and constant volume are related by **Mayer's relation**, and their ratio is called the **adiabatic index**.
- The **Second Law of Thermodynamics** establishes the direction of natural processes and sets the upper limit on the efficiency of heat engines.
- **Heat engines**, **refrigerators**, and **heat pumps** operate by transferring heat between reservoirs while performing or consuming work.
- The **Carnot engine** is an ideal reversible engine with the maximum possible efficiency, depending only on the temperatures of the source and sink.
- Thermodynamic principles are fundamental to power generation, refrigeration, industrial processes, and many natural phenomena.

# Introduction

The **Kinetic Theory of Gases** explains the macroscopic properties of gases such as pressure, temperature, and volume in terms of the microscopic motion of their molecules.

According to this theory, gases consist of a large number of tiny molecules in continuous random motion. Their collisions with the walls of the container produce pressure, while their average kinetic energy determines the temperature.

---

# 1. Kinetic Theory of Gases

## Definition

The kinetic theory explains the behaviour of gases by considering them as collections of rapidly moving molecules.

---

## Objectives

- Explain gas pressure.
- Explain gas laws.
- Relate temperature to molecular motion.
- Calculate molecular speeds.
- Explain internal energy of gases.

---

# 2. Postulates of Kinetic Theory

1. A gas consists of a very large number of tiny molecules.
2. Molecules are in continuous random motion.
3. The volume occupied by molecules is negligible compared to the volume of the gas.
4. Molecules move in straight lines between collisions.
5. Collisions between molecules and with container walls are perfectly elastic.
6. No intermolecular force acts except during collisions.
7. The duration of a collision is negligible.
8. Gas molecules obey Newton's laws of motion.

---

- Assuming **temperature measures total kinetic energy**; it is proportional to the **average translational kinetic energy** of molecules.

# Introduction

Many motions in nature repeat themselves after regular intervals of time. Such motions are called **periodic motions**. Oscillatory motion is a special type of periodic motion in which a body moves repeatedly to and fro about its mean position.

Examples include the motion of a pendulum, a vibrating tuning fork, the oscillation of a spring, and the vibration of atoms in solids.

---

# 1. Periodic Motion

## Definition

A motion that repeats itself after equal intervals of time is called **periodic motion**.

---

## Examples

- Earth's revolution around the Sun
- Rotation of a fan
- Motion of clock hands
- Oscillation of a pendulum

---

# 2. Oscillatory Motion

## Definition

Oscillatory motion is the repeated to-and-fro motion of a body about its equilibrium (mean) position.

---

## Characteristics

- Repeats after equal intervals of time.
- Has a mean position.
- Restoring force acts towards the mean position.

---

# 3. Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)

## Definition

Simple Harmonic Motion is a special type of oscillatory motion in which the restoring force (or acceleration) is directly proportional to the displacement from the mean position and is always directed towards it.

Mathematically,

```
F ∝ -x
```

or

```
F = -kx
```

where

- **F** = Restoring force
- **k** = Force constant (spring constant)
- **x** = Displacement

---

## Acceleration in SHM

Using Newton's second law,

```
F = ma
```

Therefore,

```
a = -ω²x
```

where

- **ω** = Angular frequency

---

# 4. Characteristics of SHM

- Motion is periodic.
- Restoring force is proportional to displacement.
- Acceleration is maximum at the extreme positions.
- Velocity is maximum at the mean position.
- Total mechanical energy remains constant (in the absence of damping).

---

- Forgetting the negative sign in **F = -kx** and **a = -ω²x**, which indicates that the restoring force and acceleration are directed towards the mean position.
- Using the **simple pendulum formula** for large amplitudes; it is valid only for **small oscillations**.
- Assuming the time period of a simple pendulum depends on the **mass of the bob**; it depends only on **length** and **acceleration due to gravity**.
- Mixing up **frequency**, **time period**, and **angular frequency**.
- Assuming kinetic and potential energies remain constant individually; only the **total mechanical energy** remains constant in undamped SHM.
- Confusing **forced oscillations** with **resonance**; resonance occurs only when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency.
- Forgetting to use **SI units** in numerical calculations.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Oscillatory motion** is the repeated to-and-fro motion of a body about its equilibrium position.
- **Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)** is a special type of oscillation in which the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement and acts towards the mean position.
- The displacement, velocity, and acceleration of a particle in SHM vary sinusoidally with time.
- The important quantities describing SHM are **amplitude**, **time period**, **frequency**, **angular frequency**, and **phase**.
- In SHM, **kinetic energy** and **potential energy** continuously transform into each other, while the **total mechanical energy** remains constant.
- The time period of a **spring oscillator** is **T = 2π√(m/k)**, whereas that of a **simple pendulum** is **T = 2π√(L/g)** for small oscillations.
- **Damped oscillations** gradually lose amplitude due to resistive forces, whereas **forced oscillations** are maintained by an external periodic force.
- **Resonance** occurs when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency, resulting in maximum amplitude and efficient energy transfer.
- Oscillations and SHM are widely applied in clocks, musical instruments, engineering structures, communication systems, and many natural phenomena.

# Introduction

A **wave** is a disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another without the transfer of matter. Waves are responsible for many natural phenomena such as sound, water ripples, earthquakes, and light.

The study of waves is important in acoustics, communication systems, musical instruments, medicine, and engineering.

---

# 1. Wave Motion

## Definition

Wave motion is the propagation of a disturbance through a medium or space, transferring energy without transferring matter.

---

## Characteristics

- Transfers energy.
- Does not transfer matter.
- Produced by vibrating particles.
- May require a material medium or may propagate through vacuum depending on the type of wave.

---

# 2. Types of Waves

---

## (A) Mechanical Waves

Require a material medium for propagation.

Examples

- Sound waves
- Water waves
- Seismic waves

---

## (B) Electromagnetic Waves

Do not require a material medium.

Examples

- Light
- Radio waves
- X-rays
- Microwaves

---

# 3. Mechanical Waves

Mechanical waves are classified into:

- Transverse waves
- Longitudinal waves

---

## (A) Transverse Waves

Particles vibrate **perpendicular** to the direction of wave propagation.

Examples

- Waves on a stretched string
- Electromagnetic waves (transverse in nature)

---

### Characteristics

- Have crests and troughs.
- Can be polarized.
- Usually propagate in solids and on liquid surfaces.

---

## (B) Longitudinal Waves

Particles vibrate **parallel** to the direction of wave propagation.

Examples

- Sound waves in air
- Compression waves in springs

---

### Characteristics

- Consist of compressions and rarefactions.
- Cannot be polarized.
- Propagate through solids, liquids, and gases.

---

Maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium position.

Unit

Unit

# 5. Wave Equation

A progressive wave travelling along the positive x-axis is represented by

```
y = A sin(kx − ωt + φ)
```

or

```
y = A cos(kx − ωt + φ)
```

where

- **A** = Amplitude
- **k** = Wave number
- **ω** = Angular frequency
- **φ** = Initial phase

---

# 6. Progressive (Travelling) Waves

## Definition

A wave that continuously transfers energy from one point to another is called a progressive wave.

---

## Characteristics

- Energy is transmitted.
- Every particle oscillates with the same frequency.
- Amplitude remains constant in an ideal medium.
- No particle remains permanently at rest.

---

# 7. Stationary (Standing) Waves

## Definition

Standing waves are produced by the superposition of two progressive waves of the same frequency and amplitude travelling in opposite directions.

---

## Characteristics

- No net transfer of energy.
- Fixed nodes and antinodes are formed.
- All particles between two adjacent nodes oscillate in the same phase.

---

# 8. Nodes and Antinodes

---

## Node

A point where the displacement is always zero.

---

## Antinode

A point where the displacement is maximum.

---

## Distance Between

Adjacent nodes,

```
λ/2
```

Adjacent antinodes,

```
λ/2
```

Node to adjacent antinode,

```
λ/4
```

---

# 9. Principle of Superposition

## Statement

When two or more waves travel simultaneously through a medium, the resultant displacement at any point is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **wave motion** with the movement of matter; waves transfer **energy**, not matter.
- Mixing up **transverse** and **longitudinal** waves.
- Using the incorrect relation between **frequency**, **wavelength**, and **wave speed**; always use **v = fλ**.
- Assuming the speed of sound is the same in all media; it depends on the **elasticity** and **density** of the medium.
- Confusing **echo** with **reverberation**.
- Forgetting that **stationary waves** do **not transfer net energy**.
- Mixing up **nodes** and **antinodes** in standing waves.
- Assuming all harmonics are present in a **closed organ pipe**; only **odd harmonics** are produced.

# Chapter Summary

- A **wave** is a disturbance that transfers energy without transporting matter.
- Waves are broadly classified into **mechanical** and **electromagnetic** waves; mechanical waves may be **transverse** or **longitudinal**.
- The important wave parameters are **amplitude**, **wavelength**, **frequency**, **time period**, **wave speed**, **angular frequency**, and **wave number**.
- **Progressive waves** carry energy from one point to another, whereas **stationary waves** are formed by superposition and contain fixed **nodes** and **antinodes**.
- **Sound** is a longitudinal mechanical wave that requires a material medium for propagation.
- The speed of sound depends on the properties of the medium, particularly its elasticity, density, temperature, and humidity.
- **Reflection of sound** gives rise to phenomena such as **echo** and **reverberation**, which have practical applications in acoustics and SONAR.
- The **Doppler effect** explains the apparent change in frequency due to the relative motion between the source and the observer.
- **Beats** result from the interference of two sound waves of slightly different frequencies and are useful in tuning musical instruments.
- **Resonance**, **organ pipes**, and **harmonics** are important concepts that explain the production and amplification of sound in musical instruments and many physical systems.
