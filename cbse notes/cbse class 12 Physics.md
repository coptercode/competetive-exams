# Introduction

Electricity is associated with a fundamental property of matter called **electric charge**.

There are two types of charges:

1. Positive charge (+)
2. Negative charge (−)

Examples:

- Proton → Positive charge
- Electron → Negative charge

---

# 1. Electric Charge

## Definition

Electric charge is the property of matter due to which it experiences electrical forces.

Symbol:

\[
q
\]

SI unit:

\[
\text{Coulomb (C)}
\]

---

# Properties of Electric Charge

## 1. Additivity of Charge

Charges add algebraically.

If:

\[
q_1,q_2,q_3
\]

are charges,

Total charge:

\[
Q=q_1+q_2+q_3
\]

---

## 2. Conservation of Charge

Electric charge can neither be created nor destroyed.

It can only be transferred from one body to another.

---

## 3. Quantisation of Charge

Charge exists in discrete amounts.

\[
q=ne
\]

where:

- n = integer
- e = charge of electron

\[
e=1.6\times10^{-19}C
\]

---

# 2. Conductors and Insulators

## Conductors

Materials in which charges can move freely.

Examples:

- Metals
- Human body

---

## Insulators

Materials in which charges cannot move freely.

Examples:

- Rubber
- Glass
- Plastic

---

# 3. Coulomb's Law

## Statement

The electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of distance between them.

---

For two charges:

\[
q_1,q_2
\]

separated by distance:

\[
r
\]

Force:

\[
F\propto\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
F=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}
}
\]

---

where:

\[
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}=9\times10^9 Nm^2/C^2
\]

---

# Direction of Force

## Like Charges

Repel each other.

```
+       +

←       →
```

---

## Unlike Charges

Attract each other.

```
+       -

→       ←
```

---

# Coulomb's Law in Vector Form

\[
\vec F_{12}
=

\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}
\hat r
\]

---

# 4. Principle of Superposition

The total force on a charge is the vector sum of forces due to all other charges.

\[
\vec F=\vec F_1+\vec F_2+\vec F_3+...
\]

---

# 5. Electric Field

## Definition

The region around a charged body where another charge experiences force is called an electric field.

---

Electric field intensity:

\[
\boxed{
E=\frac{F}{q}
}
\]

---

SI unit:

\[
N/C
\]

or

\[
V/m
\]

---

# Electric Field Due to Point Charge

For charge:

\[
Q
\]

at distance:

\[
r
\]

Force:

\[
F=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{Qq}{r^2}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{Q}{r^2}
}
\]

---

# Direction of Electric Field

For positive charge:

```
     ↑
   ↖ | ↗
←---+---→
   ↙ | ↘
```

Away from charge.

---

For negative charge:

Towards charge.

---

# 6. Electric Field Lines

Electric field lines represent electric field direction.

---

# Properties

1. Start from positive charge and end at negative charge.
2. Never intersect each other.
3. Density represents field strength.
4. Tangent gives field direction.

---

# 7. Electric Dipole

## Definition

A pair of equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance.

Example:

\[
+q \quad -q
\]

---

Dipole moment:

\[
\boxed{
p=q(2a)
}
\]

where:

2a = separation between charges

---

Direction:

From negative charge to positive charge.

---

# 8. Electric Field Due to Dipole

---

# A. Axial Position

For a point on dipole axis:

\[
\boxed{
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{2p}{r^3}
}
\]

(for far point)

---

# B. Equatorial Position

\[
\boxed{
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{p}{r^3}
}
\]

Direction opposite to dipole moment.

---

# 9. Torque on Electric Dipole

When placed in uniform electric field:

\[
\boxed{
\tau=pE\sin\theta
}
\]

where:

- p = dipole moment
- E = electric field
- θ = angle between p and E

---

Maximum torque:

\[
\tau=pE
\]

when:

\[
\theta=90^\circ
\]

---

# 10. Electric Flux

## Definition

Electric flux measures the number of electric field lines passing through a surface.

Symbol:

\[
\Phi_E
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Phi_E=EA\cos\theta
}
\]

---

where:

- E = electric field
- A = area
- θ = angle between E and normal

---

SI unit:

\[
Nm^2/C
\]

---

# 11. Gauss's Law

## Statement

The total electric flux through a closed surface is equal to:

\[
\frac{1}{\epsilon_0}
\]

times the net charge enclosed.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Phi_E=\frac{q}{\epsilon_0}
}
\]

---

# Applications of Gauss's Law

---

# 1. Infinite Straight Charged Wire

Electric field:

\[
\boxed{
E=
\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r}
}
\]

where:

\[
\lambda
\]

is charge per unit length.

---

# 2. Infinite Plane Sheet of Charge

Electric field:

\[
\boxed{
E=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}
}
\]

where:

\[
\sigma
\]

is surface charge density.

---

# 3. Conducting Spherical Shell

## Outside:

\[
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{Q}{r^2}
\]

---

## Inside:

\[
\boxed{E=0}
\]

---

# 12. Electric Field of Uniformly Charged Sphere

## Outside:

Acts like a point charge:

\[
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{Q}{r^2}
\]

---

## Inside:

Electric field increases linearly with distance.

\[
E\propto r
\]

---

# 13. Charge Density

---

# Linear Charge Density

Charge per unit length:

\[
\lambda=\frac{Q}{L}
\]

Unit:

\[
C/m
\]

---

# Surface Charge Density

\[
\sigma=\frac{Q}{A}
\]

Unit:

\[
C/m^2
\]

---

# Volume Charge Density

\[
\rho=\frac{Q}{V}
\]

Unit:

\[
C/m^3
\]

---

# Comparison Table

## Electric Field vs Electric Force

| Electric Field        | Electric Force         |
| --------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Force per unit charge | Actual force on charge |
| \(E=F/q\)             | \(F=qE\)               |
| Unit N/C              | Unit Newton            |

---

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** Two charges of 2 μC and 3 μC are separated by 0.5 m. Find force.

Given:

\[
q_1=2\times10^{-6}C
\]

\[
q_2=3\times10^{-6}C
\]

\[
r=0.5m
\]

Using:

\[
F=
9\times10^9
\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}
\]

\[
F=
9\times10^9
\frac{(2\times10^{-6})(3\times10^{-6})}{0.25}
\]

\[
\boxed{F=0.216N}
\]

---

## Example 2

**Question:** Calculate electric field at 2 m from a charge of 5 μC.

\[
E=
9\times10^9
\frac{5\times10^{-6}}{4}
\]

\[
\boxed{
E=11250N/C
}
\]

---

## Example 3

**Question:** What is Gauss's law?

### Solution

The total electric flux through a closed surface is equal to the net charge enclosed divided by permittivity of free space.

\[
\Phi=\frac{q}{\epsilon_0}
\]

---

## Example 4

**Question:** Find torque on dipole when p = 2×10⁻⁶ Cm, E = 5×10⁴ N/C and θ=90°.

\[
\tau=pE
\]

\[
=2\times10^{-6}\times5\times10^4
\]

\[
\boxed{\tau=0.1Nm}
\]

---

## Example 5

**Question:** Why do electric field lines never intersect?

### Solution

At any point, electric field has only one definite direction. If field lines intersect, two directions would exist at the same point, which is impossible.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Forgetting that charge is a scalar quantity while electric field and force are vectors.
- Using Coulomb’s law without considering direction.
- Confusing electric field with electric force.
- Forgetting that electric field inside a conductor is zero in electrostatic equilibrium.
- Using dipole moment direction incorrectly (from negative to positive charge).
- Forgetting the angle in electric flux calculation.
- Applying Gauss's law without choosing a suitable Gaussian surface.
- Mixing surface charge density and volume charge density.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical interactions.
- Coulomb's law gives the force between point charges.
- Electric field describes the influence of charges around them.
- Electric dipoles consist of equal and opposite charges separated by a distance.
- Dipoles experience torque when placed in an external electric field.
- Electric flux measures field passing through a surface.
- Gauss's law relates electric flux to enclosed charge.
- Gauss's law is used to calculate fields of highly symmetric charge distributions such as wires, sheets, and spherical shells.

# Chapter 2: Electrostatic Potential and Capacitance

> **Board:** CBSE  
> **Class:** 12

> **Note:** This chapter explains electric potential, potential difference, equipotential surfaces, conductors, capacitors, dielectrics, and energy stored in capacitors.

---

# Introduction

When a charge is placed in an electric field, work is required to move it. This work done per unit charge is called **electric potential**.

---

# 1. Electric Potential

## Definition

Electric potential at a point is defined as the work done in bringing a unit positive test charge from infinity to that point.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
V=\frac{W}{q}
}
\]

where:

- V = electric potential
- W = work done
- q = test charge

---

## SI Unit

\[
\boxed{\text{Volt (V)}}
\]

\[
1V=1JC^{-1}
\]

---

# 2. Potential Difference

## Definition

The potential difference between two points is the work done per unit charge in moving a charge from one point to another.

\[
\boxed{
V_B-V_A=\frac{W}{q}
}
\]

---

# 3. Potential Due to a Point Charge

Consider a charge Q at distance r.

Potential:

\[
\boxed{
V=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{Q}{r}
}
\]

---

## Important Points

- Potential is a scalar quantity.
- It can be positive or negative.
- Potential decreases with distance from a positive charge.

---

# 4. Potential Due to a System of Charges

For charges:

\[
q_1,q_2,q_3...
\]

Total potential:

\[
\boxed{
V=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\left(
\frac{q_1}{r_1} +
\frac{q_2}{r_2} +
...
\right)
}
\]

---

# 5. Relation Between Electric Field and Potential

Electric field is the negative gradient of potential.

\[
\boxed{
E=-\frac{dV}{dr}
}
\]

---

Meaning:

- Electric field points from high potential to low potential.
- Potential decreases in direction of electric field.

---

# 6. Potential Energy

## Definition

Energy possessed by a charge due to its position in an electric field.

---

## Potential Energy of Two Charges

For charges:

\[
q_1,q_2
\]

separated by distance r:

\[
\boxed{
U=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q_1q_2}{r}
}
\]

---

# 7. Potential Energy of a System of Charges

\[
\boxed{
U=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\sum\frac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}}
}
\]

---

# 8. Equipotential Surfaces

## Definition

A surface where electric potential is the same at every point is called an equipotential surface.

---

# Properties

1. No work is done in moving a charge on an equipotential surface.

\[
W=q\Delta V
\]

Since:

\[
\Delta V=0
\]

\[
W=0
\]

---

2. Electric field is always perpendicular to an equipotential surface.

---

3. Two equipotential surfaces never intersect.

---

# Examples

## Point Charge

Equipotential surfaces are:

```
Concentric spheres
```

---

## Uniform Electric Field

Equipotential surfaces are:

```
Parallel planes
```

---

# 9. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium

## Properties

---

## 1. Electric Field Inside Conductor

\[
\boxed{E=0}
\]

---

## 2. Potential Inside Conductor

Potential remains constant.

---

## 3. Charge Distribution

Charge resides only on the outer surface.

---

## 4. Electric Field at Surface

\[
\boxed{
E=\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}
}
\]

where:

σ = surface charge density

---

# 10. Capacitance

## Definition

The ability of a conductor to store electric charge is called capacitance.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
C=\frac{Q}{V}
}
\]

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Farad (F)}}
\]

---

# 11. Parallel Plate Capacitor

A capacitor consists of two parallel conducting plates separated by an insulating material.

---

Capacitance:

\[
\boxed{
C=\frac{\epsilon_0A}{d}
}
\]

where:

- A = area of plates
- d = separation

---

# 12. Effect of Dielectric

A dielectric is an insulating material placed between capacitor plates.

Examples:

- Glass
- Mica
- Plastic

---

Dielectric constant:

\[
\boxed{
K=\frac{C}{C_0}
}
\]

---

New capacitance:

\[
\boxed{
C=K C_0
}
\]

---

# 13. Polarisation of Dielectric

When dielectric is placed in an electric field:

- Molecules become polarized.
- Bound charges shift slightly.
- Electric field reduces.

---

# 14. Combination of Capacitors

---

# A. Capacitors in Parallel

Diagram:

```
 |--C1--|
 |--C2--|
 |--C3--|
```

---

Voltage is same:

\[
V=V_1=V_2=V_3
\]

Equivalent capacitance:

\[
\boxed{
C=C_1+C_2+C_3
}
\]

---

# B. Capacitors in Series

Diagram:

```
--C1--C2--C3--
```

---

Charge is same:

\[
Q=Q_1=Q_2=Q_3
\]

Equivalent capacitance:

\[
\boxed{
\frac1C=
\frac1{C_1} +
\frac1{C_2} +
\frac1{C_3}
}
\]

---

# 15. Energy Stored in Capacitor

Energy stored:

\[
\boxed{
U=\frac12CV^2
}
\]

---

Other forms:

\[
\boxed{
U=\frac{Q^2}{2C}
}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
U=\frac12QV
}
\]

---

# 16. Energy Density

Energy stored per unit volume:

\[
\boxed{
u=
\frac12\epsilon_0E^2
}
\]

---

With dielectric:

\[
\boxed{
u=
\frac12K\epsilon_0E^2
}
\]

---

# 17. Van de Graaff Generator

## Principle

Based on:

```
Electrostatic induction
```

---

## Construction

Main parts:

- Insulating belt
- Motor

---

## Uses

- Particle acceleration.

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** Find potential at 2 m from a charge of 4 μC.

Given:

\[
Q=4\times10^{-6}C
\]

\[
r=2m
\]

Formula:

\[
V=
9\times10^9

\]

---

## Example 2

**Question:** A capacitor has charge 5 μC and potential 10 V. Find capacitance.

\[
C=\frac{Q}{V}
\]

\[
=\frac{5\times10^{-6}}{10}
\]

\[
\boxed{C=0.5\mu F}
\]

---

## Example 3

**Question:** Why is electric field zero inside a conductor?

### Solution

If an electric field existed inside a conductor, free electrons would move. In electrostatic equilibrium, charges stop moving; therefore the internal electric field must be zero.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** What happens when a dielectric is inserted between capacitor plates?

### Solution

The dielectric reduces electric field and increases capacitance by a factor equal to its dielectric constant.

\[

\]

---

\[

\]

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing electric potential with electric field.
- Forgetting that potential is a scalar quantity.
- Using vector addition for potentials.
- Forgetting that electric field is zero inside a conductor at electrostatic equilibrium.
- Mixing capacitor series and parallel formulas.
- Forgetting dielectric increases capacitance.
- Using incorrect units while calculating capacitance and energy.
- Forgetting that no work is done on an equipotential surface.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Electric potential describes the work required to move a unit charge.
- Potential due to a point charge depends inversely on distance.
- Equipotential surfaces have constant potential and require no work for charge movement.
- Conductors in electrostatic equilibrium have zero electric field inside.
- Capacitors store electrical energy by accumulating charges on plates.
- Capacitance depends on plate area, separation, and dielectric material.
- Capacitors can be connected in series or parallel.
- Dielectrics increase capacitance through polarization.
- Energy stored in a capacitor is given by:

\[
U=\frac12CV^2
\]

- Van de Graaff generator is an important application of electrostatic principles.

# Chapter 3: Current Electricity

> **Class:** 12

# Introduction

The flow of electric charge through a conductor is called **electric current**.

Electric current is produced due to the movement of free electrons in a conductor.

---

# 1. Electric Current

## Definition

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a cross-sectional area of a conductor.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac{Q}{t}
}
\]

where:

- I = current
- Q = charge
- t = time

---

## SI Unit

\[
\boxed{\text{Ampere (A)}}
\]

---

# 2. Direction of Electric Current

By convention:

- Direction of current = direction of positive charge flow.
- Electron flow is opposite to conventional current.

```
Current →

Electron flow ←
```

---

# 3. Current Density

## Definition

Current flowing per unit area of cross-section.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
J=\frac{I}{A}
}
\]

where:

- J = current density
- A = area

---

## Relation with Drift Velocity

\[
\boxed{
J=ne v_d
}
\]

where:

- n = number density of electrons
- e = charge of electron
- vd = drift velocity

---

# 4. Drift Velocity

## Definition

The average velocity acquired by free electrons in a conductor due to an applied electric field.

Symbol:

\[
v_d
\]

---

Without electric field:

```
Random motion of electrons
```

With electric field:

```
Net drift motion
```

---

# Formula

\[
\boxed{
v_d=\frac{eE\tau}{m}
}
\]

where:

- E = electric field
- τ = relaxation time
- m = electron mass

---

# 5. Mobility

## Definition

Drift velocity per unit electric field.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\mu=\frac{v_d}{E}
}
\]

---

Using drift velocity:

\[
\boxed{
\mu=\frac{e\tau}{m}
}
\]

---

# 6. Ohm's Law

## Statement

At constant temperature, current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to potential difference across it.

\[
V\propto I
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
V=IR
}
\]

---

where:

- V = voltage
- I = current
- R = resistance

---

# V-I Graph

For an ohmic conductor:

```
V

|
|      /
|    /
|  /
|____________ I
```

Straight line.

---

# Non-Ohmic Conductors

Do not obey Ohm's law.

Examples:

- Diode
- Semiconductor

---

# 7. Resistance

## Definition

Opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of current.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
R=\frac{V}{I}
}
\]

---

## SI Unit

\[
\boxed{\Omega}
\]

(Ohm)

---

# 8. Resistance and Dimensions of Conductor

Resistance depends on:

1. Length (l)
2. Area (A)
3. Material

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
R=\rho\frac{l}{A}
}
\]

---

where:

ρ = resistivity

---

# 9. Resistivity

## Definition

Resistance of a material having unit length and unit cross-sectional area.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\rho=\frac{RA}{l}
}
\]

---

## SI Unit

\[
\Omega m
\]

---

# Factors Affecting Resistivity

Depends on:

- Material
- Temperature

Does not depend on:

- Length
- Area

---

# 10. Temperature Dependence of Resistance

For metals:

\[
\boxed{
R_t=R_0(1+\alpha\Delta T)
}
\]

where:

- α = temperature coefficient of resistance

---

## Metals

Resistance increases with temperature.

---

## Semiconductors

Resistance decreases with temperature.

---

# 11. Combination of Resistances

---

# A. Series Combination

Circuit:

```
R1 ---- R2 ---- R3
```

Current is same.

\[
I=I_1=I_2=I_3
\]

Equivalent resistance:

\[
\boxed{
R=R_1+R_2+R_3
}
\]

---

# B. Parallel Combination

Circuit:

```
 |--R1--|
 |--R2--|
 |--R3--|
```

Voltage is same.

\[
V=V_1=V_2=V_3
\]

Equivalent resistance:

\[
\boxed{
\frac1R=
\frac1{R_1} +
\frac1{R_2} +
\frac1{R_3}
}
\]

---

# 12. Electrical Energy and Power

## Electrical Energy

\[
\boxed{
W=VIt
}
\]

Using:

\[
V=IR
\]

\[
W=I^2Rt
\]

---

# Electric Power

Rate of consumption of electrical energy.

# Introduction

A moving charge produces a magnetic field around itself. The branch of physics dealing with magnetic effects of moving charges is called **magnetism due to current**.

---

# 1. Magnetic Field

## Definition

The region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor where magnetic force can be experienced is called a magnetic field.

Symbol:

\[
B
\]

---

## SI Unit

\[
\boxed{\text{Tesla (T)}}
\]

---

# 2. Magnetic Force on a Moving Charge

When a charge moves in a magnetic field, it experiences a force.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\vec F=q(\vec v\times \vec B)
}
\]

Magnitude:

\[
\boxed{
F=qvB\sin\theta
}
\]

where:

- q = charge
- v = velocity
- B = magnetic field
- θ = angle between v and B

---

# Special Cases

## 1. Charge at Rest

\[
v=0
\]

Therefore:

\[
F=0
\]

---

## 2. Motion Parallel to Magnetic Field

\[
\theta=0^\circ
\]

\[
F=0
\]

---

## 3. Motion Perpendicular to Magnetic Field

\[
\theta=90^\circ
\]

\[
\boxed{
F=qvB
}
\]

Maximum force.

---

# 3. Lorentz Force

When electric and magnetic fields are both present:

\[
\boxed{
\vec F=q(\vec E+\vec v\times\vec B)
}
\]

---

# 4. Motion of Charged Particle in Magnetic Field

When charged particle enters perpendicular to magnetic field:

Magnetic force provides centripetal force.

\[
qvB=\frac{mv^2}{r}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
r=\frac{mv}{qB}
}
\]

---

# Time Period

\[
\boxed{
T=\frac{2\pi m}{qB}
}
\]

---

# Frequency

\[
\boxed{
\nu=\frac{qB}{2\pi m}
}
\]

---

# 5. Cyclotron

## Definition

A device used to accelerate charged particles using electric and magnetic fields.

---

# Principle

Charged particles moving perpendicular to a magnetic field experience circular motion.

---

# Cyclotron Frequency

\[
\boxed{
\nu=\frac{qB}{2\pi m}
}
\]

---

# Limitations

- Cannot accelerate neutral particles.
- Not suitable for very high velocities due to relativistic effects.

---

# 6. Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

A current-carrying wire placed in a magnetic field experiences force.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\vec F=I(\vec l\times\vec B)
}
\]

Magnitude:

\[
\boxed{
F=IlB\sin\theta
}
\]

---

# Fleming's Left-Hand Rule

Used to find direction of force.

```
Thumb → Force

First finger → Magnetic field

Second finger → Current
```

---

\[

\]

separated by distance:

\[

Force per unit length:

\[
\boxed{
\frac FL=
\frac{\mu_0 I_1I_2}{2\pi d}
}
\]

---

# Definition of Ampere

One ampere is the current which produces a force of:

\[
2\times10^{-7}N/m
\]

between two parallel conductors separated by 1 m in vacuum.

## Statement

Formula:

\[
\boxed{

\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}

}
\]

---

- μ₀ = permeability of free space
- dl = current element

---

\[
\boxed{

}
\]

---

Direction:

Right-hand thumb rule.

---

# Right-Hand Thumb Rule

If thumb points in direction of current, curled fingers show magnetic field direction.

- N = number of turns
- R = radius

# Introduction

The phenomenon of production of an induced electromotive force (EMF) due to a change in magnetic flux linked with a circuit is called **electromagnetic induction**.

It was discovered by:

\[
\boxed{\text{Michael Faraday}}
\]

---

# 1. Magnetic Flux

## Definition

Magnetic flux is the amount of magnetic field passing through a surface.

Symbol:

\[
\Phi_B
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Phi_B=BA\cos\theta
}
\]

where:

- B = magnetic field
- A = area
- θ = angle between magnetic field and area vector

---

## SI Unit

\[
\boxed{\text{Weber (Wb)}}
\]

---

# 2. Faraday's Laws of Electromagnetic Induction

---

# First Law

## Statement

Whenever the magnetic flux linked with a circuit changes, an induced EMF is produced in the circuit.

---

# Second Law

## Statement

The magnitude of induced EMF is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux.

\[
\boxed{
\epsilon\propto\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}
}
\]

For a coil of N turns:

\[
\boxed{
\epsilon=-N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}
}
\]

---

# 3. Negative Sign in Faraday's Law

The negative sign represents:

\[
\boxed{\text{Lenz's Law}}
\]

It shows that induced current opposes the change producing it.

---

# 4. Lenz's Law

## Statement

The direction of induced current is such that it opposes the change in magnetic flux responsible for producing it.

---

## Conservation of Energy

Lenz's law ensures that energy is conserved because induced current opposes the cause producing it.

---

# 5. Motional EMF

## Definition

EMF produced due to motion of a conductor in a magnetic field is called motional EMF.

---

Consider a rod of length l moving with velocity v in magnetic field B.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\epsilon=Blv
}
\]

(for perpendicular motion)

---

# 6. Induced Current

Using Ohm's law:

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac{\epsilon}{R}
}
\]

---

# 7. Force on Moving Rod

A current-carrying rod experiences magnetic force:

\[
F=BIl
\]

Substituting:

\[
\boxed{
F=\frac{B^2l^2v}{R}
}
\]

---

# 8. Eddy Currents

## Definition

Circular currents induced in bulk conductors due to changing magnetic flux are called eddy currents.

---

# Applications of Eddy Currents

## 1. Electromagnetic Brakes

Used in trains and vehicles.

---

## 2. Induction Furnace

Used for melting metals.

---

## 3. Speedometers

Used for measuring speed.

---

## 4. Galvanometer Damping

Helps stop coil movement quickly.

---

# Disadvantages

Eddy currents cause:

- Energy loss
- Heating

---

# Reduction of Eddy Currents

Using:

- Laminated iron cores
- Thin insulated sheets

---

# 9. Self-Induction

## Definition

The phenomenon in which a changing current in a coil induces EMF in the same coil is called self-induction.

---

Induced EMF:

\[
\boxed{
\epsilon=-L\frac{dI}{dt}
}
\]

where:

L = self-inductance

---

# 10. Self-Inductance

## Definition

The property of a coil by which it opposes any change in current through it.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
L=\frac{N\Phi}{I}
}
\]

---

## SI Unit

\[
\boxed{\text{Henry (H)}}
\]

---

# 11. Factors Affecting Self-Inductance

Depends on:

1. Number of turns (N)
2. Area of coil (A)
3. Length of coil
4. Magnetic permeability of core

---

# 12. Mutual Induction

## Definition

The phenomenon in which a changing current in one coil induces EMF in another nearby coil is called mutual induction.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\epsilon_2=-M\frac{dI_1}{dt}
}
\]

where:

M = mutual inductance

---

# 13. Mutual Inductance

## Definition

Measure of the ability of one coil to induce EMF in another coil.

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Henry (H)}}
\]

---

# 14. Energy Stored in an Inductor

When current flows through an inductor, energy is stored in its magnetic field.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
U=\frac12LI^2
}
\]

---

# 15. AC Generator

## Principle

Based on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Electromagnetic induction}}
\]

---

# Construction

Main parts:

- Armature coil
- Magnetic field
- Slip rings
- Brushes

---

# Working

When a coil rotates in a magnetic field:

- Magnetic flux changes.
- EMF is induced.
- Alternating current is produced.

---

# Generated EMF

\[
\boxed{
\epsilon=NBA\omega\sin\omega t
}
\]

---

Maximum EMF:

\[
\boxed{
\epsilon_0=NBA\omega
}
\]

---

# 16. Transformer

## Definition

A device that changes AC voltage using mutual induction.

---

# Principle

\[
\boxed{\text{Mutual induction}}
\]

---

# Types

---

## Step-Up Transformer

Increases voltage.

\[
N_s>N_p
\]

---

## Step-Down Transformer

Decreases voltage.

\[
N_s<N_p
\]

---

# Transformer Equation

\[
\boxed{
\frac{V_s}{V_p}
=

\frac{N_s}{N_p}
}
\]

---

# Current Relation

For ideal transformer:

\[
\boxed{
V_pI_p=V_sI_s
}
\]

---

# Efficiency

\[
\boxed{
\eta=
\frac{\text{Output power}}
{\text{Input power}}
\times100
}
\]

---

# 17. Losses in Transformer

---

## 2. Eddy Current Loss

---

## 3. Hysteresis Loss

---

## 4. Flux Leakage

Due to incomplete coupling.

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** A rod of length 2 m moves with velocity 5 m/s perpendicular to a magnetic field of 0.4 T. Find induced EMF.

Given:

\[
l=2m
\]

\[
v=5m/s
\]

\[
B=0.4T
\]

Formula:

\[
\epsilon=Blv
\]

\[
=0.4\times2\times5
\]

\[
\boxed{\epsilon=4V}
\]

---

## Example 2

**Question:** State Faraday's second law.

### Solution

The induced EMF is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux linked with the circuit.

\[
\epsilon=-N\frac{d\Phi}{dt}
\]

---

## Example 3

**Question:** Why are transformer cores laminated?

### Solution

Lamination reduces eddy current losses and prevents unnecessary heating of the core.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** Calculate energy stored in an inductor of inductance 2 H carrying current 3 A.

\[
U=\frac12LI^2
\]

\[
=\frac12(2)(9)
\]

\[
\boxed{U=9J}
\]

---

## Example 5

**Question:** Why does Lenz's law obey conservation of energy?

### Solution

The induced current always opposes the change in flux, requiring external work. This prevents creation of energy without input.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Forgetting the negative sign in Faraday's law.
- Confusing magnetic flux with magnetic field.
- Mixing self-induction and mutual induction.
- Forgetting that transformers work only on AC.
- Using transformer equations for DC circuits.
- Confusing step-up and step-down transformers.
- Ignoring the direction given by Lenz's law.
- Forgetting units of inductance (Henry).

---

# Chapter Summary

- Electromagnetic induction is the production of EMF due to changing magnetic flux.
- Faraday's law gives the magnitude of induced EMF.
- Lenz's law determines the direction of induced current.
- Motional EMF is produced when a conductor moves in a magnetic field.
- Self-induction occurs in the same coil, while mutual induction occurs between two coils.
- Inductors store energy in magnetic fields.
- AC generators produce alternating current using electromagnetic induction.
- Transformers work on mutual induction to increase or decrease AC voltage.
- Eddy currents have both useful applications and energy-loss effects.

# Introduction

The current whose magnitude and direction change periodically with time is called **alternating current (AC)**.

Example:

Household electricity supply.

---

# 1. Alternating Current

Instantaneous value of current:

\[
\boxed{
i=I_0\sin\omega t
}
\]

where:

- i = instantaneous current
- I₀ = peak current
- ω = angular frequency

---

# Alternating Voltage

\[
\boxed{
v=V_0\sin\omega t
}
\]

where:

- V₀ = peak voltage

---

# 2. Important Terms

---

# Peak Value

Maximum value of current or voltage.

Symbol:

\[
I_0,\ V_0
\]

---

# Time Period

Time taken for one complete cycle.

\[
\boxed{
T=\frac{1}{f}
}
\]

---

# Frequency

Number of cycles per second.

Unit:

\[
Hz
\]

---

# Angular Frequency

\[
\boxed{
\omega=2\pi f
}
\]

---

# 3. Mean Value of AC

Average value of AC over a complete cycle:

\[
\boxed{0}
\]

because positive and negative halves cancel.

---

# 4. RMS Value of AC

## Definition

The effective value of AC that produces the same heating effect as an equivalent DC current.

---

RMS current:

\[
\boxed{
I_{rms}=\frac{I_0}{\sqrt2}
}
\]

---

RMS voltage:

\[
\boxed{
V_{rms}=\frac{V_0}{\sqrt2}
}
\]

---

For household supply:

\[
V_{rms}=220V
\]

---

# 5. AC Through a Resistor

Circuit:

```
AC Source ---- R
```

Voltage:

\[
v=V_0\sin\omega t
\]

Current:

\[
i=I_0\sin\omega t
\]

---

## Phase Relationship

Voltage and current are in the same phase.

\[
\boxed{\phi=0}
\]

---

# 6. AC Through an Inductor

Inductive circuit:

```
AC Source ---- L
```

---

Inductive reactance:

\[
\boxed{
X_L=\omega L
}
\]

---

Current:

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac{V}{X_L}
}
\]

---

## Phase Relationship

Current lags voltage by:

\[
90^\circ
\]

or

\[
\frac{\pi}{2}
\]

---

# 7. AC Through a Capacitor

Capacitive circuit:

```
AC Source ---- C
```

---

Capacitive reactance:

\[
\boxed{
X_C=\frac1{\omega C}
}
\]

---

Current:

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac{V}{X_C}
}
\]

---

## Phase Relationship

Current leads voltage by:

\[
90^\circ
\]

---

# 8. Series LCR Circuit

Combination of:

- Resistance (R)
- Inductance (L)
- Capacitance (C)

---

Circuit:

```
R ---- L ---- C
```

---

# Impedance

The opposition offered by an AC circuit is called impedance.

Symbol:

\[
Z
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2}
}
\]

---

Current:

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac VZ
}
\]

---

# 9. Phase Angle

The phase difference between voltage and current:

\[
\boxed{
\tan\phi=
\frac{X_L-X_C}{R}
}
\]

---

# Cases

## Inductive Circuit

\[
X_L>X_C
\]

Current lags.

---

## Capacitive Circuit

\[
X_C>X_L
\]

Current leads.

---

## Pure Resistive Circuit

\[
X_L=X_C
\]

Phase difference:

\[
\phi=0
\]

---

# 10. Resonance in LCR Circuit

## Definition

The condition when inductive reactance equals capacitive reactance.

\[
\boxed{
X_L=X_C
}
\]

---

Therefore:

\[
\omega L=\frac1{\omega C}
\]

---

Resonant frequency:

\[
\boxed{
\omega_0=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}}
}
\]

or

\[
\boxed{
f_0=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}
}
\]

---

# Characteristics of Resonance

1. Impedance is minimum.

\[
Z=R
\]

2. Current is maximum.

\[
I=\frac VR
\]

3. Power factor is unity.

\[
\cos\phi=1
\]

---

# Applications

- Radio tuning
- Signal selection
- Filters

---

# 11. Power in AC Circuit

Average power:

\[
\boxed{
P=V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\phi
}
\]

---

where:

\[
\cos\phi
\]

is the power factor.

---

# Power Factor

\[
\boxed{
\cos\phi=\frac RZ
}
\]

---

# Special Cases

## Pure Resistance

\[
\phi=0
\]

\[
P=VI
\]

---

## Pure Inductor or Capacitor

\[
\phi=90^\circ
\]

\[
P=0
\]

Called:

```
Wattless current
```

---

# 12. Transformer

## Definition

A device used to increase or decrease AC voltage.

---

Principle:

\[
\boxed{\text{Mutual Induction}}
\]

---

# Construction

Parts:

1. Primary coil
2. Secondary coil
3. Laminated iron core

---

# Transformer Equation

\[
\boxed{
\frac{V_s}{V_p}
=

\frac{N_s}{N_p}
}
\]

---

# Step-Up Transformer

Increases voltage.

Condition:

\[
N_s>N_p
\]

---

# Step-Down Transformer

Decreases voltage.

Condition:

\[
N_s<N_p
\]

---

# Current Relation

For ideal transformer:

\[
\boxed{
V_pI_p=V_sI_s
}
\]

---

# Efficiency

\[
\boxed{
\eta=
\frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}
\times100
}
\]

---

# 13. AC Generator

## Principle

Electromagnetic induction.

---

}
\]


---

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** Find RMS value of AC current having peak value 10 A.

\[
I_{rms}=\frac{I_0}{\sqrt2}

\[
=\frac{10}{1.414}
\]

\[
\boxed{I_{rms}=7.07A}
\]

---

## Example 2

**Question:** Find inductive reactance of an inductor of 0.5 H at 50 Hz.

Given:

\[
L=0.5H
\]

\[
f=50Hz
\]

\[
\omega=2\pi f
\]

\[
X_L=\omega L
\]

\]

## Example 3

**Question:** What happens at resonance in an LCR circuit?

### Solution

At resonance:

\[
X_L=X_C
\]

Impedance becomes minimum and current becomes maximum.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** Why does a transformer work only on AC?

### Solution

Transformer requires changing magnetic flux. DC produces constant flux and cannot induce EMF in the secondary coil.

---

## Example 5

**Question:** What is wattless current?

### Solution

Current in a pure inductor or capacitor where average power consumption is zero is called wattless current.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing peak value and RMS value.
- Forgetting that average AC value over one cycle is zero.
- Mixing leading and lagging phase relationships.
- Using \(X_L\) and \(X_C\) formulas incorrectly.
- Forgetting that resonance occurs when \(X_L=X_C\).
- Using transformer equations for DC.
- Forgetting power factor in AC power calculations.
- Confusing impedance with resistance.

X_L=X_C

\[
P=V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\phi
\]

- Transformers work on mutual induction and change AC voltage levels.
- AC generators convert mechanical energy into electrical energy using electromagnetic induction.

# Introduction

Electromagnetic waves are waves consisting of oscillating electric and magnetic fields that can travel through vacuum.

They were theoretically predicted by:

\[
\boxed{\text{James Clerk Maxwell}}
\]

Experimental verification was done by:

\[
\boxed{\text{Heinrich Hertz}}
\]

---

# 1. Displacement Current

## Need for Displacement Current

According to Ampere's law, current produces magnetic fields.

However, in a charging capacitor, current flows in wires but no conduction current exists between the plates.

Maxwell introduced the concept of **displacement current**.

---

# Definition

The current produced due to a changing electric field is called displacement current.

Symbol:

\[
I_d
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
I_d=\epsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}
}
\]

where:

- \(\epsilon_0\) = permittivity of free space
- \(\Phi_E\) = electric flux

---

# 2. Modified Ampere's Law

Maxwell modified Ampere's law:

\[
\boxed{
\oint B.dl=
\mu_0(I_c+I_d)
}
\]

where:

- \(I_c\) = conduction current
- \(I_d\) = displacement current

---

# 3. Electromagnetic Waves

## Definition

Electromagnetic waves are waves produced by accelerated charges consisting of time-varying electric and magnetic fields.

---

# Production

A changing electric field produces a magnetic field.

A changing magnetic field produces an electric field.

Together they propagate as electromagnetic waves.

---

# 4. Nature of Electromagnetic Waves

Electromagnetic waves are:

- Transverse waves.
- Do not require a medium.
- Can travel through vacuum.

---

# 5. Characteristics of Electromagnetic Waves

---

## 1. Electric and Magnetic Fields

Electric field:

\[
\vec E
\]

and magnetic field:

\[
\vec B
\]

are perpendicular to each other.

---

They are also perpendicular to direction of propagation.

```
        E

        ↑

        |

Wave →→→→→

        |

        ↓

        B
```

---

## 2. Speed

Speed in vacuum:

\[
\boxed{
c=3\times10^8m/s
}
\]

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
c=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}
}
\]

---

## 3. No Charge Transport

Electromagnetic waves transfer energy but not charge.

---

# 6. Properties of Electromagnetic Waves

1. Produced by accelerated charges.
2. Travel in vacuum.
3. Speed is same for all EM waves in vacuum.
4. Electric and magnetic fields are in phase.
5. Carry energy and momentum.
6. Show reflection, refraction, interference, and diffraction.

---

# 7. Energy in Electromagnetic Waves

Energy is stored in electric and magnetic fields.

---

Energy density of electric field:

\[
u_E=\frac12\epsilon_0E^2
\]

---

Energy density of magnetic field:

\[
u_B=\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}
\]

---

For electromagnetic waves:

\[
\boxed{
u_E=u_B
}
\]

---

# Introduction

Wave optics deals with the phenomena of light that cannot be explained by ray optics.

It proves that light behaves as a **wave**.

Important phenomena:

1. Interference
2. Diffraction
3. Polarisation

---

# 1. Huygens' Principle

## Statement

Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets that spread in all directions with the speed of light.

The new wavefront is the forward envelope of these wavelets.

---

# 2. Wavefront

## Definition

A wavefront is the locus of all points having the same phase of vibration.

---

# Types of Wavefronts

## 1. Spherical Wavefront

Produced by a point source.

Example:

Bulb

---

## 2. Plane Wavefront

Produced when source is very far away.

Example:

Sunlight

---

## 3. Cylindrical Wavefront

Produced by a line source.

---

# 3. Huygens' Principle and Reflection

Using Huygens' principle:

Law of reflection can be derived:

\[
\boxed{i=r}
\]

---

# 4. Huygens' Principle and Refraction

Refraction occurs due to change in speed of light in different media.

Snell's law:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}
=

\frac{v_1}{v_2}
}
\]

---

# 5. Principle of Superposition

## Statement

When two or more waves overlap, the resultant displacement is the vector sum of individual displacements.

\[
\boxed{
y=y_1+y_2
}
\]

---

# 6. Interference of Light

## Definition

The phenomenon of redistribution of intensity due to superposition of two coherent light waves is called interference.

---

# Conditions for Sustained Interference

1. Sources must be coherent.
2. Same frequency.
3. Constant phase difference.
4. Nearly equal amplitudes.

---

# 7. Constructive Interference

## Definition

When two waves meet in the same phase, intensity becomes maximum.

---

Condition:

Path difference:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta x=n\lambda
}
\]

where:

\[
n=0,1,2...
\]

---

Intensity:

\[
\boxed{
I_{max}=(\sqrt{I_1}+\sqrt{I_2})^2
}
\]

---

# 8. Destructive Interference

## Definition

When two waves meet in opposite phases, intensity becomes minimum.

---

Condition:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta x=(2n+1)\frac{\lambda}{2}
}
\]

---

Intensity:

\[
\boxed{
I_{min}=(\sqrt{I_1}-\sqrt{I_2})^2
}
\]

---

\[
\boxed{\text{Thomas Young}}
\]

\[

\]

\[

\]

\[
\boxed{
\Delta x=\frac{dy}{D}
}
\]

---

# Comparison Tables

## n-Type vs p-Type Semiconductor

| n-Type                          | p-Type                      |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Pentavalent impurity            | Trivalent impurity          |
| Electrons are majority carriers | Holes are majority carriers |
| Example: Phosphorus             | Example: Boron              |

---

## Forward Bias vs Reverse Bias

| Forward Bias               | Reverse Bias               |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Depletion region decreases | Depletion region increases |
| High current               | Small current              |
| Conducting state           | Blocking state             |

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** Why does semiconductor conductivity increase with temperature?

### Solution

Increasing temperature breaks more covalent bonds, creating more free electrons and holes, increasing conductivity.

---

## Example 2

**Question:** Why is silicon preferred over germanium?

### Solution

Silicon has:

- Larger band gap.
- Better thermal stability.
- Lower leakage current.

---

## Example 3

**Question:** What are majority carriers in n-type semiconductor?

### Solution

Electrons are majority carriers.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** What is the function of a Zener diode?

### Solution

It maintains a constant output voltage and is used as a voltage regulator.

---

## Example 5

**Question:** Why is a diode used as a rectifier?

### Solution

A diode allows current mainly in one direction, converting AC into DC.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing electrons and holes as actual particles moving in opposite directions.
- Forgetting majority carriers in p-type and n-type materials.
- Mixing forward and reverse bias connections.
- Confusing LED and photodiode applications.
- Forgetting that Zener diode works in reverse bias.
- Using transistor current equations incorrectly.
- Confusing semiconductor conductivity behaviour with metals.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Semiconductors have conductivity between conductors and insulators.
- Doping produces n-type and p-type semiconductors.
- A p-n junction forms a depletion region and barrier potential.
- Diodes allow current mainly in one direction.
- Rectifiers convert AC into DC.
- Zener diodes regulate voltage.
- LEDs emit light and photodiodes detect light.
- Solar cells convert sunlight into electricity.
- Transistors work as amplifiers and switches.
- Logic gates form the foundation of digital electronics.
