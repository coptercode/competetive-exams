# Introduction

A **chemical reaction** is a process in which one or more substances (reactants) are converted into new substances (products) with different chemical properties. During a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged, but the total number of atoms of each element remains unchanged according to the **Law of Conservation of Mass**.

Chemical reactions occur all around us, such as the rusting of iron, digestion of food, burning of fuels, cooking, respiration, and photosynthesis.

---

# 1. Chemical Reaction

A **chemical reaction** is a process in which new substances are formed with different physical and chemical properties.

### Characteristics of a Chemical Reaction

- Evolution of a gas.
- Change in colour.
- Formation of a precipitate.
- Change in temperature.
- Change in state.
- Emission of light or sound (in some reactions).

### Examples

1. Burning of magnesium ribbon

```
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
```

Observation:

- Bright white flame.
- White ash (magnesium oxide) is formed.

---

2. Reaction between zinc and hydrochloric acid

```
Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂↑
```

Observation:

- Hydrogen gas is evolved.
- Zinc gradually dissolves.

---

# 2. Chemical Equations

A **chemical equation** represents a chemical reaction using chemical symbols and formulae.

### Word Equation

```
Magnesium + Oxygen
↓

Magnesium Oxide
```

### Chemical Equation

```
Mg + O₂ → MgO
```

This equation is **unbalanced**.

---

# 3. Balanced Chemical Equation

A **balanced chemical equation** has an equal number of atoms of each element on both sides.

### Balanced Equation

```
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
```

### Importance of Balancing

- Obeys the Law of Conservation of Mass.
- Shows correct proportions of reactants and products.
- Helps in quantitative calculations.

---

# 4. Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations

### Steps

1. Write the word equation.
2. Convert it into a chemical equation.
3. Count the atoms on both sides.
4. Balance by adjusting coefficients only.
5. Verify the equation.

### Example

Unbalanced equation

```
Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃
```

Balanced equation

```
4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃
```

---

# 5. Types of Chemical Reactions

## (A) Combination Reaction

Two or more substances combine to form a single product.

### General Equation

```
A + B → AB
```

### Examples

Formation of water

```
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
```

Formation of calcium oxide

```
CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + Heat
```

This reaction is also **exothermic**.

---

## (B) Decomposition Reaction

A single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances.

### General Equation

```
AB → A + B
```

### Types of Decomposition

### (i) Thermal Decomposition

Occurs on heating.

Example

```
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
```

---

### (ii) Electrolytic Decomposition

Occurs by passing electricity.

Example

```
2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂
```

---

### (iii) Photolytic Decomposition

Occurs in the presence of sunlight.

Example

```
2AgCl → 2Ag + Cl₂
```

Silver chloride changes from **white to grey**.

Another example

```
2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂
```

These reactions are used in **black-and-white photography**.

---

## (C) Displacement Reaction

A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound.

### General Equation

```
A + BC → AC + B
```

### Example

```
Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
```

Observation

- Blue solution becomes colourless.
- Copper is deposited.

---

## (D) Double Displacement Reaction

Two compounds exchange ions to form new compounds.

### General Equation

```
AB + CD → AD + CB
```

### Example

```
Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + 2NaCl
```

A white precipitate of **barium sulfate** is formed.

---

### Precipitation Reaction

A reaction in which an insoluble solid (precipitate) is formed.

Example

```
Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂↓ + 2KNO₃
```

Observation

- Yellow precipitate of lead iodide is formed.

---

# 6. Oxidation and Reduction (Redox Reactions)

## Oxidation

A reaction involving:

- Addition of oxygen.
- Removal of hydrogen.
- Loss of electrons.

### Example

```
2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO
```

Copper is oxidized.

---

## Reduction

A reaction involving:

- Removal of oxygen.
- Addition of hydrogen.
- Gain of electrons.

### Example

```
CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O
```

Copper oxide is reduced to copper.

---

## Redox Reaction

A reaction in which oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously.

### Example

```
CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O
```

Here,

- Hydrogen is oxidized to water.
- Copper oxide is reduced to copper.

---

# 7. Oxidizing and Reducing Agents

## Oxidizing Agent

An oxidizing agent causes oxidation by supplying oxygen or accepting electrons.

### Example

```
CuO
```

acts as an oxidizing agent.

---

## Reducing Agent

A reducing agent causes reduction by removing oxygen or donating electrons.

### Example

```
H₂
```

acts as a reducing agent.

---

# 8. Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

## Exothermic Reaction

A reaction that releases heat.

### Example

Combustion of methane

```
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + Heat
```

Other examples

- Respiration
- Burning of fuels

---

## Endothermic Reaction

A reaction that absorbs heat.

### Example

Thermal decomposition

```
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
```

---

# 9. Effects of Oxidation in Daily Life

## (A) Corrosion

**Corrosion** is the gradual destruction of metals due to reaction with oxygen, moisture, or other substances in the environment.

### Example

Rusting of iron

```
4Fe + 3O₂ + xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O
```

### Prevention of Corrosion

- Painting
- Oiling and greasing
- Galvanization
- Alloying
- Electroplating

---

## (B) Rancidity

**Rancidity** is the oxidation of fats and oils, causing unpleasant smell and taste.

### Prevention

- Store food in airtight containers.
- Refrigeration.
- Vacuum packing.
- Use antioxidants.
- Pack food with nitrogen gas.

---

# 10. Importance of Chemical Reactions

Chemical reactions are essential in:

- Respiration.
- Photosynthesis.
- Digestion.
- Manufacture of medicines.
- Production of fertilizers.
- Extraction of metals.
- Food processing.
- Industrial manufacturing.

# Common Mistakes

- Writing chemical formulae incorrectly before balancing equations.
- Changing subscripts instead of coefficients while balancing equations.
- Confusing **combination** and **decomposition** reactions.
- Assuming every double displacement reaction produces a precipitate.
- Mixing up oxidation with reduction.
- Forgetting that oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously in a redox reaction.
- Confusing corrosion with rancidity.
- Ignoring the physical states of reactants and products when writing equations.

# Chapter Summary

- A **chemical reaction** converts reactants into products with new properties.
- A **balanced chemical equation** obeys the **Law of Conservation of Mass** by having equal numbers of atoms on both sides.
- **Combination reactions** produce a single product, while **decomposition reactions** break compounds into simpler substances.
- **Displacement reactions** involve a more reactive element replacing a less reactive one, whereas **double displacement reactions** involve the exchange of ions between compounds.
- **Oxidation** involves the addition of oxygen or removal of hydrogen, while **reduction** involves the removal of oxygen or addition of hydrogen.
- In a **redox reaction**, oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously.
- **Exothermic reactions** release heat, whereas **endothermic reactions** absorb heat.
- **Corrosion** damages metals through oxidation, while **rancidity** spoils food containing fats and oils due to oxidation.
- Chemical reactions play a vital role in everyday life, biological processes, and industrial applications.

# Introduction

**Acids, bases, and salts** are important classes of chemical substances that play a vital role in everyday life and industries. They are used in medicines, agriculture, food preparation, cleaning products, water treatment, and manufacturing. This chapter explains their properties, reactions, the **pH scale**, and the preparation and uses of important compounds such as **sodium hydroxide, bleaching powder, baking soda,** and **plaster of Paris**.

---

# 1. Acids

An **acid** is a substance that produces **hydrogen ions (H⁺)** in aqueous solution.

### Characteristics of Acids

- Sour in taste.
- Turn **blue litmus red**.
- Conduct electricity in aqueous solution.
- React with metals to produce hydrogen gas.
- React with bases to form salt and water.

### Examples

- Hydrochloric acid (HCl)
- Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)
- Nitric acid (HNO₃)
- Acetic acid (CH₃COOH)
- Citric acid (in lemons)
- Tartaric acid (in tamarind)

---

# 2. Bases

A **base** is a substance that produces **hydroxide ions (OH⁻)** in aqueous solution.

### Characteristics of Bases

- Bitter in taste.
- Feel soapy to touch.
- Turn **red litmus blue**.
- Conduct electricity in aqueous solution.
- Neutralize acids.

### Examples

- Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)
- Potassium hydroxide (KOH)
- Calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂]
- Magnesium hydroxide [Mg(OH)₂]

---

# 3. Indicators

**Indicators** are substances that change colour in acidic or basic solutions.

### Types of Indicators

| Indicator           | Acid                              | Base                              |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Blue Litmus         | Turns Red                         | No Change                         |
| Red Litmus          | No Change                         | Turns Blue                        |
| Phenolphthalein     | Colourless                        | Pink                              |
| Methyl Orange       | Red                               | Yellow                            |
| Universal Indicator | Different colours depending on pH | Different colours depending on pH |

---

# 4. Chemical Properties of Acids and Bases

## (A) Reaction of Acids with Metals

Acids react with metals to produce **salt** and **hydrogen gas**.

### General Equation

```
Metal + Acid → Salt + Hydrogen
```

### Example

```
Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂↑
```

Observation:

- Hydrogen gas is evolved.
- Zinc gradually dissolves.

---

## (B) Reaction of Bases with Certain Metals

Strong bases react with metals like **zinc** and **aluminium** to produce hydrogen gas.

### Example

```
2NaOH + Zn → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂↑
```

---

## (C) Reaction of Acids with Metal Carbonates

Acids react with metal carbonates to produce **salt, carbon dioxide,** and **water**.

### General Equation

```
Metal Carbonate + Acid → Salt + CO₂ + H₂O
```

### Example

```
Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + CO₂ + H₂O
```

---

## (D) Reaction of Acids with Metal Hydrogen Carbonates

### General Equation

```
Metal Hydrogen Carbonate + Acid

↓

Salt + CO₂ + H₂O
```

### Example

```
NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + CO₂ + H₂O
```

---

## (E) Neutralization Reaction

An acid reacts with a base to form **salt** and **water**.

### General Equation

```
Acid + Base → Salt + Water
```

### Example

```
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
```

This is an **exothermic reaction**.

---

# 5. pH Scale

The **pH scale** measures the acidity or basicity of a solution.

```
pH = -log[H⁺]
```

The pH scale ranges from **0 to 14**.

|       pH Range | Nature  |
| -------------: | ------- |
|    Less than 7 | Acidic  |
|              7 | Neutral |
| Greater than 7 | Basic   |

---

# 6. Importance of pH in Daily Life

## (A) Digestion

- Hydrochloric acid in the stomach helps digestion.
- Excess acid causes indigestion.
- Antacids such as **milk of magnesia** neutralize excess acid.

---

## (B) Tooth Decay

- Tooth enamel begins to dissolve when the pH falls below **5.5**.
- Basic toothpaste helps neutralize acids.

---

## (C) Soil Treatment

- Acidic soil is treated with **quicklime (CaO)** or **slaked lime [Ca(OH)₂]**.
- Basic soil is treated with organic matter.

---

## (D) Self-Defence by Animals and Plants

- Bee and ant stings contain **formic acid**.
- Baking soda relieves the effect of the sting.
- Nettle leaves also contain acidic substances.

---

# 7. Strength of Acids and Bases

### Strong Acids

These ionize completely in water.

Examples:

- HCl
- H₂SO₄
- HNO₃

---

### Weak Acids

These ionize partially.

Examples:

- CH₃COOH
- H₂CO₃

---

### Strong Bases

- NaOH
- KOH

---

### Weak Bases

- NH₄OH
- Mg(OH)₂

# 8. Preparation of Sodium Hydroxide (Chlor-Alkali Process)

Sodium hydroxide is prepared by the **electrolysis of brine (aqueous sodium chloride solution).**

### Chemical Equation

```
2NaCl + 2H₂O

Electrolysis

→ 2NaOH + Cl₂ + H₂
```

### Products Formed

- Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)
- Chlorine gas (Cl₂)
- Hydrogen gas (H₂)

The process is called the **Chlor-alkali Process** because chlorine and alkali (NaOH) are produced together.

---

# 9. Uses of Sodium Hydroxide

- Manufacture of soaps and detergents.
- Paper industry.
- Textile industry.
- Artificial fibres.
- Cleaning agents.
- Petroleum refining.

---

# 10. Bleaching Powder

### Chemical Formula

```
CaOCl₂
```

### Preparation

Bleaching powder is prepared by passing chlorine gas over dry slaked lime.

### Chemical Equation

```
Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O
```

### Properties

- White powder.
- Has a strong smell of chlorine.
- Acts as a bleaching and disinfecting agent.

### Uses

- Disinfecting drinking water.
- Bleaching cotton and linen.
- Bleaching wood pulp.
- Oxidizing agent.
- Sanitizing swimming pools.

---

# 11. Baking Soda

### Chemical Formula

```
NaHCO₃
```

### Preparation

Prepared by the Solvay process.

### Properties

- White crystalline solid.
- Mild base.
- Produces carbon dioxide on heating.

### Reaction on Heating

```
2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O
```

### Uses

- Baking powder.
- Antacid.
- Soda-acid fire extinguishers.
- Making soft and fluffy cakes.

---

# 12. Washing Soda

### Chemical Formula

```
Na₂CO₃·10H₂O
```

### Preparation

```
Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O
```

### Uses

- Cleaning agent.
- Softening hard water.
- Glass industry.
- Soap and paper industries.

---

# 13. Plaster of Paris (POP)

### Chemical Formula

```
CaSO₄·½H₂O
```

### Preparation

Prepared by heating gypsum.

```
CaSO₄·2H₂O

Heat

→ CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O
```

---

### Properties

- White powder.
- Sets into a hard mass on mixing with water.

### Setting Reaction

```
CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O

↓

CaSO₄·2H₂O
```

---

### Uses

- Making statues.
- Surgical casts.
- Decorative materials.
- False ceilings.
- Blackboard chalk.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **strong acids** with **concentrated acids**.
- Assuming all bases are alkalis (only water-soluble bases are alkalis).
- Forgetting that acids produce **H⁺ ions only in aqueous solution**.
- Mixing up the formulas of **baking soda (NaHCO₃)** and **washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O)**.
- Confusing **gypsum** with **Plaster of Paris**.
- Writing incorrect products for neutralization reactions.
- Assuming a lower pH means a stronger base (it indicates a stronger acid).

# Chapter Summary

- **Acids** produce **H⁺ ions**, while **bases** produce **OH⁻ ions** in aqueous solutions.
- Acids react with **metals**, **metal carbonates**, and **metal hydrogen carbonates**, producing characteristic products such as hydrogen gas and carbon dioxide.
- A **neutralization reaction** produces **salt and water**.
- The **pH scale (0–14)** indicates whether a solution is acidic, neutral, or basic.
- The pH of substances plays an important role in **digestion, tooth decay, soil treatment,** and **biological processes**.
- **Sodium hydroxide** is manufactured by the **Chlor-alkali process** through the electrolysis of brine.
- **Bleaching powder** is widely used for bleaching and disinfecting.

# Introduction

Elements are broadly classified into **metals** and **non-metals** based on their physical and chemical properties. Metals are generally strong, lustrous, malleable, and good conductors of heat and electricity, while non-metals usually have opposite properties. This chapter explains the properties of metals and non-metals, the **reactivity series**, **ionic compounds**, **metallurgical processes**, and methods of **preventing corrosion**.

---

# 1. Metals

**Metals** are elements that readily lose electrons to form **positive ions (cations)**.

### Examples

- Iron (Fe)
- Copper (Cu)
- Aluminium (Al)
- Zinc (Zn)
- Sodium (Na)
- Magnesium (Mg)
- Silver (Ag)
- Gold (Au)

---

# 2. Physical Properties of Metals

| Property           | Description                               |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| Lustrous           | Shiny surface                             |
| Malleable          | Can be beaten into thin sheets            |
| Ductile            | Can be drawn into wires                   |
| Sonorous           | Produce sound when struck                 |
| Conductors         | Good conductors of heat and electricity   |
| Hard               | Most metals are hard and strong           |
| High Melting Point | Most have high melting and boiling points |

### Exceptions

- **Mercury (Hg)** is a liquid metal at room temperature.
- **Sodium (Na)** and **Potassium (K)** are soft and can be cut with a knife.
- **Gallium (Ga)** and **Caesium (Cs)** have low melting points.

---

# 3. Physical Properties of Non-Metals

| Property          | Description                                               |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Dull              | Non-lustrous (except iodine)                              |
| Brittle           | Break easily when hammered                                |
| Non-sonorous      | Do not produce ringing sound                              |
| Poor Conductors   | Poor conductors of heat and electricity (except graphite) |
| Low Density       | Generally lighter than metals                             |
| Low Melting Point | Most have low melting and boiling points                  |

### Exceptions

- **Graphite** conducts electricity.
- **Diamond** is extremely hard.
- **Iodine** is lustrous.

---

# 4. Chemical Properties of Metals

## (A) Reaction with Oxygen

Metals react with oxygen to form **metal oxides**.

### General Equation

```
Metal + Oxygen → Metal Oxide
```

### Example

```
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
```

Metal oxides are generally **basic** in nature.

---

## (B) Amphoteric Oxides

Some metal oxides react with both acids and bases.

Examples:

- Aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃)
- Zinc oxide (ZnO)

### Example

```
ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O
```

```
ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O
```

---

## (C) Reaction with Water

Different metals react differently with water.

| Metal     | Reaction with Water          |
| --------- | ---------------------------- |
| Potassium | Reacts violently             |
| Sodium    | Reacts vigorously            |
| Calcium   | Reacts readily               |
| Magnesium | Reacts slowly with hot water |
| Iron      | Reacts with steam            |
| Copper    | No reaction                  |

### Example

```
2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂↑
```

---

## (D) Reaction with Acids

Metals react with dilute acids to form **salt** and **hydrogen gas**.

### General Equation

```
Metal + Acid → Salt + Hydrogen
```

### Example

```
Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂↑
```

**Note:** Copper, silver, gold, and platinum do not react with dilute acids.

---

## (E) Reaction with Metal Salt Solutions

A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution.

### Example

```
Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
```

Observation:

- Blue colour of copper sulphate fades.
- Copper is deposited.

---

# 5. Chemical Properties of Non-Metals

Non-metals generally gain electrons to form **negative ions (anions)**.

### Reaction with Oxygen

Non-metals form **acidic** or **neutral oxides**.

### Example

```
C + O₂ → CO₂
```

Carbon dioxide is an acidic oxide.

---

### Reaction with Hydrogen

Some non-metals combine with hydrogen to form covalent compounds.

Example

```
H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl
```

---

The **reactivity series** is the arrangement of metals in decreasing order of their reactivity.

| Magnesium (Mg)   |

| Iron (Fe)        |
| Lead (Pb)        |
| Hydrogen (H)     |
| Copper (Cu)      |

### Uses of the Reactivity Series

- Predict displacement reactions.
- Determine extraction methods.
- Compare metal reactivity.

---

# 7. Displacement Reactions

A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its compound.

### Example

```
Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu
```

Since iron is more reactive than copper, it displaces copper.

---

# 8. Ionic Compounds

**Ionic compounds** are formed by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal.

### Example

Formation of sodium chloride

```
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
```

```
Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
```

```
Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl
```

## Properties of Ionic Compounds

# Introduction

**Carbon** is one of the most important elements found in nature. It is present in all living organisms and forms a vast number of compounds because of its unique properties such as **tetravalency** and **catenation**. Carbon compounds are widely used as fuels, medicines, plastics, fibres, detergents, and many other useful materials. This chapter explains the properties of carbon, covalent bonding, homologous series, important reactions of carbon compounds, and the cleansing action of soaps and detergents.

---

# 1. Carbon

**Carbon (C)** is a non-metal with atomic number **6**.

### Electronic Configuration

```
2, 4
```

Carbon has **4 valence electrons**, so it is **tetravalent**.

---

# 2. Covalent Bonding

Carbon neither loses nor gains four electrons easily because doing so requires a large amount of energy. Instead, it **shares electrons** with other atoms to complete its outermost shell.

A **covalent bond** is formed by the **sharing of electrons** between atoms.

### Example: Hydrogen Molecule

```
H : H
```

Each hydrogen atom shares one electron.

---

### Example: Chlorine Molecule

```
Cl : Cl
```

Each chlorine atom shares one electron.

---

### Example: Methane (CH₄)

```
        H
        |
    H - C - H
        |
        H
```

Carbon shares one electron with each hydrogen atom, forming four covalent bonds.

---

# 3. Versatile Nature of Carbon

Carbon forms a large number of compounds because of two important properties.

## (A) Tetravalency

Carbon has **four valence electrons** and forms four covalent bonds.

Example:

```
CH₄
```

---

## (B) Catenation

**Catenation** is the property of carbon atoms to bond with one another to form long chains, branched chains, and rings.

Examples

```
C-C-C-C
```

```
C=C
```

```
C≡C
```

This ability gives rise to millions of carbon compounds.

---

# 4. Allotropes of Carbon

**Allotropes** are different forms of the same element having different structures and properties.

### Diamond

- Hardest natural substance.
- Each carbon atom is bonded to four other carbon atoms.
- Poor conductor of electricity.

---

### Graphite

- Soft and slippery.
- Carbon atoms are arranged in layers.
- Good conductor of electricity.

---

### Fullerenes

- Molecules made of carbon atoms arranged in hollow spherical structures.
- Example: Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀).

---

# 5. Saturated and Unsaturated Carbon Compounds

## Saturated Compounds

Contain only **single covalent bonds**.

Example:

```
CH₄
```

```
C₂H₆
```

These are called **alkanes**.

---

## Unsaturated Compounds

Contain one or more **double** or **triple bonds**.

### Alkenes

Contain at least one double bond.

Example:

```
C₂H₄
```

---

### Alkynes

Contain at least one triple bond.

Example:

```
C₂H₂
```

---

# 6. Homologous Series

A **homologous series** is a group of organic compounds having:

- The same functional group.
- Similar chemical properties.
- Successive members differing by **–CH₂–** (14 u).

# Chapter Summary

- **Life processes** include **nutrition, respiration, transportation,** and **excretion**, all of which are essential for survival.
- **Autotrophs** prepare food by **photosynthesis**, while **heterotrophs** obtain food from other organisms.
- The **human digestive system** breaks down food into simple nutrients, with most digestion and absorption occurring in the **small intestine**.
- **Respiration** releases energy in the form of **ATP**, and may be **aerobic** or **anaerobic** depending on the availability of oxygen.
- The **human circulatory system** consists of the heart, blood, and blood vessels, and follows the principle of **double circulation**.
- In plants, **xylem** transports water and minerals, while **phloem** transports food. **Transpiration** helps in the upward movement of water.
- The **human excretory system** removes metabolic wastes through the kidneys, where **nephrons** filter blood and form urine.
- Proper functioning of these life processes ensures growth, repair, maintenance, and survival of living organisms.

# Introduction

**Heredity** is the process by which characteristics are passed from parents to their offspring. These inherited characteristics are controlled by **genes**, which are present on **chromosomes**. Variations that arise during reproduction make individuals different from one another and play an important role in **evolution**. This chapter explains heredity, Mendel's experiments, inheritance of traits, sex determination, and the significance of variation.

---

# 1. Heredity

**Heredity** is the transmission of genetic traits from parents to offspring.

### Importance of Heredity

- Maintains continuity of species.
- Passes genetic information to the next generation.
- Determines inherited characteristics.
- Helps in the study of evolution.

---

# 2. Variation

**Variation** refers to the differences in characteristics among individuals of the same species.

### Causes of Variation

- Sexual reproduction.
- Crossing over during meiosis.
- Mutation.
- Environmental influences.

### Importance of Variation

- Helps organisms adapt to changing environments.
- Increases the chances of survival.
- Forms the basis of evolution.

---

# 3. Genetics

**Genetics** is the branch of biology that deals with the study of heredity and variation.

The scientist known as the **Father of Genetics** is **Gregor Johann Mendel**.

---

# 4. Mendel's Experiments

Mendel conducted experiments on **pea plants (Pisum sativum)** because they:

- Have distinct contrasting traits.
- Have a short life cycle.
- Produce many seeds.
- Can self-pollinate as well as cross-pollinate.

# 5. Important Genetic Terms

| Term            | Definition                                                         |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Gene            | Unit of heredity controlling a specific trait.                     |
| Chromosome      | Thread-like structure carrying genes.                              |
| DNA             | Deoxyribonucleic Acid; hereditary material of living organisms.    |
| Trait           | A characteristic feature of an organism.                           |
| Allele          | Alternative form of a gene.                                        |

# 6. Monohybrid Cross

A **monohybrid cross** involves the inheritance of a single pair of contrasting traits.

### Example

Tall pea plant (**TT**) × Dwarf pea plant (**tt**)

### Parental Generation (P)

```
TT × tt
```

### Gametes

```
T     T

t     t
```

### F₁ Generation

```
All Tt
```

All plants are **Tall** because tallness is dominant.

---

## Self-Pollination of F₁ Generation

```
Tt × Tt
```

### Punnett Square

|       | T   | t   |
| ----- | --- | --- |
| **T** | TT  | Tt  |
| **t** | Tt  | tt  |

---

### Genotypic Ratio

```
1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt
```

---

### Phenotypic Ratio

```
3 Tall : 1 Dwarf
```

---

# 7. Law of Dominance

**Law of Dominance**

When two contrasting traits are present together, only one trait (dominant) is expressed, while the other (recessive) remains hidden.

Example:

Tall (T) is dominant over dwarf (t).

---

# 8. Law of Segregation

During gamete formation, the two alleles of a gene separate so that each gamete receives only one allele.

This is also called the **Law of Purity of Gametes**.

---

# 9. Dihybrid Cross

A **dihybrid cross** studies the inheritance of two pairs of contrasting traits simultaneously.

### Example

Round Yellow Seeds

×

Wrinkled Green Seeds

Mendel observed the **9 : 3 : 3 : 1** phenotypic ratio in the F₂ generation.

### Phenotypic Ratio

```
9 : 3 : 3 : 1
```

---

# 10. Law of Independent Assortment

The inheritance of one pair of traits is independent of the inheritance of another pair.

This law explains why new combinations of traits appear in offspring.

# Introduction

**Light** is a form of energy that enables us to see objects. It travels in a straight line and exhibits phenomena such as **reflection** and **refraction**. Reflection is the bouncing back of light from a surface, while refraction is the bending of light when it passes from one medium to another. This chapter explains the laws of reflection, spherical mirrors, refraction through lenses, image formation, and related numerical concepts.

---

# 1. Light

**Light** is a form of electromagnetic energy that produces the sensation of vision.

### Characteristics of Light

- Travels in a straight line.
- Travels fastest in vacuum.
- Undergoes reflection and refraction.
- Does not require a material medium for propagation.

### Speed of Light

In vacuum,

```
c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s
```

**SI Unit of Speed:** metre per second (m/s)

---

# 2. Reflection of Light

**Reflection** is the bouncing back of light into the same medium after striking a surface.

---

## Laws of Reflection

### First Law

The **incident ray**, **reflected ray**, and the **normal** at the point of incidence lie in the same plane.

---

### Second Law

The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.

```
∠i = ∠r
```

---

### Reflection from a Plane Mirror

```
          Reflected Ray
               /
              /
             /
------------|------------- Mirror
            |
            | Normal
            |
           /
          /
 Incident Ray
```

---

# 3. Spherical Mirrors

A **spherical mirror** is a mirror whose reflecting surface forms part of a sphere.

### Types

- Concave Mirror
- Convex Mirror

---

## Concave Mirror

A **concave mirror** has its reflecting surface curved inward.

### Characteristics

- Converging mirror.
- Can form real or virtual images.
- Produces magnified images for nearby objects.

### Uses

- Shaving mirrors.
- Dentist's mirrors.
- Solar furnaces.
- Vehicle headlights.

---

## Convex Mirror

A **convex mirror** has its reflecting surface curved outward.

### Characteristics

- Diverging mirror.
- Always forms virtual, erect, and diminished images.
- Provides a wider field of view.

### Uses

- Rear-view mirrors in vehicles.
- Security mirrors in shops.
- Road intersections.

---

# 4. Important Terms Related to Mirrors

| Term                    | Definition                                                            |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pole (P)                | Centre of the mirror's reflecting surface.                            |
| Centre of Curvature (C) | Centre of the sphere of which the mirror is a part.                   |
| Radius of Curvature (R) | Distance between P and C.                                             |
| Principal Axis          | Straight line passing through P and C.                                |
| Principal Focus (F)     | Point where parallel rays meet or appear to diverge after reflection. |
| Focal Length (f)        | Distance between P and F.                                             |

---

## Relation Between Radius of Curvature and Focal Length

```
R = 2f
```

or

```
f = R/2
```

---

# 5. Image Formation by Concave Mirror

| Position of Object | Position of Image | Nature of Image                   |
| ------------------ | ----------------- | --------------------------------- |
| At infinity        | At F              | Real, inverted, highly diminished |
| Beyond C           | Between C and F   | Real, inverted, diminished        |
| At C               | At C              | Real, inverted, same size         |
| Between C and F    | Beyond C          | Real, inverted, magnified         |
| At F               | At infinity       | Real, inverted, highly enlarged   |
| Between F and P    | Behind mirror     | Virtual, erect, magnified         |

---

# 6. Image Formation by Convex Mirror

| Position of Object | Position of Image                 | Nature of Image            |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Anywhere           | Between P and F behind the mirror | Virtual, erect, diminished |

---

# 7. Mirror Formula

For spherical mirrors,

```
1/f = 1/v + 1/u
```

Where:

- **f** = Focal length
- **u** = Object distance
- **v** = Image distance

**SI Unit:** metre (m)

---

# 8. Magnification by Mirrors

Magnification is the ratio of the height of the image to the height of the object.

### Formula

```
m = hᵢ / hₒ
```

Also,

```
m = -v/u
```

Where:

- **m** = Magnification
- **hᵢ** = Height of image
- **hₒ** = Height of object

---

# 9. Sign Convention for Mirrors

According to the **New Cartesian Sign Convention**:

- All distances are measured from the pole.
- Distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive.
- Distances measured opposite to the direction of incident light are negative.
- Heights above the principal axis are positive.
- Heights below the principal axis are negative.

---

# 10. Refraction of Light

**Refraction** is the bending of light when it passes from one transparent medium to another due to a change in its speed.

---

## Laws of Refraction (Snell's Laws)

### First Law

The incident ray, refracted ray, and the normal lie in the same plane.

---

### Second Law

```
n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r
```

Where:

- **n₁** = Refractive index of first medium
- **n₂** = Refractive index of second medium

---

# 11. Refractive Index

The **refractive index** of a medium is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in that medium.

### Formula

```
n = c/v
```

Where:

- **n** = Refractive index
- **c** = Speed of light in vacuum
- **v** = Speed of light in the medium

---

# 12. Lenses

A **lens** is a transparent optical device bounded by two refracting surfaces.

---

## Types of Lenses

### Convex Lens

A **convex lens** is thicker at the centre than at the edges.

### Characteristics

- Converging lens.
- Can produce real or virtual images.

### Uses

- Magnifying glass.
- Camera.
- Microscope.
- Human eye correction (hypermetropia).

---

### Concave Lens

A **concave lens** is thinner at the centre than at the edges.

### Characteristics

- Diverging lens.
- Always forms virtual, erect, and diminished images.

### Uses

- Correcting myopia.
- Door viewers.

---

# 13. Important Terms Related to Lenses

| Term                | Definition                                                              |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Optical Centre (O)  | Central point of the lens through which light passes without deviation. |
| Principal Focus (F) | Point where parallel rays meet or appear to diverge after refraction.   |
| Focal Length (f)    | Distance between the optical centre and the principal focus.            |
| Principal Axis      | Straight line passing through the optical centre and principal foci.    |

---

# 14. Image Formation by Convex Lens

| Position of Object | Position of Image  | Nature of Image                   |
| ------------------ | ------------------ | --------------------------------- |
| At infinity        | At F₂              | Real, inverted, highly diminished |
| Beyond 2F₁         | Between F₂ and 2F₂ | Real, inverted, diminished        |
| At 2F₁             | At 2F₂             | Real, inverted, same size         |
| Between F₁ and 2F₁ | Beyond 2F₂         | Real, inverted, magnified         |
| At F₁              | At infinity        | Real, inverted, highly enlarged   |
| Between F₁ and O   | Same side of lens  | Virtual, erect, magnified         |

---

# 15. Image Formation by Concave Lens

| Position of Object | Position of Image                | Nature of Image            |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Anywhere           | Between O and F on the same side | Virtual, erect, diminished |

---

# 16. Lens Formula

For spherical lenses,

```
1/f = 1/v − 1/u
```

Where:

- **f** = Focal length
- **u** = Object distance
- **v** = Image distance

**SI Unit:** metre (m)

---

# 17. Magnification by Lenses

### Formula

```
m = hᵢ / hₒ
```

Also,

```
m = v/u
```

# 18. Power of a Lens

The **power** of a lens is the ability of the lens to converge or diverge light rays.

### Formula

```
P = 1/f
```

Where:

- **P** = Power (in dioptre)
- **f** = Focal length (in metre)

### SI Unit

```
Dioptre (D)
```

### Notes

- Convex lens → Positive power.
- Concave lens → Negative power.

**Atmospheric refraction** is the bending of light as it passes through different layers of the Earth's atmosphere.

- Air molecules scatter shorter wavelengths of light more effectively.
- Blue light is scattered much more than red light.

# 17. Reddish Appearance of the Sun

## During Sunrise and Sunset

The Sun appears reddish because:

- Sunlight travels a longer distance through the atmosphere.
- Most of the blue light is scattered away.
- Red light reaches our eyes.

- **Myopia** is corrected using a **concave lens**, **hypermetropia** using a **convex lens**, and **presbyopia** using suitable corrective lenses such as bifocals.
- **Dispersion** of white light through a prism produces the seven colours of the visible spectrum (**VIBGYOR**).
- A **rainbow** is formed due to **refraction, dispersion, internal reflection,** and **refraction** of sunlight in water droplets.
- **Atmospheric refraction** causes the **twinkling of stars** and the **advanced sunrise and delayed sunset**.
- The **blue colour of the sky** and the **reddish appearance of the Sun** during sunrise and sunset are explained by the **scattering of light** in the Earth's atmosphere.

# Introduction

Electricity and magnetism are closely related. Whenever an electric current flows through a conductor, it produces a **magnetic field** around it. This principle is used in devices such as **electric motors, generators, loudspeakers,** and **electromagnets**. This chapter explains magnetic fields, the magnetic effect of electric current, Fleming's rules, the working of electric motors and generators, and domestic electric safety.

# 1. Magnet

A **magnet** is a material that attracts magnetic substances such as iron, nickel, and cobalt.

### Properties of a Magnet

- Has two poles: **North (N)** and **South (S)**.
- Like poles repel each other.
- Unlike poles attract each other.
- Freely suspended magnet aligns itself in the north-south direction.

---

# 2. Magnetic Field

A **magnetic field** is the region around a magnet or a current-carrying conductor where its magnetic effect can be experienced.

### Representation

Magnetic fields are represented by **magnetic field lines**.

---

## Characteristics of Magnetic Field Lines

- Originate from the north pole and enter the south pole outside the magnet.
- Form closed continuous curves.
- Never intersect each other.
- Closer field lines indicate a stronger magnetic field.

---

### Magnetic Field Around a Bar Magnet

```
       N -------- S

   → → → → → → →

  Curved magnetic
     field lines
```

# 3. Magnetic Field Due to a Current-Carrying Straight Conductor

When electric current passes through a straight conductor, concentric circular magnetic field lines are produced around it.

### Diagram

```
  ⭕ ⭕ ⭕ ⭕

Current coming
out of the page
```

(⊙ indicates current coming out of the page.)

# 4. Right-Hand Thumb Rule

The **Right-Hand Thumb Rule** is used to determine the direction of the magnetic field around a current-carrying conductor.

### Statement

If the conductor is held in the **right hand** with the **thumb pointing in the direction of current**, then the curled fingers indicate the direction of the magnetic field.

---

### Diagram

```

↑ Current

Curled Fingers

↺ Magnetic Field
```

# 5. Magnetic Field Due to a Circular Current Loop

When current flows through a circular loop:

- Magnetic field lines are circular near the wire.
- At the centre, the magnetic field becomes nearly straight and parallel.
- The strength of the magnetic field increases with the number of turns.

---

### Factors Affecting Magnetic Field Strength

- Magnitude of current.
- Number of turns.
- Radius of the loop.

# 6. Solenoid

A **solenoid** is a long cylindrical coil made of many turns of insulated copper wire.

---

### Magnetic Field of a Solenoid

A current-carrying solenoid behaves like a **bar magnet**.

## Characteristics

- Produces a strong and nearly uniform magnetic field inside.
- Magnetic field is weak outside.
- One end behaves as the north pole and the other as the south pole.

# 7. Electromagnet

An **electromagnet** is a temporary magnet produced by passing electric current through a solenoid wound around a soft iron core.

## Advantages

```
Middle Finger ↓ Current

Thumb ← Force
```

## Methods of Producing Induced Current

- Vegetable waste
- Paper
- Cotton
- Food waste
- Wood

## Non-Biodegradable Substances

# 10. Ozone Layer

The **ozone layer** is a layer of ozone gas present in the **stratosphere**.

### Importance

- Absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV) rays from the Sun.
- Protects living organisms from skin cancer, cataracts, and DNA damage.

---

# 11. Ozone Depletion

**Ozone depletion** is the thinning of the ozone layer.

### Causes

- Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)
- Halons
- Nitrogen oxides

---

## Harmful Effects

- Increased UV radiation.
- Skin cancer.
- Eye cataracts.
- Reduced crop productivity.
- Damage to marine organisms.

---

## Prevention

- Reduce the use of CFCs.
- Use eco-friendly refrigerants.
- Follow international environmental agreements.
- Promote sustainable technologies.

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Who are the producers in an ecosystem?

**Solution**

Green plants and algae are producers because they prepare their own food by photosynthesis.

---

### Example 2

Define a food chain.

**Solution**

A food chain is a linear sequence of organisms through which food and energy pass from one trophic level to the next.

---

### Example 3

State the 10% Law.

**Solution**

Only about **10%** of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level, while the remaining energy is used for life processes or lost as heat.

---

### Example 4

Differentiate between biodegradable and non-biodegradable substances.

**Solution**

- **Biodegradable substances** are decomposed by microorganisms.
- **Non-biodegradable substances** persist in the environment because they do not decompose easily.

---

### Example 5

Why are decomposers important?

**Solution**

They break down dead organic matter, recycle nutrients, improve soil fertility, and help maintain ecological balance.

---

### Example 6

What causes ozone depletion?

**Solution**

The major cause of ozone depletion is the release of **chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)** and other ozone-depleting substances into the atmosphere.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **food chain** with **food web**.
- Mixing up **producers**, **consumers**, and **decomposers**.
- Assuming energy is recycled in an ecosystem (energy flows in one direction; nutrients are recycled).
- Confusing **biodegradable** and **non-biodegradable** wastes.
- Forgetting that only **10%** of energy is transferred to the next trophic level.
- Assuming the ozone layer protects against all types of radiation (it mainly absorbs harmful **ultraviolet (UV)** rays).
- Confusing **biological magnification** with decomposition.
- Assuming plastics are biodegradable.

# Chapter Summary

- The **environment** consists of **biotic** and **abiotic** components that interact within an **ecosystem**.
- Living organisms are classified into **producers, consumers,** and **decomposers**, each playing an essential role in maintaining ecological balance.
- A **food chain** represents a single pathway of energy transfer, while a **food web** shows multiple interconnected feeding relationships.
- According to the **10% Law**, only about **10%** of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next.
- **Biological magnification** leads to the accumulation of harmful non-biodegradable substances such as pesticides at higher trophic levels.
- **Biodegradable wastes** decompose naturally, whereas **non-biodegradable wastes** persist in the environment and contribute to pollution.
- The **ozone layer** protects life on Earth by absorbing harmful **ultraviolet (UV)** radiation, while **ozone depletion** caused by substances such as **CFCs** increases health and environmental risks.
- Effective **waste management**, following the **3Rs (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle)** and adopting **sustainable development** practices, helps conserve natural resources and protect the environment for future generations.
