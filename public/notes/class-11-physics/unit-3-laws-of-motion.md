# Introduction

The laws of motion explain the relationship between force and the motion of objects.

Sir Isaac Newton formulated three fundamental laws that form the foundation of classical mechanics.

This unit covers:

- Force and inertia
- Newton's laws of motion
- Momentum
- Impulse
- Conservation of momentum
- Friction
- Circular motion
- Banking of roads

Applications:

- Vehicle motion
- Sports mechanics
- Machines
- Engineering systems

---

# PART A: FORCE AND INERTIA

---

# 1. Force

## Definition

A force is an external agent that changes or tends to change the state of rest or motion of a body.

---

Force can change:

- Speed
- Direction
- Shape

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{Newton(N)}
\]

---

# 2. Effects of Force

A force can:

1. Start motion
2. Stop motion
3. Change velocity
4. Change direction
5. Change shape

---

# 3. Inertia

## Definition

The tendency of a body to resist any change in its state of rest or motion is called inertia.

---

Inertia depends on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Mass of the body}}
\]

Greater mass:

\[
\Rightarrow
\]

Greater inertia

---

# Types of Inertia

---

# 1. Inertia of Rest

A body at rest tends to remain at rest.

Example:

A passenger falls backward when a bus starts suddenly.

---

# 2. Inertia of Motion

A moving body tends to continue moving.

Example:

A passenger falls forward when a moving bus stops suddenly.

---

# 3. Inertia of Direction

A body resists change in direction.

Example:

Dust removed from a carpet by beating.

---

# 4. Momentum

## Definition

The quantity of motion possessed by a body is called momentum.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
p=mv
}
\]

where:

- m = mass
- v = velocity

---

Unit:

\[
kg\,m/s
\]

---

Momentum is a vector quantity.

---

# PART B: NEWTON'S LAWS OF MOTION

---

# 5. Newton's First Law

## Statement

A body continues in its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force.

---

This law explains:

\[
\boxed{\text{Inertia}}
\]

---

Mathematical form:

If:

\[
F=0
\]

then:

\[
v=\text{constant}
\]

---

# 6. Newton's Second Law

## Statement

The rate of change of momentum is directly proportional to the applied force and occurs in the direction of force.

---

Momentum:

\[
p=mv
\]

Change in momentum:

\[
\Delta p=m(v-u)
\]

Rate of change:

\[
\frac{\Delta p}{t}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
F=ma
}
\]

---

# 7. Newton's Third Law

## Statement

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

---

Mathematically:

\[
\boxed{
F_{12}=-F_{21}
}
\]

---

Examples:

- Rocket propulsion
- Walking
- Swimming
- Recoil of gun

---

# PART C: IMPULSE AND MOMENTUM

---

# 8. Impulse

## Definition

The product of force and time interval for which it acts is called impulse.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
J=F\Delta t
}
\]

---

Impulse is equal to change in momentum:

\[
\boxed{
J=\Delta p
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
Ns
\]

---

# 9. Conservation of Linear Momentum

## Principle

When no external force acts on a system, total momentum remains constant.

---

Before collision:

\[
m_1u_1+m_2u_2
\]

After collision:

\[
m_1v_1+m_2v_2
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
m_1u_1+m_2u_2
=

m_1v_1+m_2v_2
}
\]

---

# PART D: FRICTION

---

# 10. Friction

## Definition

The force that opposes relative motion between two surfaces in contact is called friction.

---

Direction:

Opposite to motion.

---

# Types of Friction

---

# 1. Static Friction

Friction acting on a body at rest is called static friction.

---

Maximum static friction:

\[
\boxed{
f_s\leq\mu_sN
}
\]

---

# 2. Limiting Friction

The maximum value of static friction before motion begins.

\[
\boxed{
f_{max}=\mu_sN
}
\]

---

# 3. Kinetic Friction

Friction acting when a body is moving.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
f_k=\mu_kN
}
\]

---

# 11. Coefficient of Friction

Ratio of frictional force to normal reaction.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\mu=\frac FN
}
\]

---

# PART E: ANGLE OF FRICTION

---

# 12. Angle of Friction

The angle between resultant reaction and normal reaction is called angle of friction.

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
\tan\lambda=\mu
}
\]

---

# PART F: MOTION ON AN INCLINED PLANE

---

# 13. Forces on Inclined Plane

For a body of mass m:

Weight:

\[
mg
\]

Components:

Along plane:

\[
\boxed{
mg\sin\theta
}
\]

Perpendicular:

\[
\boxed{
mg\cos\theta
}
\]

---

# PART G: CIRCULAR MOTION

---

# 14. Centripetal Force

## Definition

The force required to keep an object moving in a circular path is called centripetal force.

---

Direction:

Towards centre of circle.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
F_c=\frac{mv^2}{r}
}
\]

---

Also:

\[
\boxed{
F_c=mr\omega^2
}
\]

---

# 15. Centrifugal Force

A pseudo force appearing in a rotating frame directed away from the centre is called centrifugal force.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
F=\frac{mv^2}{r}
}
\]

---

# PART H: BANKING OF ROADS

---

# 16. Banking of Roads

## Definition

Raising the outer edge of a curved road above the inner edge is called banking.

---

Purpose:

To provide centripetal force safely.

---

# 17. Banking Angle Formula

For ideal banking:

\[
\boxed{
\tan\theta=
\frac{v^2}{rg}
}
\]

where:

- v = velocity
- r = radius
- g = acceleration due to gravity

# Solved Examples

---

## Example 1

A force of 10 N acts on a body of mass 2 kg.

Find acceleration.

Given:

\[
F=10N
\]

\[
m=2kg
\]

Using:

\[
F=ma
\]

\[
a=\frac Fm
\]

\[
=\frac{10}{2}
\]

\[
\boxed{5m/s^2}
\]

---

## Example 2

Find momentum of a body of mass 5 kg moving at 4 m/s.

\[
p=mv
\]

\[
=5\times4
\]

\[
\boxed{20kg\,m/s}
\]

---

## Example 3

A car moves in a circular path with:

\[
m=1000kg
\]

\[
v=20m/s
\]

\[
r=50m
\]

Centripetal force:

\[
F=\frac{mv^2}{r}
\]

\[
=\frac{1000(400)}{50}
\]

\[
\boxed{8000N}
\]

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing mass and weight.
- Forgetting force is a vector quantity.
- Using action-reaction forces on the same body.
- Mixing static and kinetic friction.
- Forgetting friction opposes motion.
- Using centrifugal force as a real force in inertial frames.
- Forgetting centripetal force is not a new type of force; it is the net inward force.

- Friction opposes relative motion.
- Circular motion requires centripetal force.
