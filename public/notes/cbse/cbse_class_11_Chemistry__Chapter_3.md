# Introduction

As the number of known elements increased, scientists needed a systematic method to organize them based on their properties. This led to the development of the **Periodic Table**, where elements are arranged so that similar elements occur together.

The **Modern Periodic Table** is based on the **Modern Periodic Law**, which relates the properties of elements to their **atomic numbers** rather than their atomic masses.

---

# 1. Need for Classification of Elements

Classification helps to:

- Study a large number of elements systematically.
- Predict the properties of unknown elements.
- Understand similarities and differences among elements.
- Correlate electronic configuration with chemical properties.

---

# 2. Early Attempts at Classification

---

## (A) Döbereiner's Triads

### Proposed by

**Johann Wolfgang Döbereiner (1829)**

### Features

- Elements were grouped in threes.
- Middle element had an atomic mass approximately equal to the average of the other two.
- Members showed similar properties.

### Example

```
Li      Na      K

7       23      39

(7 + 39)/2 = 23
```

### Limitations

- Applicable to only a few elements.
- Most known elements could not be grouped.

---

## (B) Newlands' Law of Octaves

### Proposed by

**John Newlands (1865)**

### Statement

When elements are arranged in increasing order of atomic mass, every eighth element has properties similar to the first.

```
1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8

↓

Similar properties
```

### Merits

- First systematic attempt.
- Showed periodic repetition of properties.

### Limitations

- Valid only up to calcium.
- Left no gaps for undiscovered elements.
- Failed for heavier elements.

---

## (C) Mendeleev's Periodic Table

### Proposed by

**Dmitri Mendeleev (1869)**

### Basis

- Atomic mass.
- Similar chemical properties.

---

## Mendeleev's Periodic Law

"The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic masses."

---

## Merits

- Left gaps for undiscovered elements.
- Predicted properties of unknown elements.
- Corrected atomic masses of several elements.

---

## Limitations

- Position of hydrogen uncertain.
- Isotopes could not be accommodated.
- Anomalous pairs (Ar-K, Co-Ni, Te-I).

---

# 3. Modern Periodic Law

### Proposed by

**Henry Moseley (1913)**

### Statement

"The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers."

# 6. Periodic Trends

Periodic properties arise due to:

- Effective nuclear charge.
- Atomic size.
- Shielding effect.

---

# 7. Atomic Radius

## Definition

Atomic radius is the distance from the nucleus to the outermost electron shell.

---

## Types of Atomic Radius

### Covalent Radius

Distance between nuclei of two bonded atoms of the same element.

---

### Metallic Radius

Half the distance between nuclei of adjacent metal atoms.

---

### van der Waals Radius

Half the distance between nuclei of two non-bonded atoms.

---

## Trend Across a Period

```
Decreases
→
```

Reason

- Increase in effective nuclear charge.
- Electrons are pulled closer to the nucleus.

---

## Trend Down a Group

```
Increases
↓

```

Reason

- Addition of new shells.
- Increased shielding effect.

---

# 8. Ionic Radius

### Cations

Radius decreases.

Example

```
Na > Na⁺
```

---

### Anions

Radius increases.

Example

```
Cl < Cl⁻
```

---

## Isoelectronic Species

For species having the same number of electrons:

```
Radius decreases as nuclear charge increases.
```

Example

```
O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺
```

# 9. Ionization Enthalpy

## Definition

Energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom.

```
M(g)

↓

M⁺(g) + e⁻
```

---

## Trend Across a Period

```
Increases
→
```

Reason

- Atomic size decreases.
- Effective nuclear charge increases.

---

## Trend Down a Group

```
Decreases
↓

```

Reason

- Atomic size increases.
- Shielding effect increases.

---

## Factors Affecting Ionization Enthalpy

- Atomic size.
- Nuclear charge.
- Shielding effect.
- Electronic configuration.

---

## Important Anomalies

### Be > B

Reason

- B has one electron in higher-energy 2p orbital.

---

### N > O

Reason

- Nitrogen has stable half-filled p³ configuration.

---

# 10. Electron Gain Enthalpy

## Definition

Energy released when an electron is added to an isolated gaseous atom.

```
X(g) + e⁻

↓

X⁻(g)
```

---

## Trend Across a Period

Generally becomes more negative.

```
→
```

---

## Trend Down a Group

Generally becomes less negative.

```
↓

```

---

## Important Exceptions

- Fluorine has less negative electron gain enthalpy than chlorine due to its very small size and higher electron-electron repulsion.
- Noble gases have positive or near-zero electron gain enthalpy due to their stable electronic configurations.
- Beryllium, magnesium, and nitrogen have relatively low electron gain enthalpies because of stable filled or half-filled subshells.

# 16. Diagonal Relationship

Certain elements of the second period resemble elements diagonally placed in the third period.

Examples

- Li ↔ Mg
- Be ↔ Al
- B ↔ Si

Reason

- Similar ionic size.
- Similar charge/radius ratio.
- Comparable electronegativity.

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Arrange the following in increasing order of atomic radius:

```
Na, Mg, Al
```

**Solution**

All three elements belong to Period 3.

Across a period, atomic radius decreases due to increasing effective nuclear charge.

Therefore,

```
Al < Mg < Na
```

---

### Example 2

Which has a higher ionization enthalpy: Be or B?

**Solution**

Although ionization enthalpy generally increases across a period, **beryllium has a completely filled 2s² subshell**, making it more stable than boron (2s²2p¹).

Therefore,

```
Be > B
```

---

### Example 3

Arrange the following isoelectronic species in decreasing order of ionic radius:

```
O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺
```

**Solution**

All have 10 electrons.

Greater nuclear charge leads to a smaller ionic radius.

Hence,

```
O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺
```

---

### Example 4

Why is fluorine more electronegative than chlorine?

**Solution**

Fluorine has a smaller atomic size and higher effective nuclear charge, allowing it to attract the shared electron pair more strongly than chlorine.

---

### Example 5

State the trend of metallic character across a period.

**Solution**

Metallic character decreases from left to right because atoms hold their valence electrons more strongly and are less likely to lose them.

---

### Example 6

Why does chlorine have a more negative electron gain enthalpy than fluorine?

**Solution**

Although fluorine is more electronegative, its very small atomic size causes greater electron-electron repulsion when an additional electron enters the compact 2p orbital. In chlorine, the larger 3p orbital accommodates the incoming electron more easily, making its electron gain enthalpy more negative.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **atomic radius** with **ionic radius**.
- Assuming all periodic trends have no exceptions; **Be–B** and **N–O** are important anomalies in ionization enthalpy.
- Mixing up **electron gain enthalpy** with **electronegativity**; the former is an energy change, whereas the latter is a relative tendency in a chemical bond.
- Forgetting that **fluorine** does not have the most negative electron gain enthalpy; **chlorine** does.
- Assuming **hydrogen** fits perfectly into one group; its position remains unique due to its dual characteristics.
- Ignoring the role of **effective nuclear charge** and **shielding effect** in explaining periodic trends.
- Confusing **periods** with **groups**.
- Assuming all oxides are either acidic or basic; some, such as **Al₂O₃**, are **amphoteric**.

# Chapter Summary

- The classification of elements evolved through **Döbereiner's Triads**, **Newlands' Law of Octaves**, **Mendeleev's Periodic Table**, and finally the **Modern Periodic Table**.
- The **Modern Periodic Law**, proposed by **Henry Moseley**, states that the properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.
- The modern periodic table consists of **7 periods**, **18 groups**, and four blocks: **s**, **p**, **d**, and **f**.
- **Atomic radius** decreases across a period and increases down a group due to changes in effective nuclear charge and shielding.
- **Ionization enthalpy** generally increases across a period and decreases down a group, with important anomalies for **Be/B** and **N/O**.
- **Electron gain enthalpy** generally becomes more negative across a period, while **electronegativity** increases from left to right and decreases down a group.
- **Metallic character** decreases across a period and increases down a group, whereas **non-metallic character** shows the opposite trend.
- **Diagonal relationships** exist between certain second- and third-period elements because of similarities in size, charge density, and electronegativity.
- Periodic trends can be explained using **effective nuclear charge**, **shielding effect**, **atomic size**, and **electronic configuration**, enabling the prediction of the chemical behaviour of elements.
