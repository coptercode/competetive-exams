# Introduction

**Chemical kinetics** is the branch of chemistry that deals with the **rate of chemical reactions** and the factors affecting them.

It helps us understand:

- How fast a reaction occurs.
- Why reaction rates differ.
- How reaction rates can be controlled.

---

# 1. Rate of a Chemical Reaction

## Definition

The change in concentration of reactants or products per unit time is called the rate of reaction.

---

For a reaction:

\[
A \rightarrow B
\]

Rate:

\[
Rate=-\frac{\Delta[A]}{\Delta t}
\]

or

\[
Rate=\frac{\Delta[B]}{\Delta t}
\]

---

## Unit of Rate

\[
mol\,L^{-1}s^{-1}
\]

---

# 2. Average Rate of Reaction

## Definition

Rate measured over a finite time interval.

---

Formula:

\[
Average\ Rate=
-\frac{\Delta R}{\Delta t}
\]

where:

- ΔR = change in concentration of reactant
- Δt = time interval

---

# Example

If concentration decreases from 0.8 M to 0.2 M in 10 seconds:

\[
Rate=
-\frac{0.2-0.8}{10}
\]

\[
=\frac{0.6}{10}
\]

\[
=0.06\,mol\,L^{-1}s^{-1}
\]

---

# 3. Instantaneous Rate

## Definition

Rate of reaction at a particular instant.

---

Mathematically:

\[
Rate=-\frac{d[R]}{dt}
\]

---

# 4. Factors Affecting Rate of Reaction

---

## A. Concentration of Reactants

Higher concentration:

- More collisions.
- Faster reaction.

---

## B. Temperature

Increase in temperature:

- Increases kinetic energy.
- Increases effective collisions.

---

## C. Catalyst

A catalyst:

- Increases reaction rate.
- Lowers activation energy.
- Remains chemically unchanged.

---

## D. Surface Area

Powdered solids react faster than large pieces.

---

## E. Nature of Reactants

Ionic reactions are generally faster than covalent reactions.

---

# 5. Rate Law

## Definition

The mathematical relationship between reaction rate and reactant concentration.

---

For:

\[
aA+bB\rightarrow Products
\]

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k[A]^m[B]^n
\]

where:

- k = rate constant
- m,n = experimentally determined powers

---

# 6. Rate Constant (k)

## Definition

The rate of reaction when concentration of all reactants is unity.

---

## Factors Affecting k

Depends on:

- Temperature
- Catalyst
- Nature of reactants

Does not depend on concentration.

---

# 7. Order of Reaction

## Definition

Sum of powers of concentration terms in rate law.

---

For:

\[
Rate=k[A]^m[B]^n
\]

Order:

\[
=m+n
\]

---

# Types of Order

## Zero Order

Rate independent of concentration.

\[
Rate=k
\]

---

## First Order

Rate depends on one concentration term.

\[
Rate=k[A]
\]

---

## Second Order

Rate depends on square of concentration or two concentration terms.

\[
Rate=k[A]^2
\]

---

# Order vs Molecularity

| Order                       | Molecularity               |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Experimental                | Theoretical                |
| Can be zero or fractional   | Always whole number        |
| Applies to overall reaction | Applies to elementary step |

---

# 8. Molecularity

## Definition

Number of reacting species participating in an elementary reaction.

---

Types:

### Unimolecular

One molecule reacts.

Example:

\[
A\rightarrow Products
\]

---

### Bimolecular

Two molecules react.

Example:

\[
A+B\rightarrow Products
\]

---

### Termolecular

Three molecules react.

Rare.

---

# 9. Integrated Rate Equations

---

# A. Zero Order Reaction

## Rate Law

\[
Rate=k
\]

---

## Integrated Equation

\[
[R]=[R]_0-kt
\]

---

where:

- [R]₀ = initial concentration
- [R] = concentration after time t

---

## Half-Life

\[
t_{1/2}=\frac{[R]_0}{2k}
\]

---

## Graph

```
[R]

|

|\
| \
|  \
|___\________ t
```

---

# B. First Order Reaction

## Rate Law

\[
Rate=k[R]
\]

---

## Integrated Equation

\[
k=\frac{2.303}{t}
\log\frac{[R]_0}{[R]}
\]

---

## Half-Life

\[
t_{1/2}=
\frac{0.693}{k}
\]

---

## Important Point

For first order reaction:

Half-life is independent of initial concentration.

---

## Graph

```
ln[R]

|

|\
| \
|  \
|___\_______ t
```

---

# 10. Half-Life of Reactions

## Definition

Time required for concentration of reactant to become half of its initial value.

---

# Comparison

| Zero Order                  | First Order    |
| --------------------------- | -------------- |
| Rate = k                    | Rate = k[R]    |
| t½ depends on concentration | t½ independent |
| Unit of k: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹      | Unit of k: s⁻¹ |

---

# 11. Arrhenius Equation

## Definition

Shows relationship between rate constant and temperature.

---

Equation:

\[
k=Ae^{-E_a/RT}
\]

---

Taking logarithm:

\[
\log k=
\log A-
\frac{E_a}{2.303RT}
\]

---

where:

- A = frequency factor
- Ea = activation energy
- R = gas constant
- T = temperature

---

# 12. Activation Energy

## Definition

Minimum energy required by reactant molecules to undergo reaction.

---

## Effect of Catalyst

Catalyst provides an alternate pathway with lower activation energy.

---

## Diagram

```
Energy

 |
 |        Without catalyst
 |       /\
 |      /  \
 |_____/    \____

 |    With catalyst
 |   /\
 |__/  \_________

 ---------------- Reaction
```

---

# 13. Collision Theory

## Basic Idea

A reaction occurs when molecules collide effectively.

---

# Effective Collision Requires:

## 1. Sufficient Energy

Collision energy must be equal to or greater than activation energy.

---

## 2. Proper Orientation

Molecules must collide in correct arrangement.

---

# Collision Theory Diagram

```
Molecules

   A → ← B

Collision

↓

Activated Complex

↓

Products
```

---

# 14. Effect of Temperature on Reaction Rate

According to Arrhenius equation:

Increase in temperature:

- Increases number of energetic molecules.
- Increases effective collisions.
- Increases rate constant.

---

# 15. Catalyst

## Definition

A substance that changes reaction rate without being consumed.

---

## Types

### Positive Catalyst

Increases rate.

---

### Negative Catalyst

Decreases rate.

---

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** A reaction has rate law:

\[
Rate=k[A]^2[B]
\]

Find order.

### Solution

Order:

\[
=2+1
\]

\[
\boxed{Order=3}
\]

---

## Example 2

**Question:** Calculate half-life of a first-order reaction when k = 0.693 s⁻¹.

### Solution

\[
t_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{k}
\]

\[
=\frac{0.693}{0.693}
\]

\[
\boxed{1s}
\]

---

## Example 3

**Question:** Why does temperature increase reaction rate?

### Solution

Higher temperature increases kinetic energy of molecules. More molecules acquire energy greater than activation energy, increasing effective collisions and reaction rate.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** What is activation energy?

### Solution

Activation energy is the minimum energy required for reactant molecules to form an activated complex and undergo chemical transformation.

---

## Example 5

**Question:** Differentiate order and molecularity.

### Solution

Order is experimentally determined from rate law and may be zero or fractional. Molecularity refers to the number of reacting species in an elementary step and is always a whole number.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing order with molecularity; they are not always the same.
- Forgetting that order is determined experimentally.
- Using the first-order half-life equation for zero-order reactions.
- Ignoring units of rate constant for different orders.
- Assuming catalysts increase equilibrium constant; catalysts only change reaction rate.
- Forgetting that effective collisions require both sufficient energy and proper orientation.
- Mixing up activation energy with heat of reaction.
- Forgetting that half-life of a first-order reaction is independent of initial concentration.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Chemical kinetics studies reaction rates and factors affecting them.
- Reaction rate depends on concentration, temperature, surface area, catalysts, and reactant nature.
- Rate law gives the relationship between reaction rate and reactant concentration.
- Order of reaction is determined experimentally, while molecularity applies to elementary reactions.
- Zero-order and first-order reactions have specific integrated rate equations and half-life expressions.
- The Arrhenius equation explains the temperature dependence of reaction rates.
- Activation energy is the minimum energy required for successful reaction.
- Collision theory explains reactions through effective molecular collisions.
- Catalysts increase reaction rate by lowering activation energy without being consumed.
