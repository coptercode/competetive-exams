# Introduction

**Evolution** is the gradual process by which living organisms change over generations, leading to the formation of new species. It explains the diversity of life on Earth through changes in inherited characteristics over long periods. Modern evolutionary biology integrates genetics, ecology, paleontology, and molecular biology to explain how populations evolve.

---

# 1. Origin of Life

## Definition

The **origin of life** refers to the process by which the first living organisms appeared on Earth.

---

## Theories of Origin of Life

### A. Theory of Special Creation

- Proposes that all living organisms were created by a supernatural power.
- Does not provide a scientific explanation.

---

### B. Theory of Spontaneous Generation

- Suggested that life arose spontaneously from non-living matter.
- Disproved by **Louis Pasteur**.

---

### C. Theory of Biogenesis

- States that life arises only from pre-existing life.

---

### D. Chemical Evolution Theory

Proposed by:

- **A.I. Oparin**
- **J.B.S. Haldane**

According to this theory:

- Early Earth had a reducing atmosphere.
- Organic molecules formed from inorganic compounds.
- Complex molecules gradually evolved into the first living cells.

---

# 2. Miller-Urey Experiment (1953)

## Objective

To test the theory of chemical evolution.

---

## Apparatus

The experiment used a closed system containing:

- Methane (CH₄)
- Ammonia (NH₃)
- Hydrogen (H₂)
- Water vapour (H₂O)

Electric sparks simulated lightning.

---

## Observation

Organic compounds such as amino acids were formed.

---

## Conclusion

Simple organic molecules can be synthesized from inorganic substances under conditions similar to those of early Earth.

---

# 3. Evidences for Evolution

## A. Paleontological Evidence

Based on **fossils**.

### Fossils

Remains or impressions of ancient organisms preserved in rocks.

---

### Importance

- Show extinct organisms.
- Reveal evolutionary changes.
- Help determine geological age.

---

## B. Comparative Anatomy

### Homologous Organs

Structures having:

- Common origin
- Different functions

Examples:

- Human arm
- Whale flipper
- Bat wing

Indicate **divergent evolution**.

---

### Analogous Organs

Structures having:

- Different origin
- Similar function

Examples:

- Wings of birds
- Wings of insects

Indicate **convergent evolution**.

---

### Comparison

| Homologous Organs   | Analogous Organs     |
| ------------------- | -------------------- |
| Same origin         | Different origin     |
| Different functions | Same function        |
| Divergent evolution | Convergent evolution |

---

## C. Vestigial Organs

Reduced, non-functional structures inherited from ancestors.

Examples:

- Human appendix
- Coccyx
- Wisdom teeth

---

## D. Embryological Evidence

Early embryos of vertebrates show similarities, suggesting common ancestry.

---

## E. Molecular Evidence

Comparison of:

- DNA sequences
- Protein sequences

Greater similarity indicates closer evolutionary relationships.

---

# 4. Darwin's Theory of Natural Selection

Proposed by:

**Charles Darwin**

Based on observations during the voyage of **HMS Beagle**.

---

## Main Principles

1. Overproduction
2. Variation
3. Struggle for existence
4. Survival of the fittest
5. Natural selection
6. Origin of new species

---

## Survival of the Fittest

Individuals possessing favourable variations are more likely to survive and reproduce.

---

# 5. Modern Synthetic Theory of Evolution

- Darwin's natural selection

- Population genetics

---

## Sources of Variation

# 6. Adaptive Radiation

## Definition

Evolution of different species from a common ancestor to occupy different ecological niches.

---

## Example

Darwin's finches of the Galápagos Islands.

Different beak shapes evolved according to available food sources.

---

## Other Example

Australian marsupials.

---

# 7. Mechanisms of Evolution

## A. Mutation

Sudden heritable change in genetic material.

---

### Types

- Gene mutation
- Chromosomal mutation

---

### Importance

- Creates new alleles.
- Source of genetic variation.

---

## B. Gene Flow

Movement of genes between populations through migration and interbreeding.

---

## C. Genetic Drift

Random change in allele frequency, especially in small populations.

---

### Founder Effect

Occurs when a small group establishes a new population.

---

### Bottleneck Effect

Occurs when population size is drastically reduced due to disasters or other events.

---

## D. Recombination

Formation of new gene combinations during meiosis.

Occurs through:

- Crossing over
- Independent assortment
- Random fertilization

---

## E. Natural Selection

Acts on variations, favoring individuals better adapted to the environment.

---

# 8. Hardy-Weinberg Principle

## Statement

In a large, randomly mating population, allele and genotype frequencies remain constant from generation to generation unless evolutionary forces act upon them.

---

## Equation

```
p² + 2pq + q² = 1
```

Where:

- **p** = frequency of dominant allele
- **q** = frequency of recessive allele

And:

```
p + q = 1
```

---

## Genotype Frequencies

```
p²

↓

Homozygous Dominant
```

```
2pq

↓

Heterozygous
```

```
q²

↓

Homozygous Recessive
```

---

## Conditions for Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

- No mutation
- No migration (gene flow)
- No natural selection
- Random mating
- Very large population
- No genetic drift

---

## Factors Disturbing Equilibrium

- Mutation
- Gene migration
- Genetic drift
- Recombination
- Natural selection

---

# 9. Types of Natural Selection

## A. Stabilizing Selection

- Intermediate phenotype favored.
- Variation decreases.

---

## B. Directional Selection

- One extreme phenotype favored.
- Population shifts toward one extreme.

---

## C. Disruptive Selection

- Both extreme phenotypes favored.
- Intermediate phenotype selected against.

---

# 10. Industrial Melanism

## Example

Peppered moth (_Biston betularia_)

---

### Before Industrialization

- Light-colored moths survived better on lichen-covered trees.

---

### After Industrialization

- Tree trunks darkened due to soot.
- Dark-colored moths survived better.

---

## Significance

Demonstrates natural selection in action.

---

# 11. Antibiotic Resistance

Random mutations may produce bacteria resistant to antibiotics.

Improper or excessive antibiotic use increases the survival of resistant strains through natural selection.

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

**Question:** Why are homologous organs considered evidence of evolution?

**Solution:**

Homologous organs have the same basic structural plan and common evolutionary origin but perform different functions. Their presence indicates that different species evolved from a common ancestor through divergent evolution.

---

### Example 2

**Question:** State the significance of the Miller-Urey experiment.

**Solution:**

The experiment demonstrated that organic molecules such as amino acids could be synthesized from simple inorganic substances under conditions resembling those of early Earth, providing experimental support for the theory of chemical evolution.

---

### Example 3

**Question:** What is adaptive radiation? Give one example.

**Solution:**

Adaptive radiation is the evolution of multiple species from a common ancestor, each adapted to a different ecological niche. Darwin's finches of the Galápagos Islands are a classic example.

---

### Example 4

**Question:** Write the Hardy-Weinberg equation.

**Solution:**

The Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium is expressed as:

```
p² + 2pq + q² = 1
```

where:

- **p²** = frequency of homozygous dominant genotype
- **2pq** = frequency of heterozygous genotype
- **q²** = frequency of homozygous recessive genotype

---

### Example 5

**Question:** Explain industrial melanism.

**Solution:**

Industrial melanism is the increase in frequency of dark-colored individuals in polluted environments. In the peppered moth, dark moths became more common after industrialization because they were better camouflaged against soot-covered trees, illustrating natural selection.

---

### Example 6

**Question:** Mention two mechanisms that produce genetic variation.

**Solution:**

Two important mechanisms are:

- **Mutation**, which creates new alleles.
- **Recombination**, which generates new combinations of existing alleles during meiosis.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **homologous organs** with **analogous organs**.
- Assuming Darwin proposed the inheritance of acquired characters; this concept was proposed by **Lamarck**.
- Forgetting that the **Hardy-Weinberg principle** applies only under ideal conditions with no evolutionary forces acting.
- Mixing up **gene flow** and **genetic drift**; gene flow results from migration, while genetic drift is due to random chance.
- Assuming all mutations are harmful; many are neutral, and some may be beneficial.
- Confusing **adaptive radiation** with **convergent evolution**.
- Believing evolution occurs in individuals; **evolution occurs in populations over generations**.
- Memorizing the sequence of human ancestors without understanding the major evolutionary trends, such as increased brain size and bipedalism.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Evolution** explains the gradual change in populations over time, leading to biological diversity.
- The **chemical evolution theory** proposed by Oparin and Haldane, supported by the **Miller-Urey experiment**, provides a scientific explanation for the origin of life.
- Fossils, homologous and analogous organs, embryology, and molecular studies provide strong evidence for evolution.
- **Darwin's theory of natural selection** states that individuals with favorable heritable variations are more likely to survive and reproduce.
- The **modern synthetic theory** integrates natural selection with genetics and recognizes mutation, recombination, gene flow, and genetic drift as sources of variation.
- The **Hardy-Weinberg principle** describes genetic equilibrium in populations and identifies factors responsible for evolutionary change.
- **Adaptive radiation** and **natural selection** explain how organisms diversify and adapt to different environments.
- **Industrial melanism** and **antibiotic resistance** are important examples of evolution occurring in observable time scales.
- Human evolution involved progressive changes such as increased cranial capacity, upright posture, and advanced cognitive abilities, culminating in modern **Homo sapiens**.
