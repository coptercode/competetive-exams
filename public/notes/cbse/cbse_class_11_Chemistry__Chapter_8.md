# Introduction

Organic chemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the study of **carbon compounds**, except a few simple compounds such as carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide (CO₂), carbonates, bicarbonates, cyanides, and carbides.

Carbon forms millions of compounds because of its unique properties such as **catenation**, **tetravalency**, and the ability to form stable covalent bonds.

Organic chemistry is important in the study of:

- Fuels
- Medicines
- Plastics
- Dyes
- Polymers
- Biomolecules

---

# 1. Characteristics of Carbon

Carbon exhibits unique properties due to:

- Tetravalency
- Catenation
- Ability to form single, double, and triple bonds
- Small atomic size
- Strong covalent bonding

---

## Tetravalency

Carbon has four valence electrons.

```
Electronic Configuration

1s² 2s² 2p²
```

Hence, it forms four covalent bonds.

---

## Catenation

Definition:

The ability of carbon atoms to form long chains by bonding with one another.

Examples:

```
C-C-C-C

C=C-C

C≡C
```

# 5. Homologous Series

- Same functional group.
- Same general formula.

Example

# 9. Electronic Displacement Effects

These effects explain the behavior and reactivity of organic molecules.

---

## (A) Inductive Effect (I-Effect)

Definition:

Permanent displacement of sigma (σ) electrons due to electronegativity differences.

---

### +I Effect

Electron-releasing groups.

Examples

- Alkyl groups

```
CH₃ >

C₂H₅ >

C₃H₇
```

---

### –I Effect

Electron-withdrawing groups.

Examples

- NO₂
- CN
- COOH
- F
- Cl

---

## Applications

- Stability of carbocations.
- Acid strength.
- Base strength.

---

# 10. Electromeric Effect (E-Effect)

Definition

Temporary complete transfer of π-electrons under the influence of an attacking reagent.

Occurs only in compounds containing multiple bonds.

---

### Types

Positive Electromeric Effect (+E)

Electron pair shifts toward attacking electrophile.

---

Negative Electromeric Effect (−E)

Electron pair shifts away from attacking nucleophile.

---

# 11. Resonance (Mesomeric Effect)

Definition

A molecule represented by two or more valid Lewis structures differing only in the position of electrons.

Example

```
Benzene

↔

Equivalent Structures
```

---

## Resonance Hybrid

Actual molecule is a resonance hybrid.

Characteristics

- More stable.
- Equal bond lengths.
- Delocalized electrons.

---

# 12. Hyperconjugation

Definition

Delocalization of σ-electrons of C–H bonds adjacent to a multiple bond or positively charged carbon.

Also called

```
No-Bond Resonance
```

---

## Applications

- Stability of carbocations.
- Stability of alkenes.
- Orientation in aromatic substitution.

# 14. Homolytic and Heterolytic Cleavage

---

## Homolytic Cleavage

Bond breaks equally.

```
A:B

↓

A• + B•
```

Produces free radicals.

---

## Heterolytic Cleavage

Bond breaks unequally.

```
A:B

↓

A⁺ + B⁻
```

Produces ions.

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Name the compound

```
CH₃CH₂CH₂OH
```

**Solution**

Longest chain contains three carbon atoms.

Functional group:

```
–OH
```

Hence,

```
Propan-1-ol
```

---

### Example 2

Identify the functional group present in

```
CH₃COOH
```

**Solution**

The functional group is

```
–COOH
```

Hence, it is a **carboxylic acid**.

---

### Example 3

Arrange the stability of carbocations.

**Solution**

Stability order:

```
3°

>

2°

>

1°

>

CH₃⁺
```

Reason:

Greater hyperconjugation and +I effect stabilize higher alkyl-substituted carbocations.

---

### Example 4

Differentiate between electrophiles and nucleophiles.

**Solution**

Electrophiles are electron-deficient species that accept an electron pair (e.g., H⁺, BF₃), whereas nucleophiles are electron-rich species that donate an electron pair (e.g., OH⁻, NH₃).

---

### Example 5

Which method is used for estimating nitrogen in nitro compounds?

**Solution**

**Kjeldahl's method** cannot estimate nitrogen in nitro compounds.

Therefore,

```
Dumas Method
```

is used.

---

### Example 6

State the products formed during homolytic cleavage of

```
Cl₂
```

**Solution**

The Cl–Cl bond breaks equally.

```
Cl₂

↓

Cl•

+

Cl•
```

Two chlorine free radicals are produced.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **functional groups** with substituents.
- Incorrectly selecting the **longest carbon chain** during IUPAC naming.
- Forgetting to give the **lowest possible number** to the principal functional group.
- Mixing up the **inductive effect** (permanent σ-electron displacement) with the **electromeric effect** (temporary π-electron displacement).
- Confusing **resonance** with **hyperconjugation**.
- Assuming **homolytic cleavage** produces ions; it produces **free radicals**.
- Reversing the roles of **electrophiles** and **nucleophiles**.
- Forgetting that **Kjeldahl's method** cannot estimate nitrogen in **nitro**, **azo**, and some other nitrogen-containing compounds.

# Chapter Summary

- Organic chemistry deals primarily with the chemistry of **carbon compounds**, whose diversity arises from **tetravalency** and **catenation**.
- Organic compounds are classified as **acyclic**, **cyclic**, **alicyclic**, **aromatic**, and **heterocyclic**, and are identified by their **functional groups**.
- The **IUPAC system** provides a systematic method for naming organic compounds.
- **Electronic displacement effects**—including the **inductive effect**, **electromeric effect**, **resonance**, and **hyperconjugation**—help explain the stability and reactivity of organic molecules.
- Organic reactions proceed through intermediates such as **carbocations**, **carbanions**, and **free radicals**, which are formed by **heterolytic** or **homolytic** bond cleavage.
- Chemical reactions involve the attack of **electrophiles** and **nucleophiles**, leading to **substitution**, **addition**, **elimination**, or **rearrangement** reactions.
- Organic compounds are purified using methods such as **crystallization**, **distillation**, **sublimation**, **chromatography**, and **extraction**.
- The elemental composition of organic compounds is determined using qualitative tests such as **Lassaigne's test** and quantitative methods including the **Kjeldahl**, **Dumas**, and **Carius** methods.
- These principles form the foundation for understanding the chemistry of hydrocarbons and other organic compounds in subsequent chapters.
