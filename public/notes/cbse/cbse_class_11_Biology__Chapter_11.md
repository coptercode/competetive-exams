# Introduction

Plants require a continuous supply of **water, minerals, and food** for growth and survival. These substances are transported through specialized tissues called **xylem** and **phloem**. Water and minerals are absorbed by the roots and transported to different parts of the plant, while food prepared during photosynthesis is distributed to all living cells.

This chapter explains the mechanisms of **diffusion, osmosis, plasmolysis, water absorption, transpiration, ascent of sap, mineral absorption, and translocation of food**.

---

# 1. Means of Transport

Plants transport substances through the following processes:

```
Transport Processes
│
├── Diffusion
├── Facilitated Diffusion
├── Osmosis
├── Active Transport
└── Bulk Flow
```

---

# 2. Diffusion

Diffusion is the movement of molecules from a region of **higher concentration** to a region of **lower concentration** until equilibrium is reached.

## Characteristics

- Passive process.
- No energy required.
- Occurs in gases, liquids, and dissolved substances.
- Continues until equilibrium.

---

## Examples

- Exchange of gases through stomata.
- Movement of oxygen and carbon dioxide.

---

# 3. Facilitated Diffusion

Facilitated diffusion is the movement of molecules across a membrane with the help of **transport proteins**.

## Characteristics

- Passive process.
- No ATP required.
- Uses carrier or channel proteins.
- Highly specific.

---

## Examples

- Movement of glucose.
- Transport of certain ions through membrane channels.

---

# 4. Osmosis

Osmosis is the movement of water through a **selectively permeable membrane** from a region of **higher water potential** to a region of **lower water potential**.

---

## Conditions Necessary

- Presence of a selectively permeable membrane.
- Difference in water potential.
- Presence of water molecules.

---

# 5. Types of Solutions

## (A) Hypotonic Solution

- Higher water potential than the cell.
- Water enters the cell.
- Cell becomes **turgid**.

---

## (B) Hypertonic Solution

- Lower water potential than the cell.
- Water leaves the cell.
- Cell undergoes **plasmolysis**.

---

## (C) Isotonic Solution

- Equal water potential inside and outside the cell.
- No net movement of water.

---

# Comparison of Solutions

| Solution   | Water Movement  | Effect on Cell |
| ---------- | --------------- | -------------- |
| Hypotonic  | Into the cell   | Turgid         |
| Hypertonic | Out of the cell | Plasmolysed    |
| Isotonic   | No net movement | Unchanged      |

---

# 6. Plasmolysis

Plasmolysis is the shrinkage of the protoplast away from the cell wall due to loss of water by osmosis.

---

## Types

### Incipient Plasmolysis

Beginning stage of plasmolysis.

---

### Complete Plasmolysis

Maximum shrinkage of the protoplast.

---

## Deplasmolysis

The recovery of a plasmolysed cell when placed in a hypotonic solution.

---

# 7. Water Potential (Ψ)

Water potential is the tendency of water to move from one region to another.

Pure water has the highest water potential.

```
Ψ = Ψs + Ψp
```

Where

- **Ψ** = Water potential
- **Ψs** = Solute potential
- **Ψp** = Pressure potential

---

# 8. Imbibition

Imbibition is the absorption of water by **hydrophilic colloids**, causing them to swell.

---

## Examples

- Dry seeds absorbing water.
- Swelling of wooden doors during rainy season.

# 10. Pathways of Water Movement

---

## (A) Apoplast Pathway

- Water moves through cell walls and intercellular spaces.
- Faster pathway.
- No plasma membrane crossed.

---

## (B) Symplast Pathway

- Water moves through cytoplasm.
- Connected by plasmodesmata.
- Selective pathway.

---

## Comparison

| Apoplast      | Symplast           |
| ------------- | ------------------ |
| Cell walls    | Cytoplasm          |
| Fast          | Comparatively slow |
| Non-selective | Selective          |

---

# 11. Transpiration

Transpiration is the loss of water in the form of water vapour from the aerial parts of plants.

---

## Types

### Stomatal Transpiration

Occurs through stomata.

Accounts for about **90–95%** of total transpiration.

---

### Cuticular Transpiration

Occurs through the cuticle.

---

### Lenticular Transpiration

Occurs through lenticels.

---

# 12. Mechanism of Stomatal Opening and Closing

### Opening

- Guard cells absorb water.
- Become turgid.
- Stomatal pore opens.

---

### Closing

- Guard cells lose water.
- Become flaccid.
- Stomatal pore closes.

---

# 13. Factors Affecting Transpiration

## External Factors

- Temperature
- Humidity
- Wind
- Light
- Atmospheric pressure

---

## Internal Factors

- Number of stomata
- Leaf area
- Thickness of cuticle
- Water availability

---

# 14. Advantages of Transpiration

- Produces transpiration pull.
- Helps in mineral transport.
- Maintains leaf temperature.
- Removes excess water.

---

# 15. Ascent of Sap

The upward movement of water and dissolved minerals from roots to leaves is called **ascent of sap**.

Occurs through:

```
Xylem
```

---

## Cohesion-Tension Theory

Proposed by

**Dixon and Joly**

### Principle

- Cohesion between water molecules.
- Adhesion between water and xylem walls.
- Transpiration pull generates tension, lifting water upward.

---

# 16. Mineral Nutrition and Active Absorption

Mineral ions are absorbed by roots through:

### Passive Absorption

- No ATP required.
- Along concentration gradient.

---

### Active Absorption

- ATP required.
- Against concentration gradient.
- Carrier proteins involved.

---

# 17. Translocation of Food

Food synthesized in leaves is transported to other plant parts.

Occurs through:

```
Phloem
```

---

## Pressure Flow (Mass Flow) Hypothesis

Proposed by

**Ernst Münch**

### Mechanism

- Sugars are actively loaded into sieve tubes.
- Water enters by osmosis.
- High pressure develops at the source.
- Food moves toward the sink.
- Sugars are unloaded at the sink.

---

## Source and Sink

### Source

Region where food is produced.

Example

- Leaves

---

### Sink

Region where food is utilized or stored.

Examples

- Roots
- Fruits
- Seeds
- Growing buds

---

# 18. Guttation

Guttation is the loss of liquid water from leaf margins through **hydathodes**.

Occurs:

- Early morning.
- When transpiration is low and root pressure is high.

---

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Differentiate between diffusion and osmosis.

**Solution**

Diffusion is the movement of molecules from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration and does not necessarily require a membrane. Osmosis is the movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane from higher to lower water potential.

---

### Example 2

Why does plasmolysis occur?

**Solution**

Plasmolysis occurs when a plant cell is placed in a hypertonic solution, causing water to move out of the cell by osmosis. The protoplast shrinks away from the cell wall.

---

### Example 3

State the significance of transpiration.

**Solution**

Transpiration helps in generating transpiration pull, transporting minerals, cooling the plant, and maintaining water balance.

---

### Example 4

Explain the Cohesion-Tension Theory.

**Solution**

According to Dixon and Joly, water molecules remain connected by cohesion and adhere to xylem walls. Transpiration from leaves creates a pulling force (transpiration pull), which lifts the continuous column of water upward through the xylem.

---

### Example 5

What is the Pressure Flow Hypothesis?

**Solution**

The Pressure Flow Hypothesis, proposed by Ernst Münch, explains that sugars are actively loaded into phloem at the source, creating high osmotic pressure. This drives the movement of food solution toward sink regions where sugars are unloaded.

---

### Example 6

Differentiate between transpiration and guttation.

**Solution**

Transpiration is the loss of water as vapour through stomata, whereas guttation is the loss of liquid water through hydathodes, usually during early morning when root pressure is high.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **diffusion** with **osmosis**.
- Assuming osmosis can occur without a selectively permeable membrane.
- Mixing up **plasmolysis** and **deplasmolysis**.
- Confusing the **apoplast** and **symplast** pathways.
- Forgetting that **stomatal transpiration** accounts for the majority of water loss.
- Mixing up **transpiration** with **guttation**.
- Assuming food transport occurs through xylem; food is transported by **phloem**.
- Forgetting that the **Cohesion-Tension Theory** was proposed by **Dixon and Joly**, while the **Pressure Flow Hypothesis** was proposed by **Ernst Münch**.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Plants transport substances through **diffusion**, **facilitated diffusion**, **osmosis**, **active transport**, and **bulk flow**.
- **Osmosis** is the movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane from higher to lower water potential, leading to conditions such as **turgidity**, **plasmolysis**, and **deplasmolysis**.
- Water is absorbed mainly by **root hairs** and moves through the **apoplast** and **symplast** pathways before entering the xylem.
- **Transpiration** is the loss of water vapour from aerial plant parts and is essential for cooling, mineral transport, and generating **transpiration pull**.
- According to the **Cohesion-Tension Theory**, water ascends through the xylem due to cohesion, adhesion, and the tension created by transpiration.
- Mineral ions are absorbed either passively or actively, depending on the concentration gradient and energy requirement.
- Food synthesized in leaves is translocated through the **phloem** according to the **Pressure Flow Hypothesis**, moving from **source** to **sink**.
- **Guttation** is the exudation of liquid water through hydathodes under conditions of high root pressure.
- Efficient transport systems ensure the distribution of water, minerals, and food necessary for plant growth, metabolism, and survival.
