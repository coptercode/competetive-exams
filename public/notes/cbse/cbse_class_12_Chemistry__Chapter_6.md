# Introduction

Organic compounds containing one or more halogen atoms (F, Cl, Br, I) attached to carbon atoms are called **halo compounds**.

General formula:

\[
R-X
\]

where:

- R = alkyl/aryl group
- X = halogen

---

# 1. Classification of Halo Compounds

## A. Haloalkanes

Halogen attached to an sp³ hybridised carbon of an alkyl group.

Example:

\[
CH_3Cl
\]

(chloromethane)

---

## B. Haloarenes

Halogen directly attached to an aromatic ring.

Example:

\[
C_6H_5Cl
\]

(chlorobenzene)

---

# 2. Classification Based on Number of Halogen Atoms

## Monohaloalkanes

One halogen atom.

Example:

\[
CH_3CH_2Cl
\]

---

## Dihaloalkanes

Two halogen atoms.

Example:

\[
CH_2Cl_2
\]

---

## Polyhaloalkanes

More than two halogens.

Example:

\[
CHCl_3
\]

---

# 3. Classification Based on Carbon Attached to Halogen

## Primary Haloalkane (1°)

Halogen attached to carbon connected with one other carbon.

Example:

\[
CH_3CH_2Cl
\]

---

## Secondary Haloalkane (2°)

Carbon attached to two carbon atoms.

Example:

\[
CH_3CHClCH_3
\]

---

## Tertiary Haloalkane (3°)

Carbon attached to three carbon atoms.

Example:

\[
(CH_3)_3CCl
\]

---

# 4. Nomenclature of Haloalkanes

Rules:

1. Select longest carbon chain.
2. Number chain to give lowest number to substituent.
3. Halogens are named as prefixes:

| Halogen | Prefix |
| ------- | ------ |
| F       | Fluoro |
| Cl      | Chloro |
| Br      | Bromo  |
| I       | Iodo   |

---

Examples:

\[
CH_3CH_2Cl
\]

Name:

Chloroethane

---

\[
CH_3CHBrCH_3
\]

Name:

2-Bromopropane

---

# 5. Nature of C-X Bond

The carbon-halogen bond is polar.

Electronegativity:

\[
F>Cl>Br>I
\]

---

Bond strength:

\[
C-F>C-Cl>C-Br>C-I
\]

---

Reactivity:

\[
RI>RBr>RCl>RF
\]

# 6. Preparation of Haloalkanes

---

# A. From Alcohols

Alcohol reacts with halogen acids.

Example:

\[
ROH+HCl\rightarrow RCl+H_2O
\]

---

Reagents:

- HCl/ZnCl₂
- SOCl₂
- PCl₅
- PCl₃

---

# B. From Alkanes

Free radical halogenation:

\[
CH_4+Cl_2
\xrightarrow{hv}
CH_3Cl+HCl
\]

---

# C. From Alkenes

Addition of hydrogen halides.

\[
CH_2=CH_2+HCl
\rightarrow
CH_3CH_2Cl
\]

---

# D. Sandmeyer Reaction

Aryl diazonium salts react with Cu salts.

\[
ArN_2^+Cl^-+CuCl
\rightarrow
ArCl+N_2
\]

---

# 7. Physical Properties

## Boiling Point

Increases with:

- Molecular mass
- Number of halogens

---

## Solubility

Haloalkanes are:

- Insoluble in water.
- Soluble in organic solvents.

---

# 8. Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions

## Definition

A reaction in which a nucleophile replaces a leaving group.

General reaction:

\[
R-X+Nu^-
\rightarrow
R-Nu+X^-
\]

---

# Nucleophile

Electron-rich species that attacks electron-deficient carbon.

Examples:

- OH⁻
- CN⁻
- NH₃

---

# 9. SN2 Reaction

## Full Form

Substitution Nucleophilic Bimolecular

---

## Mechanism

One-step reaction.

Rate depends on:

- Haloalkane concentration
- Nucleophile concentration

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k[R-X][Nu^-]
\]

---

## Mechanism

```
Nu⁻ attacks

      H
      |
Nu⁻ → C — X
      |
     R

↓

R-Nu + X⁻
```

---

## Features

- Backside attack.
- Inversion of configuration.
- Favoured by primary haloalkanes.

---

# 10. SN1 Reaction

## Full Form

Substitution Nucleophilic Unimolecular

---

## Mechanism

Two-step process.

---

Step 1:

Formation of carbocation.

\[
R-X\rightarrow R^++X^-
\]

---

Step 2:

Attack by nucleophile.

\[
R^++Nu^-
\rightarrow R-Nu
\]

---

## Rate Law

\[
Rate=k[R-X]
\]

---

## Features

- Carbocation intermediate.
- Racemization occurs.
- Favoured by tertiary haloalkanes.

---

# Comparison: SN1 vs SN2

| SN1                      | SN2              |
| ------------------------ | ---------------- |
| Two-step                 | One-step         |
| First order              | Second order     |
| Carbocation intermediate | No intermediate  |
| Tertiary favoured        | Primary favoured |
| Racemization             | Inversion        |

---

# 11. Factors Affecting SN Reactions

## A. Nature of Alkyl Halide

SN1:

\[
3^\circ>2^\circ>1^\circ
\]

SN2:

\[
1^\circ>2^\circ>3^\circ
\]

---

## B. Leaving Group Ability

Better leaving group:

\[
I^- > Br^- > Cl^- > F^-
\]

---

## C. Nucleophile Strength

Strong nucleophiles increase SN2 rate.

---

\]

---

---

## B. Sandmeyer Reaction

Diazonium salt conversion.

# 14. Why Haloarenes are Less Reactive Than Haloalkanes

Reasons:

## 1. Resonance

C-X bond gets partial double bond character.

---

## 2. sp² Carbon

Bond becomes shorter and stronger.

---

# 19. Chirality and Optical Activity

## Chirality

A molecule is chiral if it is not superimposable on its mirror image.

---

## Chiral Carbon

Carbon attached to four different groups.

Example:

\[
CHBrClF
\]

---

# Enantiomers

Non-superimposable mirror images.

---

# Optical Activity

Ability to rotate plane-polarized light.

---

## Types

### Dextrorotatory (+)

Rotates right.

---

### Laevorotatory (-)

---

Equal mixture of two enantiomers.

Shows:


# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** Arrange haloalkanes in increasing SN1 reactivity.

### Solution

SN1 depends on carbocation stability:

\[
CH_3X<1^\circ<2^\circ<3^\circ
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ}
\]

---

## Example 2

**Question:** Why is chlorobenzene less reactive than chloroethane?

### Solution

In chlorobenzene, the C-Cl bond has partial double bond character due to resonance and is attached to sp² carbon, making it stronger and less reactive.

---

## Example 3

**Question:** What is a Grignard reagent?

### Solution

A Grignard reagent is an organomagnesium compound with formula:

\[
RMgX
\]

It is prepared by reacting haloalkane with magnesium in dry ether.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** Explain racemic mixture.

### Solution

A racemic mixture contains equal amounts of two enantiomers. Their opposite optical rotations cancel each other, making the mixture optically inactive.

---

## Example 5

**Question:** Why are halogens ortho-para directing?

### Solution

Halogens donate electron density through resonance, increasing electron density at ortho and para positions, even though they deactivate the ring due to their -I effect.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing SN1 and SN2 mechanisms.
- Forgetting that SN1 depends on carbocation stability.
- Forgetting that SN2 causes inversion of configuration.
- Assuming haloarenes react faster than haloalkanes.
- Confusing Grignard reagent with ordinary alkyl halide.
- Forgetting that Grignard reagents must be prepared in dry ether.
- Mixing up Wurtz and Wurtz-Fittig reactions.
- Forgetting that halogens are deactivating but ortho/para directing.
- Confusing chirality with optical activity.
- Forgetting environmental effects of CFCs (freons).

---

# Chapter Summary

- Haloalkanes and haloarenes are organic compounds containing halogen atoms.
- Reactivity depends on the nature of the C-X bond and structure of the carbon chain.
- Haloalkanes undergo nucleophilic substitution through **SN1** and **SN2** mechanisms.
- Haloarenes are less reactive due to resonance and stronger C-X bonds.
- Grignard reagents are important organometallic compounds used in organic synthesis.
- Wurtz and Wurtz-Fittig reactions are important coupling reactions.
- Chirality, enantiomers, and optical activity explain stereochemical behaviour.
- Polyhalogen compounds have important uses but may cause environmental problems.
