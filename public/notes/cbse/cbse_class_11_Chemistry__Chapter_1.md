# Introduction

Chemistry is the branch of science that deals with the **composition, structure, properties, and transformations of matter**. Since atoms and molecules are extremely small, chemists use measurable quantities such as the **mole** to represent large numbers of particles.

Understanding chemical reactions requires knowledge of **chemical formulae**, **balanced equations**, and quantitative relationships between reactants and products, collectively known as **stoichiometry**.

---

# 1. Matter

## Definition

Matter is anything that:

- Has mass.
- Occupies space.

Examples:

- Water
- Air
- Iron
- Wood

---

# 2. Physical and Chemical Properties

## Physical Properties

Properties that can be observed without changing the chemical composition.

Examples

- Colour
- Density
- Melting point
- Boiling point

---

## Chemical Properties

Properties observed when a substance undergoes a chemical change.

Examples

- Combustibility
- Rusting
- Reactivity with acids

---

# 3. Physical and Chemical Changes

## Physical Change

A change in which no new substance is formed.

Examples

- Melting of ice
- Boiling of water
- Breaking glass

---

## Chemical Change

A change in which new substances are formed.

Examples

- Burning of magnesium
- Rusting of iron
- Digestion of food

# 4. Laws of Chemical Combination

---

## (A) Law of Conservation of Mass

### Proposed by

**Antoine Lavoisier**

### Statement

Mass is neither created nor destroyed during a chemical reaction.

```
Total Mass of Reactants
=
Total Mass of Products
```

### Example

```
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

4 g + 32 g = 36 g
```

---

## (B) Law of Constant Proportions

### Proposed by

**Joseph Proust**

### Statement

A chemical compound always contains the same elements in the same fixed proportion by mass.

Example

Water always contains:

```
Hydrogen : Oxygen

1 : 8
```

---

## (C) Law of Multiple Proportions

### Proposed by

**John Dalton**

### Statement

When two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in simple whole-number ratios.

Example

```
CO

12 g C + 16 g O

CO₂

12 g C + 32 g O

Ratio = 16 : 32 = 1 : 2
```

---

# 5. Dalton's Atomic Theory

Main postulates:

- Matter consists of tiny particles called atoms.
- Atoms are indivisible (modified later).
- Atoms of the same element are identical.
- Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios.
- Chemical reactions involve rearrangement of atoms.

---

# 6. Atomic Mass

Atomic mass is the mass of an atom relative to **1/12th of the mass of a carbon-12 atom**.

Unit:

```
Atomic Mass Unit (u)
```

Example

| Element | Atomic Mass (u) |
| ------- | --------------- |
| H       | 1               |
| C       | 12              |
| O       | 16              |
| Na      | 23              |

---

# 7. Molecular Mass

The molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms present in a molecule.

### Formula

```
Molecular Mass

= Σ Atomic Masses
```

### Example

For H₂O

```
= (2 × 1) + 16

= 18 u
```

---

# 8. Formula Mass

Used for ionic compounds.

Example

NaCl

```
23 + 35.5

= 58.5 u
```

---

# 9. Mole Concept

## Definition

One mole is the amount of substance containing **Avogadro's number** of particles.

```
1 mole

=

6.022 × 10²³ particles
```

---

## Avogadro Constant

```
Nₐ

=

6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹
```

---

## Importance of Mole

Used to relate:

- Mass
- Number of particles
- Volume (for gases)
- Chemical equations

---

# 10. Molar Mass

The mass of one mole of a substance.

Unit

```
g mol⁻¹
```

Examples

| Substance | Molar Mass |
| --------- | ---------- |
| H₂        | 2 g mol⁻¹  |
| O₂        | 32 g mol⁻¹ |
| H₂O       | 18 g mol⁻¹ |
| CO₂       | 44 g mol⁻¹ |

# 11. Mole Relationships

## (A) Mass–Mole Relation

```
Number of Moles

=

Mass

──────

Molar Mass
```

---

## (B) Mole–Particles Relation

```
Particles

=

Moles × Nₐ
```

---

## (C) Gas Volume Relation

At STP,

```
1 mole gas

=

22.4 L
```

_(For modern IUPAC standard conditions, molar volume is approximately 22.7 L at 1 bar and 273.15 K, but NCERT Class 11 commonly uses **22.4 L at STP** in numerical problems.)_

# 13. Empirical Formula

The empirical formula gives the **simplest whole-number ratio** of atoms.

Example

```
CH₂O
```

---

# Steps to Determine Empirical Formula

1. Find percentage composition.
2. Convert percentage into moles.
3. Divide by the smallest value.
4. Convert into whole numbers.

---

# 14. Molecular Formula

The molecular formula gives the **actual number of atoms** present in a molecule.

Relationship

```
Molecular Formula

=

(Empirical Formula)n
```

where

```
n

=

Molecular Mass

──────────────

Empirical Formula Mass
```

---

# 15. Chemical Equations

A chemical equation represents a chemical reaction using symbols and formulae.

Example

```
2H₂ + O₂

↓

2H₂O
```

---

# 16. Balancing Chemical Equations

Rules

- Number of atoms must be equal on both sides.
- Use coefficients only.
- Never change subscripts.

Example

```
Unbalanced

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Balanced

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
```

---

# 17. Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry deals with the quantitative relationship between reactants and products in a chemical reaction.

Example

```
N₂ + 3H₂

↓

2NH₃
```

Meaning

```
1 mol N₂

+

3 mol H₂

↓

2 mol NH₃
```

---

# 18. Limiting Reagent

The limiting reagent is the reactant that is completely consumed first and limits the amount of product formed.

Example

```
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Available

2 mol H₂

2 mol O₂

Required ratio

2 : 1

Therefore,

Hydrogen is limiting.
```

---

# 19. Excess Reagent

The reactant left after completion of the reaction is called the excess reagent.

---

# 20. Stoichiometric Calculations

General steps

1. Write balanced equation.
2. Convert mass into moles.
3. Compare mole ratio.
4. Identify limiting reagent.
5. Calculate product.
6. Convert moles into required unit.

# Comparison Tables

## Physical Change vs Chemical Change

| Physical Change         | Chemical Change          |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------ |
| No new substance formed | New substance formed     |
| Usually reversible      | Usually irreversible     |
| Composition unchanged   | Composition changes      |
| Example: Melting of ice | Example: Rusting of iron |

---

## Empirical Formula vs Molecular Formula

| Empirical Formula                 | Molecular Formula          |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Simplest ratio                    | Actual number of atoms     |
| May not represent actual molecule | Represents actual molecule |
| Example: CH₂O                     | Example: C₆H₁₂O₆           |

---

## Atomic Mass vs Molecular Mass

| Atomic Mass       | Molecular Mass       |
| ----------------- | -------------------- |
| Mass of one atom  | Mass of one molecule |
| Used for elements | Used for compounds   |

---

## Limiting Reagent vs Excess Reagent

| Limiting Reagent          | Excess Reagent      |
| ------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Completely consumed       | Left after reaction |
| Determines product formed | Remains unreacted   |

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Calculate the number of moles in **36 g of water (H₂O).**

**Solution**

Molar mass of H₂O = 18 g mol⁻¹

```
Number of moles

= Mass / Molar Mass

= 36 / 18

= 2 mol
```

---

### Example 2

Calculate the number of molecules present in **2 mol of oxygen gas.**

**Solution**

```
Number of molecules

= 2 × 6.022 × 10²³

= 1.2044 × 10²⁴ molecules
```

---

### Example 3

Find the molecular mass of carbon dioxide (CO₂).

**Solution**

```
C = 12

O = 16

CO₂

= 12 + (2 ×16)

= 44 u
```

---

### Example 4

A compound contains **40% carbon, 6.67% hydrogen, and 53.33% oxygen**. Determine its empirical formula.

**Solution**

Assume 100 g of compound.

```
C = 40/12 = 3.33

H = 6.67/1 = 6.67

O = 53.33/16 = 3.33
```

Divide by the smallest value (3.33):

```
C : H : O

= 1 : 2 : 1
```

Empirical formula:

```
CH₂O
```

---

### Example 5

Calculate the percentage of oxygen in water.

**Solution**

```
Mass of oxygen = 16

Molar mass of water = 18

Percentage

= (16/18) ×100

= 88.89%
```

---

### Example 6

In the reaction:

```
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
```

If **4 mol H₂** reacts with **1 mol O₂**, identify the limiting reagent.

**Solution**

Required ratio:

```
2 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂
```

Available:

```
4 mol H₂ : 1 mol O₂
```

Only 2 mol H₂ are required for 1 mol O₂.

Therefore,

- **Oxygen is the limiting reagent.**
- Hydrogen is present in excess.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **atomic mass** with **molar mass**; atomic mass is expressed in **u**, while molar mass is expressed in **g mol⁻¹**.
- Using an **unbalanced chemical equation** for stoichiometric calculations.
- Changing **subscripts** instead of coefficients while balancing equations.
- Forgetting to convert **mass into moles** before using mole ratios.
- Mixing up **empirical formula** and **molecular formula**.
- Ignoring the **limiting reagent**, leading to incorrect product calculations.
- Using **22.4 L** for gases under conditions other than STP without checking the given conditions.
- Confusing **molarity** (depends on solution volume) with **molality** (depends on solvent mass).

# Chapter Summary

- Matter undergoes **physical** and **chemical** changes, and chemical reactions obey the **laws of chemical combination**: conservation of mass, constant proportions, and multiple proportions.
- **Dalton's atomic theory** explains matter in terms of atoms combining in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
- **Atomic mass**, **molecular mass**, and **formula mass** help determine the masses of atoms, molecules, and ionic compounds.
- The **mole concept** provides a bridge between microscopic particles and measurable laboratory quantities using **Avogadro's constant (6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹)**.
- Quantitative relationships between **mass**, **moles**, **particles**, and **gas volume** form the basis of chemical calculations.
- **Percentage composition**, **empirical formula**, and **molecular formula** are used to determine the composition and identity of compounds.
- A correctly **balanced chemical equation** is essential for all stoichiometric calculations.
- **Stoichiometry** enables the calculation of reactant and product amounts, while the **limiting reagent** determines the maximum amount of product that can be formed.
- Concentration terms such as **mass percentage**, **ppm**, **mole fraction**, **molarity**, and **molality** are widely used to express the composition of solutions.
