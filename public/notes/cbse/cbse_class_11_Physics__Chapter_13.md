# Introduction

Many motions in nature repeat themselves after regular intervals of time. Such motions are called **periodic motions**. Oscillatory motion is a special type of periodic motion in which a body moves repeatedly to and fro about its mean position.

Examples include the motion of a pendulum, a vibrating tuning fork, the oscillation of a spring, and the vibration of atoms in solids.

---

# 1. Periodic Motion

## Definition

A motion that repeats itself after equal intervals of time is called **periodic motion**.

---

## Examples

- Earth's revolution around the Sun
- Rotation of a fan
- Motion of clock hands
- Oscillation of a pendulum

---

# 2. Oscillatory Motion

## Definition

Oscillatory motion is the repeated to-and-fro motion of a body about its equilibrium (mean) position.

---

## Characteristics

- Repeats after equal intervals of time.
- Has a mean position.
- Restoring force acts towards the mean position.

---

# 3. Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)

## Definition

Simple Harmonic Motion is a special type of oscillatory motion in which the restoring force (or acceleration) is directly proportional to the displacement from the mean position and is always directed towards it.

Mathematically,

```
F ∝ -x
```

or

```
F = -kx
```

where

- **F** = Restoring force
- **k** = Force constant (spring constant)
- **x** = Displacement

---

## Acceleration in SHM

Using Newton's second law,

```
F = ma
```

Therefore,

```
a = -ω²x
```

where

- **ω** = Angular frequency

---

# 4. Characteristics of SHM

- Motion is periodic.
- Restoring force is proportional to displacement.
- Acceleration is maximum at the extreme positions.
- Velocity is maximum at the mean position.
- Total mechanical energy remains constant (in the absence of damping).

---

# 5. Important Terms

---

## Mean Position

The equilibrium position about which the body oscillates.

---

## Displacement

The distance of the body from the mean position.

Symbol

```
x
```

---

## Amplitude

The maximum displacement from the mean position.

Symbol

```
A
```

---

## Time Period

The time taken to complete one oscillation.

Symbol

```
T
```

Unit

```
second (s)
```

---

## Frequency

The number of oscillations completed in one second.

Symbol

```
f
```

Formula

```
f = 1/T
```

Unit

```
hertz (Hz)
```

---

## Angular Frequency

Formula

```
ω = 2πf
```

Also,

```
ω = 2π/T
```

Unit

```
rad s⁻¹
```

---

# 6. Equation of SHM

The displacement of a particle performing SHM is

```
x = A sin(ωt + φ)
```

or

```
x = A cos(ωt + φ)
```

where

- **A** = Amplitude
- **ω** = Angular frequency
- **φ** = Phase constant
- **t** = Time

---

# 7. Velocity in SHM

Velocity is obtained by differentiating displacement with respect to time.

```
v = dx/dt
```

Maximum velocity,

```
vmax = ωA
```

Velocity at displacement **x**,

```
v = ±ω√(A² − x²)
```

---

# 8. Acceleration in SHM

Acceleration is

```
a = -ω²x
```

Maximum acceleration,

```
amax = ω²A
```

---

# 10. Phase

## Definition

The quantity that specifies the state of oscillation at any instant is called the **phase**.

Phase angle,

```
(ωt + φ)
```

---

## Phase Difference

Difference between the phases of two oscillating particles.

Unit

```
radian
```

---

# 11. Energy in SHM

---

## Potential Energy

```
PE = ½kx²
```

---

## Kinetic Energy

```
KE = ½k(A² − x²)
```

---

## Total Energy

```
E = ½kA²
```

or

```
E = ½mω²A²
```

---

## Energy Variation

At mean position,

- KE is maximum.
- PE is zero.

At extreme position,

- PE is maximum.
- KE is zero.

Total energy remains constant.

---

# 12. Oscillations of a Spring

For a mass attached to a spring,

Restoring force,

```
F = -kx
```

Time period,

```
T = 2π√(m/k)
```

where

- **m** = Mass attached
- **k** = Spring constant

---

## Observations

- Time period increases with mass.
- Time period decreases with spring stiffness.

---

# 13. Simple Pendulum

## Definition

A simple pendulum consists of a small heavy bob suspended by a light, inextensible string from a fixed support.

---

## Time Period

For small oscillations,

```
T = 2π√(L/g)
```

where

- **L** = Length of pendulum
- **g** = Acceleration due to gravity

---

## Characteristics

- Independent of mass.
- Independent of amplitude (for small oscillations).
- Depends only on **L** and **g**.

---

# 14. Damped Oscillations

## Definition

Oscillations in which the amplitude gradually decreases due to resistive forces are called **damped oscillations**.

Examples

- Swing coming to rest
- Vibrating tuning fork in air

---

## Causes

- Air resistance
- Friction
- Viscous forces

---

# 15. Forced Oscillations

## Definition

Oscillations produced by an external periodic force.

Examples

- Vibrating machine parts
- Loudspeaker diaphragm

---

# 16. Resonance

## Definition

Resonance occurs when the frequency of the external periodic force becomes equal to the natural frequency of the system.

---

## Effects

- Maximum amplitude
- Maximum energy transfer

---

## Applications

- Musical instruments
- Radio tuning
- Microwave cavities

---

## Harmful Effects

- Bridge collapse due to resonance
- Building vibrations during earthquakes
- Mechanical failures

---

# Comparison Tables

## Periodic Motion vs Oscillatory Motion

| Periodic Motion               | Oscillatory Motion             |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Repeats after equal intervals | To-and-fro about mean position |
| May not have restoring force  | Restoring force is essential   |
| Example: Earth's revolution   | Example: Pendulum              |

---

## SHM vs Periodic Motion

| SHM                             | Periodic Motion              |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Special type of periodic motion | General repeating motion     |
| Restoring force ∝ displacement  | No such condition necessary  |
| Sinusoidal motion               | May or may not be sinusoidal |

---

## Simple Pendulum vs Spring Oscillator

| Simple Pendulum                | Spring Oscillator              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Time period depends on L and g | Time period depends on m and k |
| Restoring force due to gravity | Restoring force due to spring  |
| Oscillates in an arc           | Oscillates in a straight line  |

---

## Damped vs Forced Oscillations

| Damped Oscillations     | Forced Oscillations           |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Amplitude decreases     | Maintained by external force  |
| Due to resistive forces | Due to periodic driving force |

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

A particle completes **10 oscillations in 20 s**. Calculate its frequency and time period.

**Solution**

Frequency,

```
f = Number of oscillations / Time
```

```
= 10 / 20

= 0.5 Hz
```

Time period,

```
T = 1/f

= 1/0.5

= 2 s
```

---

### Example 2

A spring has a spring constant of

```
200 N m⁻¹
```

and carries a mass of

```
2 kg
```

Calculate the time period.

**Solution**

```
T = 2π√(m/k)
```

```
= 2π√(2/200)

= 2π√0.01

= 0.2π

≈ 0.628 s
```

---

### Example 3

A simple pendulum has a length of

```
1 m
```

Calculate its time period.

Take

```
g = 9.8 m s⁻²
```

**Solution**

```
T = 2π√(L/g)
```

```
= 2π√(1/9.8)

≈ 2.01 s
```

---

### Example 4

A particle performs SHM with amplitude

```
0.10 m
```

and angular frequency

```
5 rad s⁻¹
```

Calculate the maximum velocity.

**Solution**

```
vmax = ωA
```

```
= 5 × 0.10

= 0.5 m s⁻¹
```

---

### Example 5

A spring has

```
k = 400 N m⁻¹
```

and amplitude

```
0.05 m
```

Calculate the total energy stored.

**Solution**

```
E = ½kA²
```

```
= ½ × 400 × (0.05)²

= 0.5 J
```

---

### Example 6

Explain why soldiers are advised not to march in step while crossing a bridge.

**Solution**

If the marching frequency becomes equal to the natural frequency of the bridge, **resonance** may occur. This greatly increases the amplitude of vibrations and may damage or even collapse the bridge. Hence, soldiers break step while crossing bridges.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **periodic motion** with **simple harmonic motion**; every SHM is periodic, but not every periodic motion is SHM.
- Forgetting the negative sign in **F = -kx** and **a = -ω²x**, which indicates that the restoring force and acceleration are directed towards the mean position.
- Using the **simple pendulum formula** for large amplitudes; it is valid only for **small oscillations**.
- Assuming the time period of a simple pendulum depends on the **mass of the bob**; it depends only on **length** and **acceleration due to gravity**.
- Mixing up **frequency**, **time period**, and **angular frequency**.
- Assuming kinetic and potential energies remain constant individually; only the **total mechanical energy** remains constant in undamped SHM.
- Confusing **forced oscillations** with **resonance**; resonance occurs only when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency.
- Forgetting to use **SI units** in numerical calculations.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Oscillatory motion** is the repeated to-and-fro motion of a body about its equilibrium position.
- **Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)** is a special type of oscillation in which the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement and acts towards the mean position.
- The displacement, velocity, and acceleration of a particle in SHM vary sinusoidally with time.
- The important quantities describing SHM are **amplitude**, **time period**, **frequency**, **angular frequency**, and **phase**.
- In SHM, **kinetic energy** and **potential energy** continuously transform into each other, while the **total mechanical energy** remains constant.
- The time period of a **spring oscillator** is **T = 2π√(m/k)**, whereas that of a **simple pendulum** is **T = 2π√(L/g)** for small oscillations.
- **Damped oscillations** gradually lose amplitude due to resistive forces, whereas **forced oscillations** are maintained by an external periodic force.
- **Resonance** occurs when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency, resulting in maximum amplitude and efficient energy transfer.
- Oscillations and SHM are widely applied in clocks, musical instruments, engineering structures, communication systems, and many natural phenomena.
