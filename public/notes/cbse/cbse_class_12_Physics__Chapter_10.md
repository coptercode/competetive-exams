# Introduction

Wave optics deals with the phenomena of light that cannot be explained by ray optics.

It proves that light behaves as a **wave**.

Important phenomena:

1. Interference
2. Diffraction
3. Polarisation

---

# 1. Huygens' Principle

## Statement

Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets that spread in all directions with the speed of light.

The new wavefront is the forward envelope of these wavelets.

---

# 2. Wavefront

## Definition

A wavefront is the locus of all points having the same phase of vibration.

---

# Types of Wavefronts

## 1. Spherical Wavefront

Produced by a point source.

Example:

Bulb

---

## 2. Plane Wavefront

Produced when source is very far away.

Example:

Sunlight

---

## 3. Cylindrical Wavefront

Produced by a line source.

---

# 3. Huygens' Principle and Reflection

Using Huygens' principle:

Law of reflection can be derived:

\[
\boxed{i=r}
\]

---

# 4. Huygens' Principle and Refraction

Refraction occurs due to change in speed of light in different media.

Snell's law:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}
=

\frac{v_1}{v_2}
}
\]

---

# 5. Principle of Superposition

## Statement

When two or more waves overlap, the resultant displacement is the vector sum of individual displacements.

\[
\boxed{
y=y_1+y_2
}
\]

---

# 6. Interference of Light

## Definition

The phenomenon of redistribution of intensity due to superposition of two coherent light waves is called interference.

---

# Conditions for Sustained Interference

1. Sources must be coherent.
2. Same frequency.
3. Constant phase difference.
4. Nearly equal amplitudes.

---

# 7. Constructive Interference

## Definition

When two waves meet in the same phase, intensity becomes maximum.

---

Condition:

Path difference:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta x=n\lambda
}
\]

where:

\[
n=0,1,2...
\]

---

Intensity:

\[
\boxed{
I_{max}=(\sqrt{I_1}+\sqrt{I_2})^2
}
\]

---

# 8. Destructive Interference

## Definition

When two waves meet in opposite phases, intensity becomes minimum.

---

Condition:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta x=(2n+1)\frac{\lambda}{2}
}
\]

---

Intensity:

\[
\boxed{
I_{min}=(\sqrt{I_1}-\sqrt{I_2})^2
}
\]

---

# 9. Young's Double Slit Experiment (YDSE)

Performed by:

\[
\boxed{\text{Thomas Young}}
\]

---

## Principle

Interference pattern is obtained by superposition of light from two coherent sources.

---

# Arrangement

Two narrow slits:

- S₁
- S₂

Separated by distance:

\[
d
\]

Screen distance:

\[
D
\]

---

# Path Difference

For a point at distance y:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta x=\frac{dy}{D}
}
\]

---

# Fringe Width

## Definition

Distance between two consecutive bright or dark fringes.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\beta=\frac{\lambda D}{d}
}
\]

where:

- λ = wavelength
- D = screen distance
- d = slit separation

---

# Position of Bright Fringes

\[
\boxed{
y_n=n\frac{\lambda D}{d}
}
\]

---

# Position of Dark Fringes

\[
\boxed{
y_n=(2n+1)\frac{\lambda D}{2d}
}
\]

---

# 10. Diffraction of Light

## Definition

Bending of light around obstacles or spreading through narrow openings is called diffraction.

---

# Single Slit Diffraction

For slit width:

\[
a
\]

Condition for minima:

\[
\boxed{
a\sin\theta=n\lambda
}
\]

---

where:

\[
n=1,2,3...
\]

---

# 11. Width of Central Maximum

Angular width:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{2\lambda}{a}
}
\]

---

Linear width:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{2D\lambda}{a}
}
\]

---

# 12. Interference vs Diffraction

| Interference                          | Diffraction               |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Due to two coherent sources           | Due to one slit/edge      |
| Fringes equally spaced                | Central maximum widest    |
| Bright fringes have similar intensity | Central maximum strongest |

---

# 13. Polarisation

## Definition

The phenomenon of restricting vibrations of light waves to one direction is called polarisation.

---

# Importance

Polarisation proves:

\[
\boxed{\text{Light is a transverse wave}}
\]

---

# 14. Polariser and Analyzer

## Polariser

Produces plane polarised light.

---

## Analyzer

Detects polarisation.

---

# 15. Malus' Law

Intensity of transmitted light:

\[
\boxed{
I=I_0\cos^2\theta
}
\]

where:

θ = angle between polariser and analyzer axes.

---

# 16. Resolving Power

## Definition

Ability of an optical instrument to distinguish two closely placed objects.

---

# Rayleigh Criterion

Two images are just resolved when the central maximum of one coincides with the first minimum of another.

---

# Resolving Power of Microscope

\[
\boxed{
RP=\frac{2n\sin\theta}{\lambda}
}
\]

---

# Resolving Power of Telescope

\[
\boxed{
RP=\frac D{1.22\lambda}
}
\]

where:

D = diameter of objective lens

---

# Comparison Tables

## Wave Optics vs Ray Optics

| Wave Optics           | Ray Optics                     |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Treats light as waves | Treats light as rays           |
| Explains interference | Explains reflection/refraction |
| Uses wavelength       | Uses ray paths                 |

---

## Interference vs Diffraction

| Interference         | Diffraction           |
| -------------------- | --------------------- |
| Requires two sources | Requires one slit     |
| Equal fringe width   | Unequal intensity     |
| Narrow fringes       | Broad central maximum |

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** In YDSE, λ = 600 nm, D = 1 m and d = 1 mm. Find fringe width.

Given:

\[
\lambda=600\times10^{-9}m
\]

\[
D=1m
\]

\[
d=10^{-3}m
\]

Formula:

\[
\beta=\frac{\lambda D}{d}
\]

\[
=\frac{600\times10^{-9}}{10^{-3}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\beta=6\times10^{-4}m}
\]

---

## Example 2

**Question:** Why is the central maximum in diffraction widest?

### Solution

At the centre, light from a larger range of angles contributes, making the central maximum twice the width of other maxima.

---

## Example 3

**Question:** What are coherent sources?

### Solution

Sources having the same frequency and a constant phase difference are called coherent sources.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** Why does polarisation prove the transverse nature of light?

### Solution

Only transverse waves can have vibrations restricted to one direction. Therefore, polarisation proves light is transverse.

---

## Example 5

**Question:** What happens to fringe width if slit separation is increased?

From:

\[
\beta=\frac{\lambda D}{d}
\]

Increasing d decreases fringe width.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing interference and diffraction.
- Forgetting coherent source conditions.
- Using wrong path difference conditions.
- Mixing bright and dark fringe formulas.
- Forgetting units while calculating fringe width.
- Confusing polarisation with reflection.
- Forgetting that diffraction occurs for all waves.
- Using telescope resolving power formula for microscopes.

- Huygens' principle explains propagation of waves.

- Young’s double slit experiment proves wave nature of light.

- Diffraction explains bending of light around obstacles.
- Polarisation confirms that light is a transverse wave.
