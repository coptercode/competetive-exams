# Introduction

Solids experience **deformation** when external forces act on them. Some solids return to their original shape after the force is removed, while others do not. The study of the elastic behaviour of materials is important in engineering, architecture, construction, and material science.

This chapter introduces the concepts of **stress**, **strain**, **elasticity**, **Hooke's law**, and the different **moduli of elasticity**.

---

# 1. Elasticity

## Definition

**Elasticity** is the property of a material by virtue of which it regains its original shape and size after the deforming force is removed.

---

## Examples

- Steel spring
- Rubber band (within elastic limit)
- Quartz fibre

---

# 2. Plasticity

## Definition

**Plasticity** is the property of a material to undergo permanent deformation after the removal of the deforming force.

---

## Examples

- Clay
- Putty
- Wax

---

# 3. Deforming Force

A **deforming force** changes the shape or dimensions of a body.

Examples

- Stretching a wire
- Compressing a spring
- Twisting a rod

---

# 4. Restoring Force

When a body is deformed, an internal force develops that opposes the deformation.

This force is called the **restoring force**.

---

# 5. Stress

## Definition

Stress is the restoring force acting per unit area of cross-section.

Formula

```
          F
Stress = ----
          A
```

where

- **F** = Restoring force
- **A** = Cross-sectional area

---

## SI Unit

```
pascal (Pa)
```

```
1 Pa = 1 N m⁻²
```

---

## Dimensional Formula

```
[ML⁻¹T⁻²]
```

---

# 7. Strain

## Definition

Strain is the ratio of the change in dimension to the original dimension.

Strain has **no unit**.

---

# 9. Hooke's Law

## Statement

Within the elastic limit,

```
Stress

∝

Strain
```

or

```
Stress

=

Constant × Strain
```

The proportionality constant is called the **modulus of elasticity**.

# 13. Poisson's Ratio

## Definition

It is the ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain.

Formula

```
          Lateral Strain
σ = --------------------------
       Longitudinal Strain
```

Since the lateral dimension decreases when the longitudinal dimension increases,

```
          - Lateral Strain
σ = ----------------------------
         Longitudinal Strain
```

(The negative sign is often included by convention.)

---

## Range

For most materials,

```
0 < σ < 0.5
```

---

# 14. Stress-Strain Curve

For a ductile material such as mild steel:

```
Stress

│

│                  E

│                /

│             D

│           /

│        C

│      /

│   B

│ /

A

└──────────────────── Strain
```

---

## Important Points

### A → Proportional Limit

Stress is directly proportional to strain.

Hooke's law is valid.

---

### B → Elastic Limit

The material returns to its original dimensions after removing the force.

---

### C → Yield Point

Large deformation occurs with a small increase in stress.

---

### D → Ultimate Tensile Strength

Maximum stress sustained by the material.

---

### E → Breaking Point

The material fractures.

---

# 15. Elastic Potential Energy

When a body is stretched,

Work done is stored as elastic potential energy.

Formula

```
PE

=

½FΔL
```

For a spring,

```
PE

=

½kx²
```

where

- **k** = Spring constant
- **x** = Extension or compression

---

# 16. Elastic After Effect

A material may take some time to regain its original shape after the deforming force is removed.

This phenomenon is called **elastic after effect**.

---

# 17. Elastic Fatigue

Repeated loading and unloading reduce the elasticity of a material.

This is known as **elastic fatigue**.

Example

- Metal wires in bridges
- Aircraft wings

---

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

A force of **500 N** acts on a wire of cross-sectional area **2 × 10⁻⁴ m²**. Calculate the stress.

**Solution**

Using

```
Stress = F/A
```

```
=

500

────────

2 × 10⁻⁴

=

2.5 × 10⁶ Pa
```

### Example 2

A wire of length **2 m** extends by **2 mm**. Find the longitudinal strain.

**Solution**

Convert

```
2 mm = 0.002 m
```

Using

```

0.002

2

=

0.001
```

### Example 6

A steel wire experiences a tensile force of **100 N** and elongates within its elastic limit. Why does it return to its original length after the force is removed?

**Solution**

The wire obeys **Hooke's law** within the elastic limit. The restoring forces developed inside the material bring it back to its original dimensions after the external force is removed.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **stress** with **pressure**; stress is an internal restoring force per unit area, while pressure is an external force per unit area.
- Treating **strain** as having units; strain is a **dimensionless ratio**.
- Applying **Hooke's law** beyond the **elastic limit**, where it is no longer valid.
- Mixing up **Young's modulus**, **Bulk modulus**, and **Shear modulus**.
- Forgetting to convert **millimetres** to **metres** before calculations.
- Assuming all materials obey Hooke's law under all conditions; it is valid only within the proportional limit.
- Confusing the **elastic limit** with the **yield point** on the stress-strain curve.
- Ignoring the negative sign in the definition of **Poisson's ratio** when using the conventional expression.

# Chapter Summary

- **Elasticity** is the property by which a material regains its original shape after the deforming force is removed, whereas **plasticity** refers to permanent deformation.
- **Stress** is the restoring force per unit area, while **strain** is the fractional deformation produced in a body.
- **Hooke's law** states that stress is directly proportional to strain within the elastic limit.
- The three elastic constants—**Young's modulus**, **Bulk modulus**, and **Shear modulus**—describe different types of deformation in solids.
- **Poisson's ratio** relates the lateral contraction to the longitudinal extension of a material under tensile stress.
- The **stress-strain curve** provides important information about the elastic limit, yield point, ultimate tensile strength, and breaking point of a material.
- Work done in deforming an elastic body is stored as **elastic potential energy**.
- The principles of elasticity are widely applied in engineering structures such as bridges, buildings, cranes, railway tracks, springs, and measuring instruments.
- Understanding the mechanical properties of solids is essential for selecting suitable materials in construction, manufacturing, and engineering design.
