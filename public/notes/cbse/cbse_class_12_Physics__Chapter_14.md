# Introduction

A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of conductors and insulators.

Examples:

- Silicon (Si)
- Germanium (Ge)

---

# 1. Energy Bands in Solids

In solids, electrons occupy energy bands.

---

# A. Valence Band

The highest energy band filled with electrons is called the valence band.

---

# B. Conduction Band

The band in which electrons are free to move and conduct electricity is called the conduction band.

---

# C. Forbidden Energy Gap

The energy difference between valence band and conduction band is called band gap.

\[
\boxed{E_g}
\]

---

# Classification of Materials

---

# 1. Conductors

Properties:

- Valence band and conduction band overlap.
- Large number of free electrons.

Examples:

- Copper
- Aluminium

---

# 2. Insulators

Properties:

- Large energy gap.
- No free electrons.

Examples:

- Glass
- Rubber

---

# 3. Semiconductors

Properties:

- Small energy gap.
- Conductivity increases with temperature.

Examples:

- Silicon
- Germanium

---

# 2. Intrinsic Semiconductor

## Definition

A pure semiconductor without any impurity is called an intrinsic semiconductor.

---

Examples:

- Pure silicon
- Pure germanium

---

At room temperature:

Some covalent bonds break and create:

- Free electrons
- Holes

---

# Electron-Hole Pair

When an electron leaves a covalent bond:

- It becomes a free electron.
- The empty space is called a hole.

---

# 3. Extrinsic Semiconductor

## Definition

A semiconductor whose conductivity is increased by adding impurities is called an extrinsic semiconductor.

Process:

\[
\boxed{\text{Doping}}
\]

---

Types:

1. n-type semiconductor
2. p-type semiconductor

---

# 4. n-Type Semiconductor

## Definition

A semiconductor doped with pentavalent impurity is called n-type.

---

Examples of impurities:

- Phosphorus
- Arsenic
- Antimony

---

Pentavalent atoms have:

\[
5
\]

valence electrons.

---

Four electrons form bonds.

One extra electron becomes free.

---

Majority carriers:

\[
\boxed{\text{Electrons}}
\]

Minority carriers:

\[
\boxed{\text{Holes}}
\]

---

# 5. p-Type Semiconductor

## Definition

A semiconductor doped with trivalent impurity is called p-type.

---

Examples:

- Boron
- Aluminium
- Gallium

---

Trivalent atoms have:

\[
3
\]

valence electrons.

---

One bond remains incomplete, creating a hole.

---

Majority carriers:

\[
\boxed{\text{Holes}}
\]

Minority carriers:

\[
\boxed{\text{Electrons}}
\]

---

# 6. p-n Junction

## Definition

The boundary formed when p-type and n-type semiconductors are joined is called a p-n junction.

---

# Formation

When p-type and n-type materials join:

- Electrons move from n-side to p-side.
- Holes move from p-side to n-side.

This creates a region without free carriers.

Called:

\[
\boxed{\text{Depletion Region}}
\]

---

# 7. Barrier Potential

The potential difference across the depletion region is called barrier potential.

---

For silicon:

\[
\boxed{0.7V}
\]

---

For germanium:

\[
\boxed{0.3V}
\]

---

# 8. Biasing of p-n Junction

Biasing means applying external voltage to a p-n junction.

Types:

1. Forward bias
2. Reverse bias

---

# 9. Forward Bias

Connection:

- p-side to positive terminal
- n-side to negative terminal

---

Effect:

- Depletion width decreases.
- Current increases.

---

# Current Flow

Due to:

- Majority carriers

---

# 10. Reverse Bias

Connection:

- p-side to negative terminal
- n-side to positive terminal

---

Effect:

- Depletion width increases.
- Very small current flows.

---

# Breakdown Voltage

At high reverse voltage, current suddenly increases.

This voltage is called breakdown voltage.

---

# 11. Diode

## Definition

A p-n junction used as an electronic device is called a semiconductor diode.

---

# Diode Symbol

```
----|>|----
```

---

# Characteristics

## Forward Bias

- Small current initially.
- Current increases rapidly after threshold voltage.

---

## Reverse Bias

- Very small reverse current.
- Large current after breakdown.

---

# 12. Applications of Diode

---

# A. Rectification

Conversion of AC into DC.

---

Types:

1. Half-wave rectifier
2. Full-wave rectifier

---

# Half-Wave Rectifier

Uses one diode.

Output:

Only one half cycle of AC.

---

# Full-Wave Rectifier

Uses two or four diodes.

Output:

Both half cycles.

---

# 13. Zener Diode

## Definition

A specially designed diode used in reverse bias region for voltage regulation.

---

# Symbol

```
----|<|----
```

---

# Working

At breakdown voltage, voltage remains almost constant.

---

# Applications

- Voltage stabilisation
- Voltage regulation

---

# 14. LED (Light Emitting Diode)

## Definition

A diode that emits light when forward biased.

---

Materials:

- Gallium arsenide
- Gallium phosphide

---

Applications:

- Displays
- Indicators
- Optical communication

---

# 15. Photodiode

## Definition

A diode operated in reverse bias that detects light.

---

Applications:

- Light sensors
- Optical communication

---

# 16. Solar Cell

## Definition

A device that converts solar energy into electrical energy.

---

Principle:

\[
\boxed{\text{Photovoltaic effect}}
\]

---

Construction:

- Large-area p-n junction

---

Applications:

- Satellites
- Solar panels

---

# 17. Transistor

## Definition

A semiconductor device consisting of two p-n junctions is called a transistor.

Types:

1. npn transistor
2. pnp transistor

---

# Parts of Transistor

1. Emitter
2. Base
3. Collector

---

# Functions

- Amplification
- Switching

---

# Transistor Current Relations

\[
\boxed{
I_E=I_B+I_C
}
\]

---

# Current Gain

For common emitter:

\[
\boxed{
\beta=\frac{I_C}{I_B}
}
\]

---

# 18. Transistor as Amplifier

A small input signal produces a larger output signal.

---

Principle:

Small variation in base current controls large collector current.

---

# 19. Logic Gates

Digital circuits use logic gates.

---

# AND Gate

Output is 1 only when all inputs are 1.

Boolean expression:

\[
\boxed{
Y=A.B
}
\]

---

# OR Gate

Output is 1 if any input is 1.

\[
\boxed{
Y=A+B
}
\]

---

# NOT Gate

Output is opposite of input.

\[
\boxed{
Y=\bar A
}
\]

---

# Universal Gates

1. NAND gate
2. NOR gate

---

# Comparison Tables

## n-Type vs p-Type Semiconductor

| n-Type                          | p-Type                      |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Pentavalent impurity            | Trivalent impurity          |
| Electrons are majority carriers | Holes are majority carriers |
| Example: Phosphorus             | Example: Boron              |

---

## Forward Bias vs Reverse Bias

| Forward Bias               | Reverse Bias               |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Depletion region decreases | Depletion region increases |
| High current               | Small current              |
| Conducting state           | Blocking state             |

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

**Question:** Why does semiconductor conductivity increase with temperature?

### Solution

Increasing temperature breaks more covalent bonds, creating more free electrons and holes, increasing conductivity.

---

## Example 2

**Question:** Why is silicon preferred over germanium?

### Solution

Silicon has:

- Larger band gap.
- Better thermal stability.
- Lower leakage current.

---

## Example 3

**Question:** What are majority carriers in n-type semiconductor?

### Solution

Electrons are majority carriers.

---

## Example 4

**Question:** What is the function of a Zener diode?

### Solution

It maintains a constant output voltage and is used as a voltage regulator.

---

## Example 5

**Question:** Why is a diode used as a rectifier?

### Solution

A diode allows current mainly in one direction, converting AC into DC.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing electrons and holes as actual particles moving in opposite directions.
- Forgetting majority carriers in p-type and n-type materials.
- Mixing forward and reverse bias connections.
- Confusing LED and photodiode applications.
- Forgetting that Zener diode works in reverse bias.
- Using transistor current equations incorrectly.
- Confusing semiconductor conductivity behaviour with metals.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Semiconductors have conductivity between conductors and insulators.
- Doping produces n-type and p-type semiconductors.
- A p-n junction forms a depletion region and barrier potential.
- Diodes allow current mainly in one direction.
- Rectifiers convert AC into DC.
- Zener diodes regulate voltage.
- LEDs emit light and photodiodes detect light.
- Solar cells convert sunlight into electricity.
- Transistors work as amplifiers and switches.
- Logic gates form the foundation of digital electronics.
