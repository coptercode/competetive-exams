# Introduction

Atoms combine with one another to attain **greater stability**, generally by achieving the electronic configuration of the nearest noble gas (octet or duplet). The attractive forces holding atoms together are called **chemical bonds**.

The nature of bonding determines the **physical properties, chemical properties, molecular shape, polarity, and reactivity** of substances.

---

# 1. Octet Rule

## Definition

Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to achieve **eight electrons** in their valence shell.

Example

```
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl
```

---

## Duplet Rule

Atoms having only one shell become stable with **2 electrons**.

Example

- Hydrogen
- Helium

---

## Limitations of Octet Rule

### Incomplete Octet

Examples

- BeCl₂
- BF₃

---

### Expanded Octet

Examples

- PCl₅
- SF₆
- IF₇

---

### Odd Electron Molecules

Examples

- NO
- NO₂
- ClO₂

# 3. Ionic Bond (Electrovalent Bond)

## Definition

A bond formed by **complete transfer of electrons** from one atom to another.

Example

```
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Na⁺Cl⁻
```

---

## Formation of NaCl

```
Na  →  Na⁺ + e⁻

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl
```

---

## Properties of Ionic Compounds

- High melting point.
- High boiling point.
- Hard and brittle.
- Conduct electricity in molten or aqueous state.
- Soluble in polar solvents.

---

# 4. Factors Favouring Ionic Bond Formation

- Low ionization enthalpy.
- High electron affinity (electron gain enthalpy).
- High lattice enthalpy.
- Large electronegativity difference.

---

# 5. Lattice Enthalpy

## Definition

Energy released when one mole of an ionic solid is formed from its gaseous ions.

Higher lattice enthalpy implies:

- Stronger ionic bond.
- Higher melting point.
- Greater stability.

---

# 10. Bond Parameters

---

## Bond Length

Distance between nuclei of bonded atoms.

Trend

```
Single > Double > Triple
```

---

## Bond Angle

Angle between two covalent bonds.

Examples

| Molecule | Bond Angle |
| -------- | ---------- |
| CH₄      | 109.5°     |
| NH₃      | 107°       |
| H₂O      | 104.5°     |

---

## Bond Enthalpy

Energy required to break one mole of bonds.

Trend

```
Triple > Double > Single
```

---

## Bond Order

```
Bond Order

=

(Number of Bonding Electrons − Number of Antibonding Electrons)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

2
```

Greater bond order means:

- Stronger bond.
- Shorter bond.
- Higher bond enthalpy.

# 11. Resonance

## Definition

When a molecule cannot be represented by a single Lewis structure.

Actual molecule is:

```

## Example

```

## Characteristics

- Bond lengths become equal.
- Electron delocalization occurs.

# 15. Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

### Main Postulates

- Covalent bond forms by overlap of half-filled atomic orbitals.
- Greater overlap leads to stronger bond.

---

## Types of Overlap

### Sigma (σ) Bond

Head-on overlap.

Characteristics

- Stronger bond.
- Free rotation possible.

---

### Pi (π) Bond

Sidewise overlap.

Characteristics

- Weaker than σ bond.
- Restricts rotation.

# Solved Examples

### Example 1

Determine the hybridization and shape of methane (CH₄).

**Solution**

Carbon forms four sigma bonds.

Hybridization:

```
sp³
```

Geometry:

```
Tetrahedral
```

Bond angle:

```
109.5°
```

---

### Example 2

Calculate the bond order of O₂ using Molecular Orbital Theory.

**Solution**

Bonding electrons = 10

Antibonding electrons = 6

```
Bond Order

=

(10 − 6)/2

= 2
```

Hence, O₂ has a double bond and is stable.

---

### Example 3

Why is O₂ paramagnetic?

**Solution**

According to Molecular Orbital Theory, O₂ contains **two unpaired electrons** in the π* antibonding orbitals. Therefore, it is attracted by a magnetic field and is paramagnetic.

---

### Example 4

State the geometry of BF₃ using VSEPR theory.

**Solution**

The central boron atom has three bond pairs and no lone pairs.

Geometry:

```
Trigonal planar
```

Bond angle:

```
120°
```

---

### Example 5

Differentiate between intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonding.

**Solution**

Intermolecular hydrogen bonding occurs between different molecules (e.g., H₂O, HF), increasing boiling point and viscosity. Intramolecular hydrogen bonding occurs within the same molecule (e.g., o-nitrophenol), affecting molecular shape and reducing intermolecular attraction.

---

### Example 6

Calculate the dipole moment if the magnitude of charge is **1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C** and the separation distance is **2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m**.

**Solution**

Using

```
μ = q × d
```

```
= (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) × (2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m)

= 3.2 × 10⁻²⁹ C·m
```

Thus, the dipole moment is **3.2 × 10⁻²⁹ C·m** (approximately **9.6 D**, using 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m).

# Common Mistakes

- Assuming every molecule follows the **octet rule**; compounds like **BF₃**, **PCl₅**, and **SF₆** are important exceptions.
- Confusing **ionic bonds** with **coordinate covalent bonds**.
- Mixing up **bond length** and **bond enthalpy**.
- Forgetting that **σ bonds are stronger** than **π bonds**.
- Assuming all molecules containing polar bonds are polar; molecular geometry must also be considered (e.g., CO₂ is non-polar despite polar C=O bonds).
- Using the incorrect molecular orbital energy order for **B₂–N₂** and **O₂–F₂**.
- Forgetting that **O₂ is paramagnetic**, a key success of Molecular Orbital Theory.
- Confusing **hybridization** with **molecular geometry**; for example, NH₃ is **sp³ hybridized** but has a **trigonal pyramidal** shape due to one lone pair.

# Chapter Summary

- Atoms form **chemical bonds** to attain greater stability, usually by achieving noble gas electronic configurations.
- The **octet rule** explains the formation of many ionic and covalent compounds but has important exceptions involving incomplete, expanded, and odd-electron octets.
- **Ionic bonds** involve electron transfer, while **covalent bonds** involve electron sharing. **Coordinate bonds** are formed when both shared electrons are donated by the same atom.
- **Lewis structures** and **formal charges** help represent and compare molecular structures.
- Bond parameters such as **bond length**, **bond angle**, **bond enthalpy**, and **bond order** determine molecular stability and properties.
- **VSEPR theory** predicts molecular shapes based on electron pair repulsions around the central atom.
- **Valence Bond Theory** explains covalent bond formation through orbital overlap and introduces **hybridization** to account for observed molecular geometries.
- **Molecular Orbital Theory** describes the formation of bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals, successfully explaining **bond order**, **bond stability**, and the **paramagnetic nature of O₂**.
- **Hydrogen bonding** and other intermolecular forces significantly influence physical properties such as boiling point, melting point, viscosity, and solubility.
