# PART A: RATE OF CHEMICAL REACTION

---

# 1. Rate of Reaction

## Definition

The change in concentration of reactants or products per unit time is called the rate of reaction.

---

For reactants:

\[
\boxed{
Rate=-\frac{\Delta[R]}{\Delta t}
}
\]

---

For products:

\[
\boxed{
Rate=\frac{\Delta[P]}{\Delta t}
}
\]

---

# 2. Average Rate

## Definition

Average rate is the change in concentration over a given time interval.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Average\ Rate=
-\frac{[R_2]-[R_1]}{t_2-t_1}
}
\]

---

# 3. Instantaneous Rate

## Definition

The rate of reaction at a particular instant of time is called instantaneous rate.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Rate=-\frac{d[R]}{dt}
}
\]

---

# 4. Factors Affecting Rate of Reaction

---

## 1. Nature of Reactants

Ionic reactions are generally faster than covalent reactions.

---

## 2. Concentration

Higher concentration:

\[
\rightarrow
\boxed{\text{Higher reaction rate}}
\]

---

## 3. Temperature

Increase in temperature increases rate because more molecules acquire activation energy.

---

## 4. Catalyst

A catalyst increases reaction rate by lowering activation energy.

---

## 5. Surface Area

Powdered solids react faster than large pieces.

---

# PART B: RATE LAW AND ORDER OF REACTION

---

# 5. Rate Law

## Definition

The relationship between rate of reaction and concentration of reactants is called rate law.

---

For reaction:

\[
aA+bB\rightarrow Products
\]

Rate law:

\[
\boxed{
Rate=k[A]^m[B]^n
}
\]

where:

- k = rate constant
- m,n = orders with respect to reactants

---

# 6. Rate Constant

## Definition

The proportionality constant in the rate law equation is called rate constant.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{k}
\]

---

# Factors Affecting k

Depends on:

- Temperature
- Catalyst
- Nature of reaction

Does not depend on:

- Concentration

---

# 7. Order of Reaction

## Definition

The sum of powers of concentration terms in rate law is called order of reaction.

---

For:

\[
Rate=k[A]^m[B]^n
\]

Order:

\[
\boxed{
m+n
}
\]

---

# Types of Reaction Order

---

# Zero Order Reaction

Rate is independent of concentration.

\[
\boxed{
Rate=k
}
\]

---

Example:

Photochemical reactions

---

# First Order Reaction

Rate depends on concentration of one reactant.

\[
\boxed{
Rate=k[A]
}
\]

---

Example:

Radioactive decay

---

# Second Order Reaction

\[
\boxed{
Rate=k[A]^2
}
\]

or:

\[
k[A][B]
\]

---

# 8. Molecularity

## Definition

The number of reacting species participating in an elementary reaction is called molecularity.

---

Types:

1. Unimolecular
2. Bimolecular
3. Termolecular

---

# Difference Between Order and Molecularity

| Order                       | Molecularity               |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Experimental quantity       | Theoretical quantity       |
| Can be zero/fractional      | Always whole number        |
| Applies to overall reaction | Applies to elementary step |

---

# PART C: INTEGRATED RATE EQUATIONS

---

# 9. Zero Order Reaction

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k
\]

---

Integrated equation:

\[
\boxed{
[A]_t=[A]_0-kt
}
\]

---

# Half-Life Period

Time required for concentration to become half.

\[
\boxed{
t_{1/2}
=

\frac{[A]_0}{2k}
}
\]

---

# Graph

Plot of concentration vs time:

Straight line.

---

# 10. First Order Reaction

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k[A]
\]

---

Integrated equation:

\[
\boxed{
k=
\frac{2.303}{t}
\log
\frac{[A]_0}{[A]_t}
}
\]

---

# Half-Life Period

\[
\boxed{
t_{1/2}
=

\frac{0.693}{k}
}
\]

---

Important property:

Half-life is independent of initial concentration.

---

# 11. Second Order Reaction

For:

\[
Rate=k[A]^2
\]

Integrated equation:

\[
\boxed{
\frac1{[A]_t}
=

\frac1{[A]_0}+kt
}
\]

---

Half-life:

\[
\boxed{
t_{1/2}
=

\frac1{k[A]_0}
}
\]

---

# PART D: ACTIVATION ENERGY

---

# 12. Collision Theory

## Basic Idea

Reacting molecules must:

1. Collide.
2. Have sufficient energy.
3. Have proper orientation.

---

# Effective Collision

A collision that results in product formation is called an effective collision.

---

# 13. Activation Energy

## Definition

The minimum energy required for reactants to undergo reaction is called activation energy.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{E_a}
\]

---

# Energy Profile

Reactants:

\[
\rightarrow
\]

Activated complex:

\[
\rightarrow
\]

Products

---

# Catalyst Effect

A catalyst:

- Lowers activation energy.
- Provides alternative pathway.
- Does not change equilibrium.

---

# PART E: ARRHENIUS EQUATION

---

# 14. Arrhenius Equation

Relationship between rate constant and temperature:

\[
\boxed{
k=Ae^{-E_a/RT}
}
\]

where:

- A = frequency factor
- Ea = activation energy
- R = gas constant
- T = temperature

---

# Logarithmic Form

\[
\boxed{
\log k=
\log A-
\frac{E_a}{2.303RT}
}
\]

---

# Two Temperature Form

\[
\boxed{
\log
\frac{k_2}{k_1}
=

\frac{E_a}{2.303R}
\left(
\frac1{T_1}
-

\frac1{T_2}
\right)
}
\]

---

# PART F: MECHANISM OF REACTION

---

# 15. Elementary Reaction

A reaction occurring in a single step is called elementary reaction.

---

# 16. Complex Reaction

A reaction occurring through multiple steps is called complex reaction.

---

# Rate Determining Step

The slowest step in a reaction mechanism that controls the overall rate is called rate determining step.

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

A first-order reaction has:

\[
k=0.693s^{-1}
\]

Find half-life.

Formula:

\[
t_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{k}
\]

\[
=\frac{0.693}{0.693}
\]

\[
\boxed{
t_{1/2}=1s
}
\]

---

## Example 2

For reaction:

\[
Rate=k[A]^2[B]
\]

Find order.

Powers:

A = 2

B = 1

Therefore:

\[
Order=2+1
\]

\[
\boxed{Order=3}
\]

---

## Example 3

Why does catalyst increase reaction rate?

Answer:

Because it:

\[
\boxed{\text{decreases activation energy}}
\]

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing order and molecularity.
- Forgetting negative sign for reactant rate.
- Mixing zero-order and first-order equations.
- Using incorrect half-life formula.
- Forgetting rate constant depends on temperature.
- Confusing catalyst effect on activation energy.
- Using Celsius instead of Kelvin in Arrhenius equation.

---

# Unit Summary

- Chemical kinetics studies reaction speed.
- Rate depends on concentration, temperature and catalysts.
- Rate law gives relationship between concentration and rate.
- Order is determined experimentally.
- First-order reactions have constant half-life relation:

\[
\boxed{
t_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{k}
}
\]

- Arrhenius equation explains temperature dependence of reaction rates.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Higher temperature → More effective collisions → Faster reaction}
}
\]
