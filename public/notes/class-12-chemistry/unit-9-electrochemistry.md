# Introduction

Electrochemistry deals with the relationship between:

- Chemical reactions
- Electrical energy

It explains how chemical energy can be converted into electrical energy and vice versa.

This unit covers:

- Electrolytic conductance
- Conductivity
- Molar conductivity
- Kohlrausch's law
- Galvanic cells
- Electrode potential
- Nernst equation
- Electrochemical cells

Applications:

- Batteries
- Electroplating
- Corrosion prevention
- Fuel cells

---

# PART A: ELECTROLYTIC CONDUCTANCE

---

# 1. Electrolytes

## Definition

Substances that produce ions in aqueous solution and conduct electricity are called electrolytes.

Examples:

\[
NaCl\rightarrow Na^++Cl^-
\]

\[
HCl\rightarrow H^++Cl^-
\]

---

# 2. Conductance

## Definition

The ability of a solution to conduct electricity is called conductance.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{G}
\]

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
G=\frac1R
}
\]

where:

- G = conductance
- R = resistance

Unit:

\[
\boxed{Siemens(S)}
\]

---

# 3. Resistance

According to Ohm's law:

\[
\boxed{
R=\frac{V}{I}
}
\]

where:

- V = potential difference
- I = current

---

# 4. Specific Conductance (Conductivity)

## Definition

Conductance of a solution kept between two electrodes of unit area separated by unit distance is called specific conductance.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{\kappa}
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\kappa=G\frac{l}{A}
}
\]

where:

- l = distance between electrodes
- A = area of electrodes

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{
S\,cm^{-1}
}
\]

---

# 5. Molar Conductivity

## Definition

The conductance of all ions produced by dissolving one mole of electrolyte in solution is called molar conductivity.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{\Lambda_m}
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Lambda_m=
\frac{\kappa\times1000}{C}
}
\]

where:

- C = concentration in mol L⁻¹

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{
S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}
}
\]

---

# 6. Variation of Molar Conductivity

---

## Strong Electrolytes

Example:

- HCl
- NaCl

Molar conductivity increases slightly on dilution.

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
\Lambda_m=\Lambda_m^0-A\sqrt C
}
\]

---

## Weak Electrolytes

Example:

- CH₃COOH

Molar conductivity increases greatly on dilution.

Reason:

Increase in ionisation.

---

# PART B: KOHLRAUSCH'S LAW

---

# 7. Kohlrausch's Law

## Statement

At infinite dilution, each ion contributes independently to the total molar conductivity of an electrolyte.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Lambda_m^0=
\lambda_+^0+\lambda_-^0
}
\]

---

where:

- \(\lambda_+^0\) = ionic conductivity of cation
- \(\lambda_-^0\) = ionic conductivity of anion

---

# Applications

---

## 1. Finding Limiting Conductivity

For weak electrolytes:

Example:

\[
CH_3COOH
\]

---

## 2. Degree of Dissociation

\[
\boxed{
\alpha=
\frac{\Lambda_m}{\Lambda_m^0}
}
\]

---

## 3. Dissociation Constant

\[
\boxed{
K_a=
\frac{C\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}
}
\]

---

# PART C: ELECTROCHEMICAL CELLS

---

# 8. Electrochemical Cell

## Definition

A device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy is called an electrochemical cell.

---

Types:

1. Galvanic cell
2. Electrolytic cell

---

# 9. Galvanic Cell

## Definition

A cell in which spontaneous chemical reaction produces electrical energy.

Example:

\[
\boxed{
Zn|Zn^{2+}||Cu^{2+}|Cu
}
\]

(Daniell cell)

---

# 10. Components of Galvanic Cell

---

## Anode

Oxidation occurs.

Example:

\[
Zn\rightarrow Zn^{2+}+2e^-
\]

---

## Cathode

Reduction occurs.

Example:

\[
Cu^{2+}+2e^-
\rightarrow Cu
\]

---

# Cell Reaction

\[
Zn+Cu^{2+}
\rightarrow
Zn^{2+}+Cu
\]

---

# 11. Salt Bridge

## Functions

1. Completes electrical circuit.
2. Maintains electrical neutrality.
3. Prevents mixing of solutions.

---

# PART D: ELECTRODE POTENTIAL

---

# 12. Electrode Potential

## Definition

The potential difference developed between electrode and electrolyte solution is called electrode potential.

---

# 13. Standard Electrode Potential

Measured under standard conditions:

- Concentration = 1M
- Pressure = 1 atm
- Temperature = 298K

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{E^0}
\]

---

# 14. Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

## Definition

The standard hydrogen electrode is used as reference electrode for measuring electrode potentials.

---

Reaction:

\[
2H^++2e^-
\rightleftharpoons H_2
\]

---

Standard potential:

\[
\boxed{
E^0=0V
}
\]

---

# PART E: CELL POTENTIAL

---

# 15. EMF of Cell

The potential difference between cathode and anode is called cell potential.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
E_{cell}=E_{cathode}-E_{anode}
}
\]

---

If:

\[
E_{cell}>0
\]

Reaction is spontaneous.

---

# PART F: GIBBS ENERGY AND ELECTROCHEMISTRY

---

# 16. Relation Between Gibbs Energy and EMF

\[
\boxed{
\Delta G=-nFE_{cell}
}
\]

where:

- n = number of electrons
- F = Faraday constant

---

# 17. Equilibrium Constant Relation

\[
\boxed{
\Delta G=-RT\ln K
}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\log K=
\frac{nE^0}{0.0591}
}
\]

(at 298K)

---

# PART G: NERNST EQUATION

---

# 18. Nernst Equation

## Definition

The equation used to calculate electrode potential under non-standard conditions is called Nernst equation.

---

General equation:

\[
\boxed{
E=E^0-\frac{0.0591}{n}
\log Q
}
\]

(at 298K)

---

For cell:

\[
\boxed{
E_{cell}
=

E^0_{cell}
-\frac{0.0591}{n}
\log Q
}
\]

---

# Example

For:

\[
Zn+Cu^{2+}
\rightarrow
Zn^{2+}+Cu
\]

Reaction quotient:

\[
Q=
\frac{[Zn^{2+}]}{[Cu^{2+}]}
\]

---

# PART H: ELECTROLYSIS AND FARADAY'S LAWS

---

# 19. Faraday's First Law

## Statement

The mass of substance deposited is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed.

---

\[
\boxed{
m\propto Q
}
\]

---

# 20. Faraday's Second Law

## Statement

When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes, the masses deposited are proportional to their equivalent masses.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
m=\frac{EIt}{F}
}
\]

---

---

# 21. Primary Cells

Example:

\[
\boxed{\text{Dry cell}}

---

# 22. Secondary Cells

Rechargeable batteries.

Example:

\[

\]

---

# 23. Fuel Cells

Convert fuel energy directly into electrical energy.

Example:

Hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell.

Reaction:

Anode:

\[
2H_2+4OH^-
\rightarrow
4H_2O+4e^-
\]

Cathode:

\[
O_2+2H_2O+4e^-
\rightarrow
4OH^-
\]

Overall:

\[
\boxed{
2H_2+O_2
\rightarrow
2H_2O
}
\]

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing anode and cathode reactions.
- Forgetting oxidation occurs at anode.
- Mixing conductivity and molar conductivity.
- Using wrong sign in cell potential equation.
- Forgetting Nernst equation uses Kelvin temperature.
- Confusing primary and secondary cells.
- Forgetting salt bridge functions.
