# Introduction

Physics is the branch of science that studies the fundamental laws governing matter, energy, space and time.

Measurement is the process of comparing an unknown physical quantity with a known standard quantity.

This unit covers:

- Physical quantities and units
- Measurement systems
- Errors in measurement
- Accuracy and precision
- Significant figures
- Dimensional analysis

Applications:

- Scientific experiments
- Engineering measurements
- Technology development
- Mathematical modelling

---

# PART A: PHYSICAL QUANTITIES

---

# 1. Physical Quantity

## Definition

A quantity that can be measured and expressed by a numerical value with a unit is called a physical quantity.

---

A physical quantity consists of:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Physical Quantity}=
\text{Numerical Value}\times\text{Unit}
}
\]

---

Examples:

Length:

\[
5m
\]

Mass:

\[
10kg
\]

Time:

\[
20s
\]

---

# 2. Fundamental Quantities

## Definition

Physical quantities that are independent and cannot be expressed in terms of other quantities are called fundamental quantities.

---

# SI Fundamental Quantities

| Quantity            | SI Unit  | Symbol |
| ------------------- | -------- | ------ |
| Length              | metre    | m      |
| Mass                | kilogram | kg     |
| Time                | second   | s      |
| Electric current    | ampere   | A      |
| Temperature         | kelvin   | K      |
| Amount of substance | mole     | mol    |
| Luminous intensity  | candela  | cd     |

---

# 3. Derived Quantities

## Definition

Quantities obtained by combining fundamental quantities are called derived quantities.

---

Examples:

---

## Velocity

\[
\boxed{
Velocity=\frac{Distance}{Time}
}
\]

Unit:

\[
m/s
\]

---

## Force

\[
\boxed{
F=ma
}
\]

Unit:

\[
kg\,m/s^2
\]

or

\[
\boxed{Newton(N)}
\]

---

## Density

\[
\boxed{
\rho=\frac{Mass}{Volume}
}
\]

Unit:

\[
kg/m^3
\]

# PART C: MEASUREMENT

---

# 6. Measurement

## Definition

Measurement is the comparison of an unknown quantity with a standard quantity.

---

Example:

Measuring length using a metre scale.

---

# 7. Least Count

## Definition

The smallest measurement that an instrument can measure accurately is called its least count.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Least\ Count=
\frac{Value\ of\ one\ main\ scale\ division}
{Number\ of\ vernier\ divisions}
}
\]

---

Examples:

Vernier calipers:

\[
0.01cm
\]

Screw gauge:

\[
0.001cm
\]

---

# PART D: ERRORS IN MEASUREMENT

---

# 8. Error

## Definition

The difference between the measured value and the true value is called error.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Error=
Measured\ Value-True\ Value
}
\]

---

# 9. Types of Errors

---

# 1. Systematic Errors

Errors that occur in a fixed pattern are called systematic errors.

---

Causes:

- Instrument defects
- Zero error
- Calibration errors
- Environmental conditions

---

Types:

### Instrumental Error

Due to faulty instruments.

---

### Environmental Error

Due to temperature, pressure or humidity changes.

---

### Observational Error

Due to incorrect reading by observer.

---

# 2. Random Errors

Errors that occur unpredictably are called random errors.

---

Causes:

- Small fluctuations
- Human limitations
- Unpredictable changes

---

# 3. Gross Errors

Large errors caused by mistakes.

Examples:

- Wrong reading
- Incorrect calculation
- Wrong recording

---

# PART E: ACCURACY AND PRECISION

---

# 10. Accuracy

## Definition

Accuracy describes how close a measured value is to the true value.

---

Example:

True value:

\[
10.0cm
\]

Measured:

\[
9.99cm
\]

High accuracy.

---

# 11. Precision

## Definition

Precision describes the closeness of repeated measurements to each other.

---

Example:

Measurements:

\[
5.01,5.02,5.01
\]

High precision.

---

# Difference Between Accuracy and Precision

| Accuracy                      | Precision                       |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Closeness to true value       | Closeness among repeated values |
| Indicates correctness         | Indicates consistency           |
| Affected by systematic errors | Affected by random errors       |

---

# PART F: ERROR CALCULATIONS

---

# 12. Absolute Error

For measured values:

\[
a_1,a_2,a_3...
\]

Mean value:

\[
\bar a
\]

Absolute error:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta a_i=|a_i-\bar a|
}
\]

---

# 13. Mean Absolute Error

\[
\boxed{
\Delta a=
\frac{\sum|\Delta a_i|}{n}
}
\]

---

# 14. Relative Error

\[
\boxed{
Relative\ Error=
\frac{\Delta a}{\bar a}
}
\]

---

# 15. Percentage Error

\[
\boxed{
Percentage\ Error=
\frac{\Delta a}{\bar a}\times100
}
\]

---

# PART G: PROPAGATION OF ERRORS

---

# 16. Error in Addition/Subtraction

If:

\[
Z=A+B
\]

or:

\[
Z=A-B
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta Z=\Delta A+\Delta B
}
\]

---

# 17. Error in Multiplication/Division

If:

\[
Z=AB
\]

or:

\[
Z=\frac AB
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Delta Z}{Z}
=

\frac{\Delta A}{A} +
\frac{\Delta B}{B}
}
\]

---

# 18. Error in Power

If:

\[
Z=A^n
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Delta Z}{Z}
=

n\frac{\Delta A}{A}
}
\]

# PART H: SIGNIFICANT FIGURES

---

# 19. Significant Figures

## Definition

The meaningful digits in a measured quantity are called significant figures.

---

Rules:

---

# Rule 1

All non-zero digits are significant.

Example:

\[
123
\]

has:

\[
3
\]

significant figures.

---

# Rule 2

Zeros between non-zero digits are significant.

Example:

\[
1005
\]

has:

\[
4
\]

significant figures.

---

# Rule 3

Leading zeros are not significant.

Example:

\[
0.0045
\]

has:

\[
2
\]

significant figures.

---

# Rule 4

Trailing zeros after decimal are significant.

Example:

\[
5.00
\]

has:

\[
3
\]

significant figures.

# PART I: DIMENSIONAL ANALYSIS

---

# 20. Dimensions

## Definition

Dimensions represent the powers of fundamental quantities used to express a physical quantity.

---

Fundamental dimensions:

Length:

\[
[L]
\]

Mass:

\[
[M]
\]

Time:

\[
[T]
\]

---

# 21. Dimensional Formula

The expression showing dependence on fundamental quantities is called dimensional formula.

---

Example:

Velocity:

\[
Velocity=
\frac{Length}{Time}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
[M^0L^1T^{-1}]
}
\]

# 23. Principle of Homogeneity

## Statement

A physical equation is dimensionally correct only if dimensions of all terms are the same.

---

Example:

\[
s=ut+\frac12at^2
\]

Dimensions:

Left side:

\[
[L]
\]

Right side:

\[
[LT^{-1}][T]+[LT^{-2}][T^2]
\]

\[
=[L]+[L]
\]

Therefore dimensionally correct.

---

# 24. Applications of Dimensional Analysis

---

## 1. Checking equations

Determines whether an equation is dimensionally correct.

---

## 2. Conversion of units

Used to convert one system of units into another.

---

## 3. Deriving relations

Can derive relationships between physical quantities.

---

# 25. Limitations of Dimensional Analysis

- Cannot determine numerical constants.
- Cannot derive equations involving trigonometric functions.
- Cannot distinguish quantities having same dimensions.
- Cannot verify complete physical correctness.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing accuracy with precision.
- Forgetting units in final answers.
- Ignoring zero error in instruments.
- Incorrect significant figure counting.
- Mixing dimensions and units.
- Assuming dimensional correctness means the equation is completely correct.
- Forgetting error rules for multiplication and addition are different.
