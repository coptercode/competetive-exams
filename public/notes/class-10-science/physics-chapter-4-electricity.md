# Introduction

Electricity is the study of electric charges, current, resistance and electrical circuits.

This chapter covers:

- Electric current
- Potential difference
- Ohm's law
- Resistance
- Resistivity
- Series and parallel combinations of resistors
- Electrical energy and power

---

# PART A: ELECTRIC CHARGE

---

# 1. Electric Charge

## Definition

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical interactions.

---

Types of charges:

1. Positive charge
2. Negative charge

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Coulomb (C)}}
\]

---

# PART B: ELECTRIC CURRENT

---

# 2. Electric Current

## Definition

The rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor is called electric current.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac{Q}{t}
}
\]

---

Where:

I = current

Q = charge

t = time

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Ampere (A)}}
\]

---

# 3. Direction of Current

Conventional current flows from:

\[
\boxed{\text{Positive terminal to negative terminal}}
\]

---

Electron flow is opposite:

\[
\boxed{\text{Negative to positive}}
\]

---

# PART C: ELECTRIC POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE

---

# 4. Potential Difference

## Definition

The work done to move a unit charge from one point to another is called potential difference.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
V=\frac{W}{Q}
}
\]

---

Where:

V = potential difference

W = work done

Q = charge

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Volt (V)}}
\]

---

# PART D: OHM'S LAW

---

# 5. Ohm's Law

## Statement

At constant temperature, the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends.

---

Mathematically:

\[
\boxed{
V\propto I
}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
V=IR
}
\]

---

Where:

V = voltage

I = current

R = resistance

---

# 6. V-I Graph

For an ohmic conductor:

- V-I graph is a straight line.
- Slope gives resistance.

---

# PART E: RESISTANCE

---

# 7. Resistance

## Definition

The opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of electric current is called resistance.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
R=\frac VI
}
\]

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Ohm }(\Omega)}
\]

---

# 8. Factors Affecting Resistance

Resistance depends on:

1. Length of conductor
2. Area of cross-section
3. Nature of material
4. Temperature

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
R\propto L
}
\]

---

\[
\boxed{
R\propto\frac1A
}
\]

---

# PART F: RESISTIVITY

---

# 9. Resistivity

## Definition

The resistance offered by a material of unit length and unit area of cross-section is called resistivity.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
R=\rho\frac LA
}
\]

---

Where:

ρ = resistivity

L = length

A = area

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\Omega m}
\]

---

# PART G: COMBINATION OF RESISTORS

---

# 10. Resistors in Series

## Definition

When resistors are connected one after another in a single path, they are said to be in series.

---

Diagram:

\[
R_1-R_2-R_3
\]

---

Current:

\[
\boxed{\text{Same through all resistors}}
\]

---

Voltage:

\[
\boxed{\text{Divided}}
\]

---

# Equivalent Resistance in Series

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
R=R_1+R_2+R_3
}
\]

---

Example:

If:

\[
R_1=2\Omega
\]

\[
R_2=3\Omega
\]

then:

\[
R=2+3
\]

\[
\boxed{5\Omega}
\]

---

# 11. Resistors in Parallel

## Definition

When resistors are connected between the same two points, they are in parallel.

---

Current:

\[
\boxed{\text{Divided}}
\]

---

Voltage:

\[
\boxed{\text{Same across all resistors}}
\]

---

# Equivalent Resistance in Parallel

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\frac1R=
\frac1{R_1}+
\frac1{R_2}+
\frac1{R_3}
}
\]

---

For two resistors:

\[
\boxed{
R=
\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}
}
\]

---

# PART H: ELECTRIC POWER

---

# 12. Electric Power

## Definition

The rate at which electrical energy is consumed is called electric power.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
P=VI
}
\]

---

Using Ohm's law:

\[
\boxed{
P=I^2R
}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
P=\frac{V^2}{R}
}
\]

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Watt (W)}}
\]

---

# PART I: ELECTRICAL ENERGY

---

# 13. Electrical Energy

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
E=Pt
}
\]

---

Also:

\[
\boxed{
E=VIt
}
\]

---

Commercial unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{kWh}}
\]

---

# 14. One Unit of Electricity

\[
\boxed{
1kWh=3.6\times10^6J
}
\]

---

# PART J: APPLICATIONS

---

# 15. Fuse

A fuse is a safety device that protects electrical circuits from excessive current.

---

It has:

- Low melting point
- High resistance

---

# 16. Domestic Electric Circuit

Components:

- Live wire
- Neutral wire
- Earth wire

---

# 17. Earthing

Earthing provides a path for leakage current and prevents electric shocks.

# Solved Examples

---

## Example 1

A current of 2 A flows through a conductor for 5 seconds.

Find charge.

Given:

\[
I=2A
\]

\[
t=5s
\]

Using:

\[
I=\frac Qt
\]

\[
Q=It
\]

\[
=2\times5
\]

\[
\boxed{10C}
\]

---

## Example 2

Find resistance when:

\[
V=12V
\]

\[
I=3A
\]

Using:

\[
R=\frac VI
\]

\[
R=\frac{12}{3}
\]

\[
\boxed{4\Omega}
\]

---

## Example 3

Two resistors 4Ω and 6Ω are connected in series.

\[
R=4+6
\]

\[
\boxed{10\Omega}
\]

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing current and voltage.
- Forgetting unit conversions.
- Using series formula for parallel circuits.
- Forgetting voltage is same in parallel circuits.
- Mixing electrical power and energy.
- Forgetting kWh is a unit of energy.

# Chapter Summary

- Electric current is the flow of charge.
- Ohm's law relates voltage, current and resistance.
- Resistance depends on length, area and material.
- Resistors can be connected in series or parallel.
- Electrical power measures energy consumption rate.
- Safety devices protect electrical circuits.
