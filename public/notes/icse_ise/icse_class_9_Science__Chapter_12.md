# Introduction

Everything around us is made up of atoms, the smallest units of matter that retain the properties of an element. Understanding the structure of an atom and the way atoms combine to form compounds is essential for studying chemistry. This chapter covers the fundamental particles of an atom, atomic models, electronic configuration, isotopes, and the formation of ionic (electrovalent) and covalent bonds.

---

# 1. Fundamental Particles of an Atom

An atom consists of three basic subatomic particles:

- Electrons
- Protons
- Neutrons

---

## Electron

An **electron** is a negatively charged particle present outside the nucleus.

| Property      | Value               |
| ------------- | ------------------- |
| Symbol        | e⁻                  |
| Charge        | -1                  |
| Relative Mass | 1/1836 u            |
| Location      | Outside the nucleus |

---

## Proton

A **proton** is a positively charged particle present inside the nucleus.

| Property      | Value   |
| ------------- | ------- |
| Symbol        | p⁺      |
| Charge        | +1      |
| Relative Mass | 1 u     |
| Location      | Nucleus |

---

## Neutron

A **neutron** is an electrically neutral particle present inside the nucleus.

| Property      | Value   |
| ------------- | ------- |
| Symbol        | n⁰      |
| Charge        | 0       |
| Relative Mass | 1 u     |
| Location      | Nucleus |

---

## Comparison of Subatomic Particles

| Particle | Symbol | Charge | Relative Mass | Location        |
| -------- | ------ | ------ | ------------- | --------------- |
| Electron | e⁻     | -1     | 1/1836 u      | Outside nucleus |
| Proton   | p⁺     | +1     | 1 u           | Nucleus         |
| Neutron  | n⁰     | 0      | 1 u           | Nucleus         |

# 2. Atomic Models

## Rutherford's Atomic Model

Rutherford proposed that:

- The atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus.
- Most of the atom is empty space.
- Electrons revolve around the nucleus.

### Limitations

- Could not explain the stability of atoms.
- Could not explain the arrangement of electrons.

---

## Bohr's Atomic Model

Niels Bohr improved Rutherford's model by introducing fixed energy levels.

### Postulates

- Electrons revolve around the nucleus in fixed circular paths called **shells** or **orbits**.
- Electrons do not lose energy while moving in these permitted orbits.
- Energy is absorbed or emitted only when electrons move between shells.

---

## Electron Shells

| Shell  | Symbol | Maximum Electrons |
| ------ | ------ | ----------------: |
| First  | K      |                 2 |
| Second | L      |                 8 |
| Third  | M      |                18 |
| Fourth | N      |                32 |

# 3. Atomic Mechanics

## Atomic Number (Z)

The **atomic number** is the number of protons present in the nucleus.

For a neutral atom:

```
Atomic Number (Z)

= Number of Protons

= Number of Electrons
```

---

## Mass Number (A)

The **mass number** is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

### Formula

```
A = Z + N
```

where:

- **A** = Mass Number
- **Z** = Atomic Number
- **N** = Number of Neutrons

---

## Number of Neutrons

```
N = A − Z
```

---

## Electronic Configuration

Electrons are arranged in shells according to their capacity.

### Examples

| Element   | Atomic Number | Electronic Configuration |
| --------- | ------------: | ------------------------ |
| Hydrogen  |             1 | 1                        |
| Helium    |             2 | 2                        |
| Lithium   |             3 | 2,1                      |
| Carbon    |             6 | 2,4                      |
| Oxygen    |             8 | 2,6                      |
| Sodium    |            11 | 2,8,1                    |
| Magnesium |            12 | 2,8,2                    |
| Chlorine  |            17 | 2,8,7                    |

---

## Valence Electrons

The electrons present in the outermost shell are called **valence electrons**.

They determine the chemical properties and bonding behavior of an atom.

# 4. Isotopes

## Definition

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element having:

- The same atomic number.
- Different mass numbers.

This is because they have different numbers of neutrons.

---

## Characteristics

- Same chemical properties.
- Different physical properties.
- Same number of electrons and protons.
- Different number of neutrons.

---

## Examples

### Hydrogen

| Isotope   | Symbol | Protons | Neutrons |
| --------- | ------ | ------- | -------- |
| Protium   | ¹H     | 1       | 0        |
| Deuterium | ²H     | 1       | 1        |
| Tritium   | ³H     | 1       | 2        |

---

### Carbon

- Carbon-12
- Carbon-13
- Carbon-14

---

### Chlorine

- Chlorine-37

# 5. Chemical Bonding

A **chemical bond** is the attractive force that holds atoms together in a molecule or compound.

Atoms form bonds to achieve a stable electronic configuration (octet or duplet).

---

## Octet Rule

Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to obtain **8 electrons** in their outermost shell.

Hydrogen and helium follow the **duplet rule** (2 electrons).

---

## Electrovalent (Ionic) Bond

An **electrovalent bond** is formed by the complete transfer of electrons from one atom to another.

### Formation of Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

```
Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
```

Result:

```
Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl
```

---

### Properties of Ionic Compounds

- High melting and boiling points.
- Conduct electricity in molten or aqueous state.
- Soluble in water.
- Hard and brittle.

---

## Covalent Bond

A **covalent bond** is formed by the sharing of electrons between atoms.

---

### Examples

#### Hydrogen Molecule

```
H + H → H₂
```

---

#### Oxygen Molecule

```
O + O → O₂
```

---

#### Water

```
H₂O
```

---

#### Methane

```
CH₄
```

---

### Properties of Covalent Compounds

- Low melting and boiling points.
- Poor conductors of electricity.
- Usually insoluble in water.
- Exist as gases, liquids, or soft solids.

---

# Difference Between Ionic and Covalent Compounds

| Ionic Compounds                                | Covalent Compounds             |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Formed by electron transfer                    | Formed by electron sharing     |
| Conduct electricity in molten or aqueous state | Do not conduct electricity     |
| High melting and boiling points                | Low melting and boiling points |
| Usually soluble in water                       | Usually insoluble in water     |
| Hard and brittle                               | Soft in nature                 |

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

### Question

Find the number of neutrons in **Chlorine-37**.

Atomic Number = 17

### Solution

```
N = A − Z

= 37 − 17

= 20
```

### Answer

```
20 Neutrons
```

---

## Example 2

### Question

Write the electronic configuration of Sodium.

Atomic Number = 11

### Solution

```
K = 2

L = 8

M = 1
```

### Answer

```
2,8,1
```

Electrovalent (Ionic) Bond

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **atomic number** with **mass number**.
- Assuming isotopes have different chemical properties.
- Forgetting that electrons equal protons only in neutral atoms.
- Mixing ionic and covalent bonding.
- Incorrect electronic configurations.
- Confusing valence electrons with total electrons.
- Assuming all compounds conduct electricity.
- Forgetting the octet rule while explaining bonding.

# Chapter Summary

- Atoms consist of **electrons**, **protons**, and **neutrons**.
- **Rutherford's model** introduced the nuclear atom, while **Bohr's model** explained electron arrangement in fixed shells.
- The **atomic number (Z)** is the number of protons, and the **mass number (A)** is the sum of protons and neutrons.
- Electrons are arranged in shells according to their capacities, and the outermost electrons are called **valence electrons**.
- **Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
- Atoms combine by forming **chemical bonds** to achieve stable electronic configurations.
- **Electrovalent (ionic) bonds** involve electron transfer, while **covalent bonds** involve electron sharing.
- Ionic and covalent compounds differ in their physical properties, electrical conductivity, and solubility.
- Understanding atomic structure and chemical bonding is fundamental to studying all branches of chemistry.
