# Introduction

**Polarisation** is a phenomenon that describes the transverse nature of light waves.

It explains the restriction of vibrations of light waves in a particular direction.

---

# 1. Transverse Nature of Light

Light is an electromagnetic transverse wave.

In an unpolarised light wave:

```
Vibrations occur in all directions
perpendicular to propagation
```

---

After polarisation:

```
Vibrations occur in only one direction
```

---

# 2. Unpolarised Light

## Definition

Light waves in which electric field vibrations occur in all possible directions perpendicular to the direction of propagation are called unpolarised light.

---

Examples:

- Sunlight
- Bulb light

---

# 3. Polarised Light

## Definition

Light whose vibrations are restricted to a single direction perpendicular to the direction of propagation is called plane polarised light.

---

# 4. Polarisation

## Definition

The phenomenon of converting unpolarised light into plane polarised light is called polarisation.

---

# Importance

Polarisation proves:

```
Light is a transverse wave
```

---

# 6. Polarisation by Reflection

When unpolarised light is incident on a transparent surface at a particular angle, reflected light becomes polarised.

---

The angle at which complete polarisation occurs is called:

```
Polarising angle
```

---

# 7. Brewster's Law

## Statement

The refractive index of a transparent medium is equal to the tangent of the polarising angle.

---

Formula:

\[
\mu=\tan i_p
\]

---

Where:

- μ = Refractive index
- ip = Polarising angle

---

# 8. Brewster Angle

## Definition

The angle of incidence at which reflected light becomes completely plane polarised is called Brewster angle.

---

At Brewster angle:

Reflected ray and refracted ray are:

\[
90^\circ
\]

apart.

---

Relation:

\[
i_p+r=90^\circ
\]

---

# 9. Polarisation by Double Refraction

## Definition

The splitting of light into two rays when it passes through certain crystals is called double refraction.

---

Discovered by:

```
Erasmus Bartholin
```

---

Example:

```
Calcite crystal
```

---

# 10. Ordinary Ray (O-Ray)

Properties:

- Obeys Snell's law
- Has constant velocity
- Refractive index is constant

---

# 11. Extraordinary Ray (E-Ray)

Properties:

- Does not obey ordinary Snell's law
- Velocity changes with direction
- Refractive index varies

---

# 12. Nicol Prism

## Definition

A Nicol prism is an optical device used to produce and analyse plane polarised light.

---

Made from:

```
Calcite crystal
```

---

# Principle:

Based on:

```
Double refraction and total internal reflection
```

---

---


# 15. Malus Law

## Statement

The intensity of plane polarised light transmitted through an analyser varies as the square of cosine of angle between polariser and analyser.

---

Formula:

\[
I=I_0\cos^2\theta
\]

---

Where:

- I₀ = Initial intensity
- I = Transmitted intensity
- θ = Angle between axes

---

# Special Cases

---

## θ = 0°

Axes parallel.

\[
I=I_0
\]

Maximum intensity.

---

## θ = 90°

Axes perpendicular.

\[
I=0
\]

No light passes.

---

Symbol:

\[

\]

---

# Laevorotatory

Rotates plane anticlockwise.

Symbol:

\[

\]

---

# 18. Specific Rotation

Formula:

\[
[\alpha]=
\frac{\theta}{lc}

---

| --------------------------------- | --------------------------- |

| Produced by ordinary sources      | Produced using polariser    |
| Cannot show complete polarisation | Shows polarisation          |

# Board Important Questions

## 1. What is polarisation?

**Answer:**

Polarisation is the phenomenon of restricting vibrations of light waves to one direction perpendicular to propagation.

---

## 2. Why does polarisation prove transverse nature of light?

**Answer:**

Only transverse waves can be polarised because their vibrations can be restricted to one direction.

---

## 3. State Brewster's law.

**Answer:**

The refractive index of a medium is equal to the tangent of the polarising angle.

\[
\mu=\tan i_p
\]

---

## 4. State Malus law.

**Answer:**

The intensity of transmitted light through an analyser is proportional to the square of cosine of angle between polariser and analyser.

\[
I=I_0\cos^2\theta
\]

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing polarisation with reflection.
- Forgetting that only transverse waves can be polarised.
- Mixing Brewster's law and Malus law.
- Confusing polariser and analyser.
- Forgetting double refraction produces two rays.
