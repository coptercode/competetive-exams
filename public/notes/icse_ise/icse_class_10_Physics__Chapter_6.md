# Introduction

A **spectrum** is the band of colours produced when white light is dispersed into its constituent colours. This phenomenon occurs because different colours of light have different wavelengths and are refracted by different amounts when passing through a prism. The chapter also covers the **electromagnetic spectrum, ultraviolet rays, infrared rays, and scattering of light**.

---

# 2. Dispersion of White Light

## Definition

**Dispersion** is the splitting of white light into its constituent colours when it passes through a glass prism.

---

## Cause of Dispersion

Different colours travel with different speeds inside the prism and hence suffer different amounts of refraction.

# 6. Ultraviolet Rays (UV)

## Definition

Ultraviolet rays are electromagnetic waves with wavelengths shorter than visible violet light.

---

## Properties

- Invisible.
- High energy.
- Cause fluorescence.
- Can kill bacteria.

---

## Uses

- Sterilization.
- Detecting forged currency.
- Water purification.
- Medical instruments.

---

## Harmful Effects

- Skin burns.
- Eye damage.
- Skin cancer.
- Sunburn.

---

# 7. Infrared Rays (IR)

## Definition

Infrared rays are electromagnetic waves with wavelengths longer than visible red light.

---

## Properties

- Invisible.
- Produce heating effect.
- Emitted by hot objects.

---

## Uses

- TV remote controls.
- Night vision devices.
- Thermal imaging.
- Physiotherapy.
- Weather forecasting.

---

# Difference Between UV and IR Rays

| Ultraviolet Rays | Infrared Rays   |
| ---------------- | --------------- |
| Short wavelength | Long wavelength |
| High frequency   | Low frequency   |
| Chemical effect  | Heating effect  |
| Beyond violet    | Beyond red      |

# 8. Scattering of Light

## Definition

**Scattering** is the phenomenon in which light is redirected in different directions after striking tiny particles.

---

# Rayleigh's Law

The intensity of scattering is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength.

## Formula

```
           1
I ∝ -------------
          λ⁴
```

Where,

- I = Intensity of scattering
- λ = Wavelength

---

## Applications of Scattering

### Blue Colour of Sky

Blue light has a shorter wavelength and is scattered more than red light.

---

### Red Colour of Sunrise and Sunset

Sunlight travels a longer distance through the atmosphere, scattering shorter wavelengths and allowing mainly red light to reach the observer.

---

### White Appearance of Clouds

Water droplets are much larger than the wavelength of visible light, so they scatter all colours nearly equally.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **dispersion** with **scattering**.
- Forgetting the correct **VIBGYOR** order.
- Assuming red light deviates the most (it is **violet**).
- Mixing up **ultraviolet** and **infrared** rays.
- Forgetting that **UV has shorter wavelength** and **IR has longer wavelength** than visible light.
- Writing Rayleigh's law incorrectly.
- Confusing the causes of **blue sky** and **red sunset**.

# Chapter Summary

- **White light** consists of seven colours that can be separated by a prism through the process of **dispersion**, forming the **visible spectrum (VIBGYOR)**.
- The separated colours can be combined again using a second prism in the process of **recombination**, producing white light.
- The **electromagnetic spectrum** includes all electromagnetic waves, ranging from **gamma rays** with the shortest wavelengths to **radio waves** with the longest wavelengths.
- **Ultraviolet rays** possess high energy and are used for sterilization and fluorescence, while **infrared rays** are associated with heat and are widely used in remote controls and thermal imaging.
- **Scattering of light**, explained by **Rayleigh's Law (I ∝ 1/λ⁴)**, is responsible for natural phenomena such as the **blue colour of the sky**, **red sunrise and sunset**, and the **white appearance of clouds**.
- The study of the spectrum is fundamental to optics and has numerous applications in **astronomy, medicine, communication, environmental science, and spectroscopy**.
