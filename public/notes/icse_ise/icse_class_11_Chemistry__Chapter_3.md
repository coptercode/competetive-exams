# Introduction

As the number of known elements increased, it became necessary to arrange them systematically. The **Modern Periodic Table**, based on the **Modern Periodic Law**, organizes elements according to their **atomic numbers**, revealing recurring (periodic) trends in their physical and chemical properties.

The periodic table helps predict the properties of known and newly discovered elements.

---

# 1. Need for Classification

## Reasons

- To study a large number of elements systematically.
- To compare properties of elements.
- To predict properties of unknown elements.
- To understand periodic trends.
- To simplify the study of chemistry.

---

# 2. Early Attempts at Classification

## Dobereiner's Triads

### Proposed By

```
Johann Dobereiner
```

### Features

- Elements grouped in threes.
- Middle element's atomic mass ≈ average of the other two.

Example

| Element | Atomic Mass |
| ------- | ----------- |
| Li      | 7           |
| Na      | 23          |
| K       | 39          |

---

### Limitations

- Applicable only to a few elements.

---

# Newlands' Law of Octaves

### Proposed By

```
John Newlands
```

### Statement

Every eighth element has properties similar to the first element.

---

### Limitations

- Valid only up to calcium.
- Failed for heavier elements.

---

# Mendeleev's Periodic Table

### Proposed By

```
Dmitri Mendeleev
```

---

### Periodic Law

The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their **atomic masses**.

---

### Merits

- Left gaps for undiscovered elements.
- Predicted properties of new elements.
- Corrected some atomic masses.

---

### Limitations

- Position of hydrogen uncertain.
- Could not explain isotopes.
- Atomic mass order anomalies.

---

# 3. Modern Periodic Law

### Proposed By

```
Henry Moseley
```

---

## Statement

The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their **atomic numbers**.

---

# Modern Periodic Table

## Features

- Arranged by increasing atomic number.
- Contains **7 periods**.
- Contains **18 groups**.
- Divided into s, p, d, and f blocks.

# 4. Periodic Properties

Periodic properties depend mainly on

- Atomic size
- Nuclear charge
- Shielding effect

---

# Effective Nuclear Charge

## Definition

The net positive charge experienced by valence electrons after accounting for shielding by inner-shell electrons.

---

# Shielding Effect

Inner electrons reduce the attractive force between the nucleus and valence electrons.

---

# 5. Atomic Radius

## Definition

Half the distance between the nuclei of two bonded identical atoms.

---

# Trend Across a Period

```
Decreases

→
```

Reason

- Increase in effective nuclear charge.
- Electrons pulled closer to nucleus.

---

# Trend Down a Group

```
Increases

↓

```

Reason

- Addition of new electron shells.

---

# Types of Atomic Radius

- Covalent radius
- Metallic radius
- Van der Waals radius

---

# Difference Between Atomic Radius and Ionic Radius

| Atomic Radius          | Ionic Radius      |
| ---------------------- | ----------------- |
| Neutral atom           | Ion               |
| Depends on atomic size | Depends on charge |

---

# 6. Ionic Radius

## Cations

Radius

```
Decreases
```

Reason

- Loss of electrons.
- Greater nuclear attraction.

---

## Anions

Radius

```
Increases
```

Reason

- Gain of electrons.
- Increased electron-electron repulsion.

---

# Difference Between Cation and Anion

| Cation            | Anion            |
| ----------------- | ---------------- |
| Positive          | Negative         |
| Smaller than atom | Larger than atom |
| Loses electrons   | Gains electrons  |

---

# 7. Ionisation Enthalpy (ΔᵢH)

## Definition

The minimum amount of energy required to remove the most loosely held electron from an isolated gaseous atom.

---

# Unit

```
kJ mol⁻¹
```

---

# Trend Across a Period

```
Increases

→
```

Reason

- Smaller atomic size.
- Higher nuclear attraction.

---

# Trend Down a Group

```
Decreases

↓

```

Reason

- Larger atomic size.
- Greater shielding effect.

---

# Factors Affecting Ionisation Enthalpy

- Atomic size.
- Nuclear charge.
- Shielding effect.
- Electronic configuration.

---

# 8. Electron Gain Enthalpy (ΔegH)

## Definition

The enthalpy change when an isolated gaseous atom gains an electron.

---

# Trend Across a Period

Becomes

```
More Negative

→
```

---

# Trend Down a Group

Generally becomes

```
Less Negative

↓

```

---

# Halogens

Have highly negative electron gain enthalpy.

---

# Noble Gases

Electron gain enthalpy is

```
Positive or nearly zero
```

due to stable electronic configuration.

---

# Difference Between Ionisation Enthalpy and Electron Gain Enthalpy

| Ionisation Enthalpy | Electron Gain Enthalpy  |
| ------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Electron removed    | Electron added          |
| Energy absorbed     | Usually energy released |
| Endothermic         | Mostly exothermic       |

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

### Question

Why does atomic radius decrease across a period?

### Solution

Effective nuclear charge increases while the number of shells remains the same, pulling electrons closer to the nucleus.

### Answer

Atomic radius decreases due to increased effective nuclear charge.

---

## Example 2

### Question

Which element has the highest electronegativity?

### Solution

Fluorine has the strongest attraction for shared electron pairs.

### Answer

Fluorine.

---

## Example 3

### Question

Arrange Na, Mg, and Al in increasing order of ionisation enthalpy.

### Solution

Ionisation enthalpy generally increases across a period.

```
Na < Mg < Al
```

_(Note: Experimental exceptions are discussed in advanced cases.)_

### Answer

Na < Mg < Al.

---

## Example 4

### Question

Why are cations smaller than their parent atoms?

### Solution

Loss of electrons reduces electron-electron repulsion and increases effective nuclear attraction.

### Answer

Cations have a smaller radius than their parent atoms.

---

## Example 5

### Question

Why are noble gases chemically inert?

### Solution

They possess completely filled valence shells, making them highly stable.

### Answer

Because they have stable electronic configurations.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **atomic radius** with **ionic radius**.
- Mixing up ionisation enthalpy and electron gain enthalpy.
- Assuming all periodic trends have no exceptions.
- Confusing groups with periods.
- Forgetting that electronegativity applies mainly to atoms in covalent bonds.
- Assuming hydrogen fits perfectly into one group.

# Chapter Summary

- The **Modern Periodic Table**, based on **Moseley's Modern Periodic Law**, classifies elements according to increasing atomic number, revealing recurring trends in their properties.
- Elements are arranged into **7 periods, 18 groups, and four blocks (s, p, d, and f)** based on their electronic configurations.
- Periodic properties such as **atomic radius, ionic radius, ionisation enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy, electronegativity, and metallic character** show predictable variations due to changes in effective nuclear charge and shielding effect.
- Understanding these trends helps explain the **chemical behavior, bonding patterns, and reactivity** of elements.
- The periodic table serves as a powerful tool for predicting the properties of known and unknown elements and forms the foundation for the study of inorganic chemistry.
