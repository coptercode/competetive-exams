# Introduction

Magnetism is the branch of physics that studies magnetic fields and their effects on moving charges and current-carrying conductors.

This unit covers:

- Magnetic fields
- Bar magnets
- Magnetic dipoles
- Magnetic properties of materials
- Biot-Savart law
- Ampere's law
- Lorentz force
- Moving coil galvanometer

Applications include:

- Electric motors
- Generators
- Speakers
- Measuring instruments
- Magnetic storage devices

---

# PART A: MAGNETIC FIELD

---

# 1. Magnetic Field

## Definition

The region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor where magnetic force can be experienced is called a magnetic field.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{B}
\]

Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Tesla (T)}}
\]

---

# Magnetic Field Lines

Properties:

1. They emerge from the north pole and enter the south pole outside a magnet.
2. Inside the magnet, they move from south to north.
3. They form closed loops.
4. Two magnetic field lines never intersect.
5. Density of lines represents field strength.

---

# 2. Magnetic Dipole

A pair of equal and opposite magnetic poles separated by a distance is called a magnetic dipole.

Examples:

- Bar magnet
- Current loop

---

# Magnetic Dipole Moment

For a bar magnet:

\[
\boxed{
M=m(2l)
}
\]

where:

- m = pole strength
- 2l = magnetic length

Unit:

\[
\boxed{Am^2}
\]

---

# 3. Magnetic Field Due to Bar Magnet

---

# Axial Position

Magnetic field:

\[
\boxed{
B=
\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}
\frac{2M}{r^3}
}
\]

---

# Equatorial Position

Magnetic field:

\[
\boxed{
B=
\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}
\frac{M}{r^3}
}
\]

Direction:

Opposite to magnetic moment.

---

# 4. Torque on Magnetic Dipole

When a magnetic dipole is placed in a uniform magnetic field:

\[
\boxed{
\tau=MB\sin\theta
}
\]

where:

- M = magnetic moment
- B = magnetic field
- θ = angle between M and B

---

Maximum torque:

\[
\boxed{
\tau_{max}=MB
}
\]

---

# Potential Energy of Magnetic Dipole

\[
\boxed{
U=-MB\cos\theta
}
\]

---

# PART B: MAGNETIC MATERIALS

---

# 5. Magnetic Properties of Materials

Materials are classified into:

1. Diamagnetic
2. Paramagnetic
3. Ferromagnetic

---

# 6. Diamagnetic Materials

## Definition

Materials weakly repelled by magnetic fields are called diamagnetic materials.

Examples:

- Copper
- Silver
- Gold
- Water

---

Properties:

- Magnetic susceptibility is negative.

\[
\boxed{
\chi<0
}
\]

- Weak repulsion
- No permanent magnetism

---

# 7. Paramagnetic Materials

## Definition

Materials weakly attracted by magnetic fields are called paramagnetic materials.

Examples:

- Aluminium
- Oxygen
- Platinum

---

Properties:

\[
\boxed{
\chi>0
}
\]

- Weak attraction
- Lose magnetism when field is removed

---

# 8. Ferromagnetic Materials

## Definition

Materials strongly attracted by magnetic fields and capable of retaining magnetism are called ferromagnetic materials.

Examples:

- Iron
- Cobalt
- Nickel

---

Properties:

- Large positive susceptibility.

\[
\boxed{
\chi\gg0
}
\]

- Form magnetic domains.
- Can become permanent magnets.

---

# 9. Curie Temperature

## Definition

The temperature above which a ferromagnetic substance loses its magnetism and becomes paramagnetic is called Curie temperature.

---

Example:

Iron:

\[
\approx770^\circ C
\]

---

# Comparison Table

| Property         | Diamagnetic | Paramagnetic     | Ferromagnetic      |
| ---------------- | ----------- | ---------------- | ------------------ |
| Attraction       | Repelled    | Weakly attracted | Strongly attracted |
| Susceptibility   | Negative    | Small positive   | Large positive     |
| Example          | Copper      | Aluminium        | Iron               |
| Permanent magnet | No          | No               | Yes                |

# PART C: BIOT-SAVART LAW

---

# 10. Biot-Savart Law

## Statement

The magnetic field produced by a small current element is directly proportional to:

- Current
- Length element
- Sine of angle

and inversely proportional to square of distance.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
dB=
\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}
\frac{Idl\sin\theta}{r^2}
}
\]

---

# 11. Magnetic Field Due to Straight Current Carrying Wire

For a long straight wire:

\[
\boxed{
B=
\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}
}
\]

---

Direction:

Given by:

\[
\boxed{\text{Right Hand Thumb Rule}}
\]

---

# 12. Magnetic Field Due to Circular Loop

At centre:

\[
\boxed{
B=
\frac{\mu_0NI}{2R}
}
\]

where:

- N = number of turns
- R = radius

---

# 13. Magnetic Field Inside Solenoid

For a long solenoid:

\[
\boxed{
B=\mu_0nI
}
\]

where:

- n = turns per unit length

---

# PART D: AMPERE'S LAW

---

# 14. Ampere's Circuital Law

## Statement

The line integral of magnetic field around a closed path equals μ₀ times current enclosed.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\oint B\cdot dl=\mu_0I
}
\]

---

Applications:

- Infinite straight wire
- Solenoid
- Toroid

---

# PART E: FORCE ON MOVING CHARGES

---

# 15. Lorentz Force

A moving charge in electric and magnetic fields experiences force.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
F=q(E+v\times B)
}
\]

---

For magnetic field only:

\[
\boxed{
F=qvB\sin\theta
}
\]

---

# Cases

If:

\[
\theta=0^\circ
\]

then:

\[
F=0
\]

---

If:

\[
\theta=90^\circ
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
F=qvB
}
\]

---

# 16. Motion of Charged Particle in Magnetic Field

The particle moves in a circular path.

Radius:

\[
\boxed{
r=\frac{mv}{qB}
}
\]

---

Time period:

\[
\boxed{
T=\frac{2\pi m}{qB}
}
\]

---

# 17. Force on Current Carrying Conductor

A conductor carrying current in magnetic field experiences force.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
F=BIL\sin\theta
}
\]

---

Direction:

Fleming's Left Hand Rule.

---

# Force Between Parallel Conductors

\[
\boxed{
\frac FL=
\frac{\mu_0I_1I_2}{2\pi d}
}
\]

---

# PART F: MOVING COIL GALVANOMETER

---

# 18. Principle

A current-carrying coil placed in a magnetic field experiences torque.

---

Torque:

\[
\boxed{
\tau=NIAB
}
\]

where:

- N = number of turns
- I = current
- A = area
- B = magnetic field

---

# 19. Current Sensitivity

Current sensitivity:

\[
\boxed{
S_i=\frac{\theta}{I}
}
\]

---

# 20. Conversion of Galvanometer

---

# Galvanometer to Ammeter

A low resistance shunt is connected in parallel.

---

# Galvanometer to Voltmeter

A high resistance is connected in series.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing magnetic field with magnetic flux.
- Forgetting direction rules.
- Using cosine instead of sine in force equations.
- Mixing electric dipole and magnetic dipole moments.
- Forgetting that magnetic force does no work.
- Using wrong formula for axial and equatorial fields.
- Confusing diamagnetic, paramagnetic, and ferromagnetic properties.

---

# Unit Summary

- Magnetism describes forces due to magnetic fields.
- Current produces magnetic fields.
- Biot-Savart law calculates magnetic fields from currents.
- Ampere's law simplifies symmetric magnetic field calculations.
- Moving charges experience Lorentz force.
- Magnetic materials have different responses to magnetic fields.
- Galvanometers measure small currents and can be converted into ammeters and voltmeters.

\[
\boxed{
F=qvB\sin\theta
}
\]

is the fundamental equation describing magnetic force on moving charges.
