# PART A: ATOMIC STRUCTURE

---

# 1. Discovery of Electron

Electron was discovered by:

\[
\boxed{\text{J.J. Thomson}}
\]

using cathode ray experiments.

---

# 2. Thomson Atomic Model

## Description

Atom was considered as a positively charged sphere containing electrons embedded inside it.

Also called:

\[
\boxed{\text{Plum pudding model}}
\]

---

# Limitations

- Could not explain atomic stability.
- Failed to explain scattering experiments.

---

# PART B: RUTHERFORD ATOMIC MODEL

---

# 3. Rutherford's Alpha Scattering Experiment

## Experiment

Alpha particles were allowed to fall on a thin gold foil.

Observations:

1. Most particles passed straight through.
2. Some particles were slightly deflected.
3. Very few particles bounced back.

---

# Conclusions

1. Atom is mostly empty space.
2. Positive charge is concentrated in a small nucleus.
3. Almost all mass is concentrated in nucleus.

---

# Limitations

According to classical theory:

- Electrons moving around nucleus should lose energy.
- They should spiral into nucleus.

Therefore, Rutherford model could not explain atomic stability.

---

# PART C: BOHR ATOMIC MODEL

---

# 4. Bohr's Postulates

---

## Postulate 1: Stationary Orbits

Electrons revolve around nucleus only in certain permitted orbits without radiating energy.

---

## Postulate 2: Quantisation of Angular Momentum

\[
\boxed{
mvr=\frac{nh}{2\pi}
}
\]

where:

- n = principal quantum number

---

## Postulate 3: Radiation of Energy

Energy is emitted or absorbed when electrons jump between orbits.

\[
\boxed{
h\nu=E_2-E_1
}
\]

---

# 5. Radius of Bohr Orbit

For hydrogen atom:

\[
\boxed{
r_n=
\frac{n^2h^2}
{4\pi^2me^2}
}
\]

Simplified:

\[
\boxed{
r_n=n^2a_0
}
\]

where:

\[
a_0=0.529\text{ Å}
\]

---

# 6. Velocity of Electron

\[
\boxed{
v_n=
\frac{2.18\times10^6}{n}
m/s
}
\]

---

# 7. Energy of Electron

For hydrogen:

\[
\boxed{
E_n=
-\frac{13.6}{n^2}eV
}
\]

---

For ground state:

\[
n=1
\]

\[
\boxed{
E=-13.6eV
}
\]

---

# 8. Ionisation Energy

## Definition

Minimum energy required to remove electron completely from an atom is called ionisation energy.

For hydrogen:

\[
\boxed{
13.6eV
}
\]

# PART D: HYDROGEN SPECTRUM

---

# 9. Hydrogen Spectrum

When hydrogen atoms are excited, they emit light of specific wavelengths forming spectral lines.

---

# 10. Rydberg Formula

\[
\boxed{
\frac1\lambda
=

R
\left(
\frac1{n_1^2}
-

\frac1{n_2^2}
\right)
}
\]

where:

- R = Rydberg constant
- \(n_2>n_1\)

---

# PART E: NUCLEAR STRUCTURE

---

# 11. Atomic Nucleus

The nucleus contains:

- Protons
- Neutrons

Together called:

\[
\boxed{\text{Nucleons}}
\]

---

# Nuclear Notation

\[
\boxed{
^A_ZX
}
\]

where:

- Z = atomic number
- A = mass number

---

# 12. Nuclear Forces

## Definition

The force that holds nucleons together inside the nucleus is called nuclear force.

---

Properties:

1. Very strong.
2. Short range.
3. Charge independent.
4. Saturation property.

---

# PART F: MASS DEFECT AND BINDING ENERGY

---

# 13. Mass Defect

## Definition

The difference between the mass of separated nucleons and actual nuclear mass is called mass defect.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta m=
(Zm_p+Nm_n)-M
}
\]

---

# 14. Binding Energy

## Definition

The energy required to separate a nucleus completely into nucleons is called binding energy.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
BE=\Delta mc^2
}
\]

---

In MeV:

\[
\boxed{
BE=\Delta m\times931.5MeV
}
\]

---

# 15. Binding Energy Per Nucleon

\[
\boxed{
\frac{BE}{A}
}
\]

It represents nuclear stability.

---

# Binding Energy Curve

Features:

- Increases for light nuclei.
- Maximum near iron.
- Decreases for very heavy nuclei.

---

Most stable nucleus:

\[
\boxed{\text{Iron }(^{56}Fe)}
\]

---

# PART G: NUCLEAR FISSION

---

# 16. Nuclear Fission

## Definition

The splitting of a heavy nucleus into smaller nuclei with release of energy is called nuclear fission.

---

Example:

\[
^{235}U+n
\rightarrow
\text{Fragments}+Energy
\]

---

# Chain Reaction

A neutron released from one fission causes further fissions.

---

Applications:

- Nuclear reactors
- Atomic bombs

---

# Nuclear Reactor Components

1. Fuel
2. Moderator
3. Control rods
4. Coolant

---

# PART H: NUCLEAR FUSION

---

# 17. Nuclear Fusion

## Definition

The combination of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus with release of energy is called fusion.

---

Example:

Hydrogen nuclei combine to form helium.

---

# Conditions Required

- Extremely high temperature
- High pressure

---

# Source of Energy in Stars

\[
\boxed{\text{Nuclear fusion}}
\]

---

# Comparison: Fission vs Fusion

| Fission                 | Fusion                    |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Heavy nucleus splits    | Light nuclei combine      |
| Used in reactors        | Occurs in stars           |
| Chain reaction possible | Requires high temperature |
| Example: Uranium        | Example: Hydrogen         |

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing atomic number and mass number.
- Forgetting negative sign in Bohr energy equation.
- Mixing mass defect and mass number.
- Forgetting that binding energy represents nuclear stability.
- Confusing fission and fusion processes.
- Using wrong hydrogen spectral series.
- Forgetting that iron has maximum binding energy per nucleon.

- Nuclear forces bind nucleons together.

- Fusion releases energy by combining light nuclei.
