Applications include:

# PART A: ELECTRIC CURRENT

---

# 1. Electric Current

## Definition

The rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor is called electric current.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac{Q}{t}
}
\]

where:

- I = current
- Q = charge
- t = time

---

# SI Unit

\[
\boxed{\text{Ampere (A)}}
\]

---

# Direction of Current

Conventional current flows:

\[
\boxed{
\text{From positive terminal to negative terminal}
}
\]

Electron flow is opposite:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Negative to positive}
}
\]

---

# 2. Current Density

## Definition

Current flowing per unit cross-sectional area of conductor is called current density.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
J=\frac IA
}
\]

Unit:

\[
\boxed{A/m^2}
\]

---

# 3. Drift Velocity

## Definition

The average velocity acquired by free electrons in a conductor due to an applied electric field is called drift velocity.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{v_d}
\]

---

Without electric field:

Electrons move randomly.

With electric field:

Electrons acquire a small average velocity.

---

# Relation Between Current and Drift Velocity

\[
\boxed{
I=neAv_d
}
\]

where:

- n = number density of electrons
- e = charge of electron
- A = area of conductor

---

# 4. Mobility

## Definition

Mobility is the drift velocity produced per unit electric field.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\mu=\frac{v_d}{E}
}
\]

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
v_d=\mu E
}
\]

---

# PART B: OHM'S LAW AND RESISTANCE

---

# 5. Ohm's Law

## Statement

At constant temperature, current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it.

\[
\boxed{
V\propto I
}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
V=IR
}
\]

---

where:

- V = potential difference
- I = current
- R = resistance

---

# V-I Graph

For an ohmic conductor:

- Straight line
- Passes through origin

---

# 6. Resistance

## Definition

Resistance is the opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of current.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
R=\frac VI
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{\Omega}
\]

---

# 7. Resistivity

## Definition

Resistance of a conductor having unit length and unit area of cross-section is called resistivity.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\rho=\frac{RA}{l}
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{\Omega m}
\]

---

# Factors Affecting Resistance

\[
\boxed{
R=\rho\frac lA
}
\]

Resistance increases with:

- Length
- Temperature

Resistance decreases with:

- Larger area

---

# 8. Temperature Dependence of Resistance

For small temperature changes:

\[
\boxed{
R_t=R_0(1+\alpha\Delta T)
}
\]

where:

- \(R_0\) = resistance at 0°C
- α = temperature coefficient

---

# Temperature Coefficient

\[
\boxed{
\alpha=
\frac{R_t-R_0}{R_0T}
}
\]

---

# PART C: CELLS AND CIRCUITS

---

# 9. Electromotive Force (EMF)

## Definition

The energy supplied by a cell per unit charge is called EMF.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
E=\frac WQ
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{Volt}
\]

---

# 10. Internal Resistance

A cell has resistance inside it called internal resistance.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed r
\]

---

# Terminal Voltage

When current flows:

\[
\boxed{
V=E-Ir
}
\]

---

# Current in Circuit

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac E{R+r}
}
\]

where:

- R = external resistance
- r = internal resistance

---

# PART D: KIRCHHOFF'S LAWS

---

# 11. Kirchhoff's First Law

## Junction Rule

## Statement

The algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero.

\[
\boxed{
\sum I=0
}
\]

---

Based on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Conservation of charge}}
\]

---

# 12. Kirchhoff's Second Law

## Loop Rule

## Statement

The algebraic sum of potential differences in a closed loop is zero.

\[
\boxed{
\sum V=0
}
\]

---

Based on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Conservation of energy}}
\]

---

# Sign Convention

Moving through a resistor:

- Along current:

\[
-I R
\]

- Opposite current:

\[
+IR
\]

---

# PART E: WHEATSTONE BRIDGE

---

# 13. Wheatstone Bridge

A device used to measure unknown resistance.

Condition for balance:

\[
\boxed{
\frac PR=\frac QS
}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{
P S=Q R
}
\]

---

At balance:

\[
\boxed{
I_g=0
}
\]

No current flows through galvanometer.

---

# 14. Meter Bridge

A practical form of Wheatstone bridge.

Balance condition:

\[
\boxed{
\frac XR=
\frac l{100-l}
}
\]

where:

- X = unknown resistance
- R = known resistance
- l = balancing length

---

# PART F: POTENTIOMETER

---

# 15. Principle of Potentiometer

The potential drop across a uniform wire is directly proportional to its length.

\[
\boxed{
V\propto l
}
\]

---

# Potential Gradient

\[
\boxed{
k=\frac Vl
}
\]

---

# Comparison of EMF

For two cells:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{E_1}{E_2}
=

\frac{l_1}{l_2}
}
\]

---

# Applications

1. Comparing EMF of cells.
2. Measuring internal resistance.
3. Measuring potential difference.

---

# PART G: ELECTRIC POWER

---

# 16. Electric Power

## Definition

Rate at which electrical energy is consumed.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
P=VI
}
\]

---

Using Ohm's law:

\[
\boxed{
P=I^2R
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
P=\frac{V^2}{R}
}
\]

---

# Electrical Energy

\[
\boxed{
W=Pt
}
\]

---

Commercial Unit:

\[
\boxed{kWh}
\]

---

# 17. Joule's Heating Law

## Statement

Heat produced in a resistor is directly proportional to:

- Square of current
- Resistance
- Time

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
H=I^2Rt
}
\]

---

# PART H: THERMOELECTRIC EFFECT

---

# 18. Thermoelectric Effect

The phenomenon of producing electrical potential due to temperature difference is called thermoelectric effect.

---

# Seebeck Effect

## Definition

When two dissimilar metals form a closed circuit and their junctions are maintained at different temperatures, an EMF is produced.

---

# Thermocouple

A device based on Seebeck effect.

Applications:

- Temperature measurement
- Thermal sensors

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing current direction with electron flow.
- Forgetting current is a scalar quantity.
- Using resistance instead of resistivity.
- Applying Kirchhoff signs incorrectly.
- Forgetting internal resistance of a cell.
- Mixing EMF and terminal voltage.
- Using wrong units in power calculations.
- Forgetting potentiometer works on potential gradient.
