# Introduction

Wave optics deals with the **wave nature of light**.

It explains phenomena that cannot be explained by ray optics:

- Interference
- Diffraction
- Polarisation

Wave optics proves that light behaves as a transverse wave.

Applications:

- Optical instruments
- Holography
- Polarising devices
- Spectroscopy
- Communication technology

---

# PART A: WAVE THEORY OF LIGHT

---

# 1. Huygens' Principle

## Statement

Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets which spread in all directions with the speed of light.

---

# Wavefront

## Definition

A surface joining all points having the same phase of vibration is called a wavefront.

---

# Types of Wavefronts

## 1. Spherical Wavefront

Produced by:

- Point source of light

---

## 2. Plane Wavefront

Produced when:

- Source is very far away

---

## 3. Cylindrical Wavefront

Produced by:

- Line source

---

# PART B: INTERFERENCE OF LIGHT

---

# 2. Interference

## Definition

The phenomenon of redistribution of intensity due to superposition of two light waves is called interference.

---

# Principle of Superposition

When two waves overlap:

\[
\boxed{
y=y_1+y_2
}
\]

---

# Conditions for Sustained Interference

Two sources must be:

1. Coherent
2. Same frequency
3. Constant phase difference
4. Nearly equal intensity

---

# Coherent Sources

## Definition

Sources having the same frequency and constant phase difference are called coherent sources.

---

# Types of Interference

## 1. Constructive Interference

When two waves meet in same phase.

Condition:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta=n\lambda
}
\]

Intensity is maximum.

---

## 2. Destructive Interference

When two waves meet in opposite phase.

Condition:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta=(2n+1)\frac{\lambda}{2}
}
\]

Intensity is minimum.

---

# PART C: YOUNG'S DOUBLE SLIT EXPERIMENT (YDSE)

---

# 3. Young's Double Slit Experiment

Performed by:

\[
\boxed{\text{Thomas Young}}
\]

It proved the wave nature of light.

---

# Arrangement

Two coherent sources:

\[
S_1,S_2
\]

are separated by distance:

\[
d
\]

Screen distance:

\[
D
\]

where:

\[
D>>d
\]

---

# Path Difference

For any point on screen:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta=\frac{dx}{D}
}
\]

---

# 4. Fringe Width

## Definition

The distance between two successive bright or dark fringes is called fringe width.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{\beta}
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\beta=\frac{\lambda D}{d}
}
\]

where:

- λ = wavelength
- D = distance between screen and slit
- d = slit separation

---

# 5. Position of Bright Fringes

Condition:

\[
\boxed{
x_n=n\beta
}
\]

where:

\[
n=0,1,2,...
\]

---

# 6. Position of Dark Fringes

Condition:

\[
\boxed{
x_n=
(2n+1)\frac{\beta}{2}
}
\]

---

# 7. Intensity in Interference

Intensity:

\[
\boxed{
I=I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\phi
}
\]

---

For equal intensities:

Maximum intensity:

\[
\boxed{
I_{max}=4I_0
}
\]

Minimum intensity:

\[
\boxed{
I_{min}=0
}
\]

---

# PART D: DIFFRACTION OF LIGHT

---

# 8. Diffraction

## Definition

The bending of light around obstacles and spreading into the region of shadow is called diffraction.

---

Diffraction is significant when:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Obstacle size}\approx\lambda
}
\]

---

# 9. Single Slit Diffraction

When light passes through a narrow slit, diffraction pattern is formed.

---

# Condition for Minima

\[
\boxed{
a\sin\theta=n\lambda
}
\]

where:

- a = slit width
- n = order of minima

---

# 10. Width of Central Maximum

Angular width:

\[
\boxed{
\theta=\frac{2\lambda}{a}
}
\]

---

Linear width:

\[
\boxed{
W=
\frac{2D\lambda}{a}
}
\]

---

# Comparison: Interference vs Diffraction

| Interference                        | Diffraction                          |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Due to two coherent sources         | Due to bending around obstacle       |
| Bright fringes equally spaced       | Central maximum is widest            |
| Fringes have nearly equal intensity | Intensity decreases away from centre |

---

# PART E: POLARISATION

---

# 11. Polarisation

## Definition

The phenomenon of restricting vibrations of light waves to one direction is called polarisation.

---

It proves:

\[
\boxed{\text{Light is a transverse wave}}
\]

---

# 12. Unpolarised Light

Light vibrations occur in all directions perpendicular to propagation.

---

# 13. Plane Polarised Light

Light vibrations occur only in one direction.

---

# 14. Polaroids

## Definition

A polaroid is a material that allows vibrations only in one direction.

---

Applications:

- Sunglasses
- Camera filters
- LCD displays
- Stress analysis

---

# 15. Malus' Law

## Statement

The intensity of plane polarised light transmitted through an analyser is proportional to the square of cosine of angle between transmission axes.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
I=I_0\cos^2\theta
}
\]

---

# 16. Brewster's Law

## Statement

At a particular angle of incidence, the reflected light becomes completely polarised.

This angle is called polarising angle.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\mu=\tan i_p
}
\]

where:

- μ = refractive index
- \(i_p\) = polarising angle

---

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

In YDSE:

\[
\lambda=600nm
\]

\[
D=2m
\]

\[
d=0.5mm
\]

Find fringe width.

Formula:

\[
\beta=\frac{\lambda D}{d}
\]

Convert:

\[
\lambda=600\times10^{-9}m
\]

\[
d=0.5\times10^{-3}m
\]

\[
\beta=
\frac{600\times10^{-9}\times2}
{0.5\times10^{-3}}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\beta=2.4\times10^{-3}m
}
\]

---

## Example 2

Find intensity after analyser:

\[
I_0=100W/m^2
\]

\[
\theta=60^\circ
\]

Using Malus law:

\[
I=I_0\cos^2\theta
\]

\[
=100(\frac12)^2
\]

\[
\boxed{
I=25W/m^2
}
\]

---

## Example 3

Find polarising angle for glass:

\[
\mu=1.5
\]

Using Brewster law:

\[
\mu=\tan i_p
\]

\[
\tan i_p=1.5
\]

\[
\boxed{
i_p\approx56.3^\circ
}
\]

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing interference and diffraction.
- Forgetting that interference requires coherent sources.
- Using wrong fringe width formula.
- Confusing bright and dark fringe conditions.
- Forgetting that polarisation proves transverse nature of light.
- Applying Malus law without considering angle between axes.
- Mixing wavelength units.
