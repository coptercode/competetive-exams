# Introduction

Chemistry is the branch of science that studies the composition, structure, properties and transformations of matter.

This chapter introduces the fundamental concepts required for chemical calculations.

Topics covered:

- Classification of matter
- Atomic and molecular concepts
- Mole concept
- Molar mass
- Chemical equations
- Stoichiometry
- Limiting reagent
- Equivalent mass

---

# PART A: CLASSIFICATION OF MATTER

---

# 1. Matter

## Definition

Anything that has mass and occupies space is called matter.

---

Examples:

- Water
- Air
- Metals
- Minerals

---

# 2. Classification of Matter

Matter is classified into:

1. Pure substances
2. Mixtures

---

# PART B: PURE SUBSTANCES

---

# 3. Pure Substance

## Definition

A substance having fixed composition and definite properties is called a pure substance.

---

Types:

1. Elements
2. Compounds

---

# 4. Elements

## Definition

A substance that cannot be broken into simpler substances by chemical methods is called an element.

---

Examples:

- Hydrogen (H)
- Oxygen (O)
- Iron (Fe)

---

Types:

### Metals

Examples:

- Iron
- Copper
- Sodium

---

### Non-metals

Examples:

- Carbon
- Sulphur
- Oxygen

---

### Metalloids

Examples:

- Silicon
- Boron

---

# 5. Compounds

## Definition

A compound is a pure substance formed by chemical combination of two or more elements in a fixed ratio.

---

Examples:

Water:

\[
H_2O
\]

Carbon dioxide:

\[
CO_2
\]

---

Properties:

- Fixed composition
- New properties different from elements
- Can be separated chemically

---

# PART C: MIXTURES

---

# 6. Mixture

## Definition

A mixture is a combination of two or more substances physically mixed together.

---

Properties:

- Variable composition
- Components retain their properties
- Can be separated physically

---

# Types of Mixtures

---

## 1. Homogeneous Mixture

Uniform composition throughout.

Examples:

- Salt solution
- Air

---

## 2. Heterogeneous Mixture

Non-uniform composition.

Examples:

- Sand and water
- Oil and water

---

# PART D: ATOMIC MASS AND MOLECULAR MASS

---

# 7. Atomic Mass

## Definition

The relative mass of an atom compared with 1/12th mass of carbon-12 atom is called atomic mass.

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{u}
\]

---

Examples:

Hydrogen:

\[
1u
\]

Oxygen:

\[
16u
\]

Carbon:

\[
12u
\]

---

# 8. Molecular Mass

## Definition

The sum of atomic masses of all atoms present in a molecule.

---

Example:

Water:

\[
H_2O
\]

\[
=2(1)+16
\]

\[
\boxed{18u}
\]

---

# 9. Formula Mass

Used for ionic compounds.

Example:

NaCl:

\[
=23+35.5
\]

\[
\boxed{58.5u}
\]

---

# PART E: MOLE CONCEPT

---

# 10. Mole

## Definition

A mole is the amount of substance containing \(6.022\times10^{23}\) particles.

---

Avogadro number:

\[
\boxed{
N_A=6.022\times10^{23}
}
\]

---

One mole may contain:

- Atoms
- Molecules
- Ions
- Electrons

---

# 11. Molar Mass

## Definition

The mass of one mole of a substance is called molar mass.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Molar\ mass=
\frac{Mass}{Number\ of\ moles}
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{g/mol}
\]

---

# 12. Number of Moles

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
n=\frac{Given\ mass}{Molar\ mass}
}
\]

---

# 13. Number of Particles

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Number\ of\ particles
=

n\times N_A
}
\]

---

# PART F: PERCENTAGE COMPOSITION

---

# 14. Percentage Composition

The percentage of each element present in a compound is called percentage composition.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\%\ of\ element=
\frac{Mass\ of\ element}
{Molecular\ mass}
\times100
}
\]

---

Example:

Water:

Molecular mass:

\[
18
\]

Hydrogen mass:

\[
2
\]

Percentage:

\[
\frac2{18}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{11.11\%}
\]

---

# PART G: CHEMICAL EQUATIONS

---

# 15. Chemical Equation

## Definition

A symbolic representation of a chemical reaction is called a chemical equation.

---

Example:

\[
H_2+O_2\rightarrow H_2O
\]

---

# 16. Balancing Chemical Equations

According to the law of conservation of mass:

Atoms cannot be created or destroyed.

Number of atoms on both sides must be equal.

---

Example:

Unbalanced:

\[
H_2+O_2\rightarrow H_2O
\]

Balanced:

\[
\boxed{
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O
}
\]

---

# PART H: STOICHIOMETRY

---

# 17. Stoichiometry

## Definition

The quantitative relationship between reactants and products in a chemical reaction is called stoichiometry.

---

Based on:

- Balanced chemical equation
- Mole ratios

---

Example:

\[
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O
\]

Ratio:

\[
2:1:2
\]

---

# 18. Limiting Reagent

## Definition

The reactant that gets completely consumed first and limits product formation is called limiting reagent.

---

Example:

If:

\[
2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O
\]

If hydrogen is insufficient:

Hydrogen is limiting reagent.

---

# 19. Excess Reagent

The reactant left after completion of reaction is called excess reagent.

---

# PART I: EQUIVALENT MASS

---

# 20. Equivalent Mass

## Definition

Equivalent mass is the mass of a substance that combines with or replaces one equivalent of another substance.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Equivalent\ mass=
\frac{Molar\ mass}{n-factor}
}
\]

---

# 21. Equivalent Mass of Acids

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Eq.\ mass=
\frac{Molecular\ mass}
{Basicity}
}
\]

---

Examples:

HCl:

Basicity = 1

\[
Eq.mass=36.5
\]

---

H₂SO₄:

Basicity = 2

\[
Eq.mass=
\frac{98}{2}
\]

\[
=49
\]

---

# 22. Equivalent Mass of Bases

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Eq.mass=
\frac{Molecular\ mass}
{Acidity}
}
\]

---

Example:

NaOH:

\[
Eq.mass=40
\]

---

# 23. Equivalent Mass of Salts

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Eq.mass=
\frac{Molar\ mass}
{Total\ positive\ charge}
}
\]

---

- Confusing atomic mass and molecular mass.
- Forgetting Avogadro number.
- Not balancing equations before calculations.
- Using incorrect molar mass.

# Introduction

The quantum mechanical model explains the structure of atoms based on the principles of quantum theory.

It describes:

- Arrangement of electrons
- Nature of orbitals
- Energy levels
- Electronic configuration

This chapter covers:

- Sub-atomic particles
- Atomic models

---

# PART A: SUB-ATOMIC PARTICLES

---

# 1. Fundamental Particles of Atom

Atoms are made up of three major particles:

| Particle | Charge | Mass            |
| -------- | ------ | --------------- |
| Electron | -1     | 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg |
| Proton   | +1     | 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg |
| Neutron  | 0      | 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg |

---

# 2. Discovery of Electron

## J.J. Thomson

Electron was discovered using cathode ray discharge tube experiments.

---

Properties of electrons:

- Negatively charged
- Present outside nucleus
- Same mass in all atoms

---

# 3. Discovery of Proton

## Goldstein

Observed positive rays (canal rays).

---

Proton:

- Positively charged
- Present in nucleus

---

# 4. Discovery of Neutron

## James Chadwick

Discovered neutron in 1932.

---

Properties:

- No charge
- Present in nucleus

---

# PART B: ATOMIC MODELS

---

# 5. Thomson's Atomic Model

## Statement

Atom is a positively charged sphere containing electrons embedded in it.

---

Also called:

\[
\boxed{\text{Plum pudding model}}
\]

---

Limitations:

- Could not explain nucleus.
- Failed to explain scattering experiments.

---

# 6. Rutherford Atomic Model

Based on alpha particle scattering experiment.

---

Observations:

1. Most particles passed straight.
2. Few particles deviated.
3. Very few bounced back.

---

Conclusions:

- Atom has a small dense nucleus.
- Most of atom is empty space.
- Electrons revolve around nucleus.

---

Limitations:

Could not explain:

- Stability of atom
- Line spectrum

---

# 7. Bohr Atomic Model

## Postulates

---

## 1. Stationary Orbits

Electrons revolve around nucleus in fixed energy levels.

---

Energy levels:

\[
K,L,M,N...
\]

or

\[
n=1,2,3,4...
\]

---

## 2. Quantisation of Energy

Electrons absorb or emit energy only when they jump between energy levels.

---

Energy difference:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta E=E_2-E_1
}
\]

---

## 3. Angular Momentum Quantisation

\[
\boxed{
mvr=\frac{nh}{2\pi}
}
\]

---

# Limitations of Bohr Model

- Works only for hydrogen-like atoms.
- Cannot explain spectra of multi-electron atoms.
- Cannot explain splitting of spectral lines.

---

# PART C: DUAL NATURE OF MATTER

---

# 8. Wave Nature of Matter

According to de Broglie:

Every moving particle has wave nature.

---

# 9. de Broglie Equation

Wavelength:

\[
\boxed{
\lambda=\frac hp
}
\]

Since:

\[
p=mv
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\lambda=\frac h{mv}
}
\]

---

where:

- h = Planck's constant
- m = mass
- v = velocity

---

# Importance

Explains:

- Electron diffraction
- Wave behaviour of particles

---

# PART D: HEISENBERG UNCERTAINTY PRINCIPLE

---

# 10. Uncertainty Principle

## Statement

It is impossible to determine simultaneously the exact position and momentum of an electron.

---

Mathematical expression:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta x\Delta p\geq\frac h{4\pi}
}
\]

---

where:

- Δx = uncertainty in position
- Δp = uncertainty in momentum

---

# Significance

- Electron cannot have a fixed path.
- Led to concept of orbitals.

---

# PART E: SCHRÖDINGER EQUATION

---

# 11. Wave Mechanical Model

According to Schrödinger:

Electrons are described by wave functions.

---

Schrödinger equation:

\[
\boxed{
H\psi=E\psi
}
\]

---

where:

- H = Hamiltonian operator
- ψ = wave function
- E = energy

---

# 12. Wave Function

The wave function describes the state of an electron.

---

Probability of finding electron:

\[
\boxed{
\psi^2
}
\]

---

# PART F: QUANTUM NUMBERS

---

Quantum numbers describe the position and energy of electrons in an atom.

There are four quantum numbers:

1. Principal quantum number
2. Azimuthal quantum number
3. Magnetic quantum number
4. Spin quantum number

---

Represents:

- Main energy level
- Size of orbital

---

Values:

\[
\boxed{

}

---

Shell names:

| n value | Shell |
| ------- | ----- |
| 1       | K     |
| 2       | L     |
| 3       | M     |
| 4       | N     |

---

Maximum electrons:

\[
\boxed{

}
\]

---

# 15. Azimuthal Quantum Number (l)

Represents:

- Subshell
- Shape of orbital

---

Values:

\[
\boxed{
l=0\text{ to }(n-1)
}
\]

---

Subshells:

| l   | Subshell |
| --- | -------- |
| 0   | s        |
| 1   | p        |
| 2   | d        |
| 3   | f        |

---

# 16. Magnetic Quantum Number (m)

Represents orientation of orbital.

---

Values:

\[
\boxed{
m=-l\text{ to }+l
}
\]

---

Number of orbitals:

\[
\boxed{
2l+1
}
\]

---

# 17. Spin Quantum Number (s)

Represents direction of electron spin.

---

Values:

\[
\boxed{
+\frac12,\ -\frac12
}
\]

---

# Introduction

Periodic classification is the arrangement of elements in a systematic order based on their properties.

The modern periodic table helps us understand:

- Arrangement of elements
- Similarities among elements
- Periodic trends
- Chemical behaviour

This chapter covers:

- Historical classification
- Mendeleev periodic table
- Modern periodic law
- Periods and groups
- Periodic properties

---

# PART A: HISTORICAL CLASSIFICATION OF ELEMENTS

---

# 1. Need for Classification

As the number of discovered elements increased, scientists needed a method to arrange them systematically.

Classification helps to:

- Study properties easily
- Predict properties of unknown elements
- Understand relationships among elements

---

# 2. Dobereiner's Triads

## Definition

Dobereiner grouped elements into sets of three having similar properties called triads.

---

Example:

Lithium (Li)

Sodium (Na)

Potassium (K)

---

Relationship:

Atomic mass of middle element:

\[
\boxed{
=

\frac{
\text{Atomic mass of first + Atomic mass of third}
}{2}
}
\]

---

Limitations:

- Only few elements could be arranged.
- Not applicable to all elements.

---

# 3. Newlands' Law of Octaves

## Statement

When elements are arranged in increasing order of atomic masses, every eighth element has properties similar to the first element.

---

Example:

Li → Be → B → C → N → O → F → Na

---

Limitations:

- Worked only up to calcium.
- Some unlike elements were grouped together.

---

# 4. Mendeleev's Periodic Law

## Statement

The properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic masses.

---

Basis:

Arrangement according to increasing atomic mass.

---

Achievements:

- Left gaps for undiscovered elements.
- Predicted properties of new elements.
- Corrected atomic masses of some elements.

---

# Limitations of Mendeleev Table

1. Position of hydrogen was uncertain.
2. Isotopes could not be explained.
3. Increasing atomic mass order had exceptions.

---

# PART B: MODERN PERIODIC TABLE

---

The physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.

---

Given by:

\[
\boxed{

---

Increasing atomic number.

# PART D: PERIODIC PROPERTIES

---

# 13. Atomic Radius

## Definition

The distance from nucleus to the outermost electron shell is called atomic radius.

---

Trend:

Across a period:

\[
\boxed{\text{Decreases}}
\]

---

Down a group:

\[
\boxed{\text{Increases}}
\]

---

Reason:

Across period:

- Nuclear charge increases
- Electrons are pulled closer

Down group:

- Number of shells increases

---

# 14. Ionic Radius

## Definition

The size of an ion is called ionic radius.

---

Cations:

Smaller than parent atom.

Example:

\[
Na^+<Na
\]

---

Anions:

Larger than parent atom.

Example:

\[
Cl^->Cl
\]

---

# 15. Ionization Energy

## Definition

The minimum energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom is called ionization energy.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
X(g)\rightarrow X^+(g)+e^-
}
\]

---

Trend:

Across period:

\[
\boxed{\text{Increases}}
\]

---

Down group:

\[
\boxed{\text{Decreases}}
\]

---

Factors affecting ionization energy:

- Atomic size
- Nuclear charge
- Shielding effect

---

# 16. Electron Affinity

## Definition

The energy released when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom is called electron affinity.

---

Trend:

Generally:

Across period:

\[
\boxed{\text{Increases}}
\]

---

Down group:

\[
\boxed{\text{Decreases}}
\]

---

# 17. Electronegativity

## Definition

The tendency of an atom to attract shared electrons towards itself is called electronegativity.

---

Given by:

\[
\boxed{
Linus Pauling
}
\]

---

Trend:

Across period:

\[
\boxed{\text{Increases}}
\]

---

Down group:

\[
\boxed{\text{Decreases}}
\]

---

Most electronegative element:

\[
\boxed{Fluorine(F)}
\]

---

# 18. Metallic Character

## Definition

The tendency of an element to lose electrons and form positive ions is called metallic character.

---

Trend:

Across period:

\[
\boxed{\text{Decreases}}
\]

---

Down group:

\[
\boxed{\text{Increases}}
\]

---

\[
\boxed{1mol}
\]

# Common Mistakes

- Using Celsius instead of Kelvin.
- Forgetting units of pressure and volume.
- Confusing ideal and real gases.
- Ignoring intermolecular forces in real gases.
- Using Dalton's law for reacting gases.
- Forgetting compressibility factor of ideal gas is 1.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Gas laws describe the relationship between pressure, volume and temperature.
- Ideal gas equation combines all gas laws.
- Dalton's law explains gas mixtures.
- Real gases deviate due to molecular volume and attraction.
- Van der Waals equation corrects ideal gas behaviour.
- Compressibility factor measures deviation from ideal behaviour.

# Introduction

Thermodynamics is the branch of chemistry that deals with energy changes during physical and chemical processes.

It explains:

- Heat changes in reactions
- Work done by systems
- Energy transfer
- Stability of reactions
- Spontaneity of processes

This chapter covers:

- Thermodynamic terms
- System and surroundings
- State and path functions
- First law of thermodynamics
- Enthalpy
- Hess's law
- Second law of thermodynamics
- Entropy
- Gibbs free energy

---

# PART A: BASIC THERMODYNAMIC TERMS

---

# 1. Thermodynamic System

## Definition

The part of the universe selected for study is called a thermodynamic system.

---

Examples:

- Gas in a cylinder
- Chemical reaction mixture

---

# 2. Surroundings

Everything outside the system that can exchange energy or matter with it is called surroundings.

---

# 3. Universe

The combination of system and surroundings is called universe.

\[
\boxed{
Universe=System+Surroundings
}
\]

---

# 4. Boundary

The imaginary or real surface separating system and surroundings is called boundary.

---

# PART B: TYPES OF SYSTEMS

---

# 5. Open System

A system that exchanges both matter and energy with surroundings.

---

Examples:

- Open vessel
- Human body

---

# 6. Closed System

A system that exchanges energy but not matter.

---

Example:

- Gas in a closed container

---

# 7. Isolated System

A system that exchanges neither matter nor energy.

---

Example:

- Ideal thermos flask

---

# PART C: PROPERTIES OF SYSTEMS

---

# 8. Extensive Properties

Properties that depend on the amount of substance present.

---

Examples:

- Mass
- Volume
- Internal energy
- Enthalpy

---

# 9. Intensive Properties

Properties that do not depend on amount of substance.

---

Examples:

- Temperature
- Pressure
- Density

---

# 10. State Functions

Properties that depend only on initial and final states.

---

Examples:

- Internal energy (U)
- Enthalpy (H)
- Entropy (S)
- Gibbs energy (G)

---

# 11. Path Functions

Properties that depend on the path followed.

---

Examples:

- Heat (q)
- Work (w)

---

# PART D: INTERNAL ENERGY

---

# 12. Internal Energy

## Definition

The total energy present inside a system due to molecular motion and interactions is called internal energy.

---

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{U}
\]

---

Change in internal energy:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta U=U_2-U_1
}
\]

---

# PART E: FIRST LAW OF THERMODYNAMICS

---

# 13. First Law

## Statement

Energy can neither be created nor destroyed. It can only be converted from one form to another.

---

Mathematical expression:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta U=q+w
}
\]

---

where:

- ΔU = change in internal energy
- q = heat supplied
- w = work done

---

# 14. Sign Convention

---

## Heat

Heat absorbed:

\[
q=+
\]

Heat released:

\[
q=-
\]

---

## Work

Work done on system:

\[
w=+
\]

Work done by system:

\[
w=-
\]

---

# 15. Pressure-Volume Work

When gas expands:

\[
\boxed{
w=-P\Delta V
}
\]

---

Expansion:

\[
\Delta V>0
\]

\[
w<0
\]

---

Compression:

\[
\Delta V<0
\]

\[
w>0
\]

---

# PART F: ENTHALPY

---

# 16. Enthalpy

## Definition

The heat content of a system at constant pressure is called enthalpy.

---

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{H}
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
H=U+PV
}
\]

---

Change in enthalpy:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta H=H_2-H_1
}
\]

---

# 17. Relation Between ΔH and ΔU

\[
\boxed{
\Delta H=\Delta U+\Delta(PV)
}
\]

For gaseous reactions:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta H=\Delta U+\Delta n_gRT
}
\]

---

where:

\[
\Delta n_g=
\text{moles of gaseous products - moles of gaseous reactants}
\]

---

# PART G: TYPES OF ENTHALPY CHANGES

---

# 18. Enthalpy of Formation

Heat change when one mole of compound is formed from its elements.

---

Example:

\[
H_2+\frac12O_2\rightarrow H_2O
\]

---

# 19. Enthalpy of Combustion

Heat released when one mole of substance burns completely in oxygen.

---

Example:

\[
CH_4+2O_2\rightarrow CO_2+2H_2O
\]

---

# 20. Enthalpy of Neutralization

Heat change when acid reacts with base.

---

Example:

\[
HCl+NaOH\rightarrow NaCl+H_2O
\]

---

# 21. Enthalpy of Fusion

Heat required to convert solid into liquid.

---

# 22. Enthalpy of Vaporization

Heat required to convert liquid into gas.

---

# PART H: HESS'S LAW

---

# 23. Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation

## Statement

The total enthalpy change of a reaction is the same whether it occurs in one step or multiple steps.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta H_{overall}
=

\Delta H_1+\Delta H_2+\Delta H_3
}
\]

---

Applications:

- Calculation of enthalpy changes
- Determining heat of formation

---

# PART I: SECOND LAW OF THERMODYNAMICS

---

# 24. Second Law

## Statement

A spontaneous process occurs in the direction that increases the total entropy of the universe.

---

# PART J: ENTROPY

---

# 25. Entropy

## Definition

The measure of randomness or disorder of a system is called entropy.

---

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{S}
\]

---

Change in entropy:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta S=S_2-S_1
}
\]

---

# Factors Affecting Entropy

Entropy increases with:

1. Increase in temperature
2. Increase in volume
3. Change from solid to liquid
4. Change from liquid to gas

---

Order of entropy:

\[
\boxed{
Solid<Liquid<Gas
}
\]

---

# PART K: GIBBS FREE ENERGY

---

# 26. Gibbs Free Energy

## Definition

The energy available in a system to perform useful work is called Gibbs free energy.

---

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{G}
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S
}
\]

---

# 27. Spontaneity Criteria

---

## Spontaneous Reaction

\[
\boxed{
\Delta G<0
}
\]

---

## Non-spontaneous Reaction

\[
\boxed{
\Delta G>0
}
\]

---

## Equilibrium

\[
\boxed{
\Delta G=0
}
\]
