# Introduction

Physics is the branch of science that studies the fundamental laws governing matter, energy, space and time.

Measurement is the process of comparing an unknown physical quantity with a known standard quantity.

This unit covers:

- Physical quantities and units
- Measurement systems
- Errors in measurement
- Accuracy and precision
- Significant figures
- Dimensional analysis

Applications:

- Scientific experiments
- Engineering measurements
- Technology development
- Mathematical modelling

---

## Definition

A quantity that can be measured and expressed by a numerical value with a unit is called a physical quantity.

---

\[
\boxed{

\text{Numerical Value}\times\text{Unit}
}

\[

# PART D: ERRORS IN MEASUREMENT

---

# 8. Error

## Definition

The difference between the measured value and the true value is called error.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Error=
Measured\ Value-True\ Value
}
\]

---

# 9. Types of Errors

---

# 1. Systematic Errors

Errors that occur in a fixed pattern are called systematic errors.

---

Causes:

- Instrument defects
- Zero error
- Calibration errors
- Environmental conditions

---

Types:

### Instrumental Error

Due to faulty instruments.

---

### Environmental Error

Due to temperature, pressure or humidity changes.

---

### Observational Error

Due to incorrect reading by observer.

---

# 2. Random Errors

Errors that occur unpredictably are called random errors.

---

Causes:

- Small fluctuations
- Human limitations
- Unpredictable changes

---

# 3. Gross Errors

Large errors caused by mistakes.

Examples:

- Wrong reading
- Incorrect calculation
- Wrong recording

---

# PART E: ACCURACY AND PRECISION

---

# 10. Accuracy

## Definition

Accuracy describes how close a measured value is to the true value.

---

Example:

True value:

\[
10.0cm
\]

Measured:

\[
9.99cm
\]

High accuracy.

---

# 11. Precision

## Definition

Precision describes the closeness of repeated measurements to each other.

---

Example:

Measurements:

\[
5.01,5.02,5.01
\]

High precision.

---

# Difference Between Accuracy and Precision

| Accuracy                      | Precision                       |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Closeness to true value       | Closeness among repeated values |
| Indicates correctness         | Indicates consistency           |
| Affected by systematic errors | Affected by random errors       |

# PART F: ERROR CALCULATIONS

---

# 12. Absolute Error

For measured values:

\[
a_1,a_2,a_3...
\]

Mean value:

\[
\bar a
\]

Absolute error:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta a_i=|a_i-\bar a|
}
\]

---

# 13. Mean Absolute Error

\[
\boxed{
\Delta a=
\frac{\sum|\Delta a_i|}{n}
}
\]

---

# 14. Relative Error

\[
\boxed{
Relative\ Error=
\frac{\Delta a}{\bar a}
}
\]

---

# 15. Percentage Error

\[
\boxed{
Percentage\ Error=
\frac{\Delta a}{\bar a}\times100
}
\]

---

# PART G: PROPAGATION OF ERRORS

---

# 16. Error in Addition/Subtraction

If:

\[
Z=A+B
\]

or:

\[
Z=A-B
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta Z=\Delta A+\Delta B
}
\]

---

# 17. Error in Multiplication/Division

If:

\[
Z=AB
\]

or:

\[
Z=\frac AB
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Delta Z}{Z}
=

\frac{\Delta A}{A} +
\frac{\Delta B}{B}
}
\]

---

# 18. Error in Power

If:

\[
Z=A^n
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Delta Z}{Z}
=

n\frac{\Delta A}{A}
}
\]

# 23. Principle of Homogeneity

## Statement

A physical equation is dimensionally correct only if dimensions of all terms are the same.

---

Example:

\[
s=ut+\frac12at^2
\]

Dimensions:

Left side:

\[
[L]
\]

Right side:

\[
[LT^{-1}][T]+[LT^{-2}][T^2]
\]

\[
=[L]+[L]
\]

Therefore dimensionally correct.

---

## 2. Conversion of units

## 3. Deriving relations

---

# 25. Limitations of Dimensional Analysis

- Cannot determine numerical constants.
- Cannot derive equations involving trigonometric functions.
- Cannot distinguish quantities having same dimensions.
- Cannot verify complete physical correctness.

# PART A: FRAME OF REFERENCE

---

# 1. Frame of Reference

## Definition

A coordinate system with respect to which the position and motion of an object are measured is called a frame of reference.

---

Example:

A person sitting in a moving train:

- Relative to train → at rest
- Relative to ground → moving

---

# 2. Position

## Definition

The location of an object with respect to a reference point is called its position.

---

In one dimension:

\[
\boxed{x}
\]

represents position.

---

In two dimensions:

\[
\boxed{(x,y)}
\]

---

In three dimensions:

\[
\boxed{(x,y,z)}
\]

---

# 3. Distance

## Definition

The total path length travelled by an object is called distance.

---

Properties:

- Scalar quantity
- Always positive
- Depends on actual path

Unit:

\[
\boxed{metre(m)}
\]

---

# 4. Displacement

## Definition

The shortest distance between initial and final positions in a specified direction is called displacement.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta x=x_f-x_i
}
\]

---

Properties:

- Vector quantity
- Can be positive, negative or zero
- Independent of path

---

# Difference Between Distance and Displacement

| Distance                              | Displacement                   |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Scalar quantity                       | Vector quantity                |
| Actual path length                    | Shortest path                  |
| Always positive                       | Can be positive or negative    |
| Greater than or equal to displacement | Less than or equal to distance |

---

# Introduction

# PART A: FORCE AND INERTIA

---

# 1. Force

## Definition

A force is an external agent that changes or tends to change the state of rest or motion of a body.

---

Force can change:

- Speed
- Direction
- Shape

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{Newton(N)}
\]

---

# 2. Effects of Force

A force can:

1. Start motion
2. Stop motion
3. Change velocity
4. Change direction
5. Change shape

---

# 3. Inertia

## Definition

The tendency of a body to resist any change in its state of rest or motion is called inertia.

---

Inertia depends on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Mass of the body}}
\]

Greater mass:

\[
\Rightarrow
\]

Greater inertia

---

# Types of Inertia

---

# 1. Inertia of Rest

A body at rest tends to remain at rest.

Example:

A passenger falls backward when a bus starts suddenly.

---

# 2. Inertia of Motion

A moving body tends to continue moving.

Example:

A passenger falls forward when a moving bus stops suddenly.

---

# 3. Inertia of Direction

A body resists change in direction.

Example:

Dust removed from a carpet by beating.

---

# 4. Momentum

## Definition

The quantity of motion possessed by a body is called momentum.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
p=mv
}
\]

where:

- m = mass
- v = velocity

---

Unit:

\[
kg\,m/s
\]

---

Momentum is a vector quantity.

---

# PART B: NEWTON'S LAWS OF MOTION

---

# 5. Newton's First Law

## Statement

A body continues in its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force.

---

This law explains:

\[
\boxed{\text{Inertia}}
\]

---

Mathematical form:

If:

\[
F=0
\]

then:

\[
v=\text{constant}
\]

---

# 6. Newton's Second Law

## Statement

The rate of change of momentum is directly proportional to the applied force and occurs in the direction of force.

---

Momentum:

\[
p=mv
\]

Change in momentum:

\[
\Delta p=m(v-u)
\]

Rate of change:

\[
\frac{\Delta p}{t}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
F=ma
}
\]

---

# 7. Newton's Third Law

## Statement

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

---

Mathematically:

\[
\boxed{
F_{12}=-F_{21}
}
\]

---

Examples:

- Rocket propulsion
- Walking
- Swimming
- Recoil of gun

---

# PART C: IMPULSE AND MOMENTUM

---

# 8. Impulse

## Definition

The product of force and time interval for which it acts is called impulse.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
J=F\Delta t
}
\]

---

# Introduction

Work, energy and power are fundamental concepts used to understand how forces produce changes in motion and how energy is transferred.

# PART A: WORK

---

# 1. Work

## Definition

Work is said to be done when a force acting on a body produces displacement in the direction of the force.

---

Work depends on:

1. Force applied
2. Displacement produced
3. Angle between force and displacement

---

# 2. Work Done by Constant Force

If a force F acts on a body producing displacement s at an angle θ:

\[
\boxed{
W=Fs\cos\theta
}
\]

---

where:

- F = force
- s = displacement
- θ = angle between force and displacement

---

# 3. Work as Scalar Product

Work is the dot product of force and displacement.

\[
\boxed{
W=\vec F\cdot\vec s
}
\]

---

# 4. Cases of Work

---

## Case 1: Force and displacement in same direction

\[
\theta=0^\circ
\]

\[
W=Fs
\]

Work is positive.

Example:

Pushing a box forward.

---

## Case 2: Force opposite to displacement

\[
\theta=180^\circ
\]

\[
W=-Fs
\]

Work is negative.

Example:

Friction acting on moving object.

---

## Case 3: Force perpendicular to displacement

\[
\theta=90^\circ
\]

\[
W=0
\]

Example:

Centripetal force in circular motion.

---

# 5. Work Done by Variable Force

When force changes with displacement:

\[
\boxed{
W=\int F\,dx
}
\]

---

Graphically:

Work = Area under force-displacement graph.

---

# PART B: ENERGY

---

# 6. Energy

## Definition

Energy is the capacity of a body to do work.

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{Joule(J)}
\]

---

Types:

- Kinetic energy
- Potential energy

---

# PART C: KINETIC ENERGY

---

# 7. Kinetic Energy

## Definition

The energy possessed by a body due to its motion is called kinetic energy.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
KE=\frac12mv^2
}
\]

---

where:

- m = mass
- v = velocity

---

# 8. Derivation of Kinetic Energy

Work done:

\[
W=Fs
\]

From Newton's law:

\[
F=ma
\]

Therefore:

\[
W=mas
\]

Using equation:

\[
v^2-u^2=2as
\]

For initial velocity:

\[
u=0
\]

\[
v^2=2as
\]

Therefore:

\[
as=\frac{v^2}{2}
\]

Substituting:

\[
W=m\frac{v^2}{2}
\]

Hence:

\[
\boxed{
KE=\frac12mv^2
}
\]

---

# PART D: WORK-ENERGY THEOREM

---

# 9. Work-Energy Theorem

## Statement

The work done by the net force on a body is equal to the change in its kinetic energy.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
W=K_f-K_i
}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{
W=\Delta KE
}
\]

---

# PART E: POTENTIAL ENERGY

---

# 10. Potential Energy

## Definition

The energy possessed by a body due to its position or configuration is called potential energy.

---

# 11. Gravitational Potential Energy

For a body of mass m raised to height h:

\[
\boxed{
PE=mgh
}
\]

---

where:

- m = mass
- g = acceleration due to gravity
- h = height

---

# 12. Spring Potential Energy

Energy stored in a stretched or compressed spring:

\[
\boxed{
PE=\frac12kx^2
}
\]

---

where:

- k = spring constant
- x = extension

---

# PART F: CONSERVATION OF ENERGY

---

# 13. Law of Conservation of Energy

## Statement

Energy can neither be created nor destroyed. It can only be transformed from one form to another.

---

For mechanical energy:

\[
\boxed{
KE+PE=Constant
}
\]

---

# Example

At top:

\[

\]

\[
KE=0
\]

At ground:

\[

\]

\[
KE=max
\]
