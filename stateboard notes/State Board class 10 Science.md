# Physics – Chapter 1: Laws of Motion

> **Subject:** Physics  
> **Class:** 10  
> **Chapter:** Laws of Motion

---

# Introduction

Motion is the change in position of an object with respect to time.

The laws of motion explain the relationship between:

- Force
- Motion
- Mass
- Acceleration

This chapter covers:

- Inertia
- Momentum
- Newton's laws of motion
- Impulse
- Conservation of momentum

---

# PART A: FORCE AND MOTION

---

# 1. Force

## Definition

A force is a push or pull that can change the state of rest or motion of an object.

---

Effects of force:

- Changes speed
- Changes direction
- Changes shape

---

# 2. Types of Forces

## Contact Forces

Forces acting through physical contact.

Examples:

- Frictional force
- Muscular force

---

## Non-contact Forces

Forces acting without physical contact.

Examples:

- Gravitational force
- Magnetic force

---

# PART B: INERTIA

---

# 3. Inertia

## Definition

The property of an object by which it resists any change in its state of rest or uniform motion is called inertia.

---

Inertia depends on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Mass of the object}}
\]

Greater mass → Greater inertia

---

# 4. Types of Inertia

---

## 1. Inertia of Rest

An object at rest tends to remain at rest.

Example:

When a bus starts suddenly, passengers fall backward.

---

## 2. Inertia of Motion

A moving object tends to continue moving.

Example:

Passengers fall forward when a moving bus stops suddenly.

---

## 3. Inertia of Direction

An object tends to continue moving in the same direction.

Example:

A stone tied to a string flies away tangentially when the string breaks.

---

# PART C: NEWTON'S LAWS OF MOTION

---

# 5. Newton's First Law

## Statement

An object remains at rest or continues in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force.

---

Also called:

\[
\boxed{\text{Law of Inertia}}
\]

---

Examples:

- Dust removed by beating a carpet.
- Passengers moving forward when a bus stops.

---

# 6. Newton's Second Law

## Statement

The rate of change of momentum of an object is directly proportional to the applied force and occurs in the direction of force.

---

Mathematical form:

\[
\boxed{
F\propto\frac{p_2-p_1}{t}
}
\]

---

Momentum:

\[
p=mv
\]

---

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
F=ma
}
\]

---

Where:

F = Force

m = Mass

a = Acceleration

---

# 7. Newton's Third Law

## Statement

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

---

Examples:

- Recoil of gun
- Rocket propulsion
- Walking

---

# PART D: LINEAR MOMENTUM

---

# 8. Momentum

## Definition

The quantity of motion possessed by an object is called momentum.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
p=mv
}
\]

---

Where:

p = momentum

m = mass

v = velocity

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{kg\,m/s}
\]

---

# 9. Characteristics of Momentum

- It is a vector quantity.
- Depends on mass and velocity.
- Direction is same as velocity.

---

# Example

Find momentum of a body:

Mass:

\[
5kg
\]

Velocity:

\[
4m/s
\]

Solution:

\[
p=mv
\]

\[
=5\times4
\]

\[
\boxed{20\,kg\,m/s}
\]

---

# PART E: IMPULSE

---

# 10. Impulse

## Definition

The product of force and time for which it acts is called impulse.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
Impulse=F\times t
}
\]

---

Also:

\[
\boxed{
Impulse=\Delta p
}
\]

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{N\,s}
\]

---

# Applications of Impulse

---

## Airbags

Airbags increase collision time and reduce force.

---

## Catching a Ball

A player moves hands backward to increase stopping time and reduce force.

---

# PART F: CONSERVATION OF MOMENTUM

---

# 11. Law of Conservation of Momentum

## Statement

When no external force acts on a system, the total momentum before collision is equal to the total momentum after collision.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
m_1u_1+m_2u_2
=

m_1v_1+m_2v_2
}
\]

---

Where:

Before collision:

u = initial velocity

After collision:

v = final velocity

---

# Example

Two bodies collide.

Before collision:

Total momentum:

\[
=m_1u_1+m_2u_2
\]

After collision:

\[
=m_1v_1+m_2v_2
\]

Both are equal.

---

# PART G: APPLICATIONS OF NEWTON'S LAWS

---

# 12. Rocket Propulsion

Rocket moves upward due to:

- Gas expelled downward (action)
- Rocket moves upward (reaction)

Based on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Newton's Third Law}}
\]

---

# 13. Recoil of Gun

When bullet moves forward, gun moves backward.

---

Reason:

Conservation of momentum.

---

# 14. Walking

When we push the ground backward, ground pushes us forward.

---

# Important Formula Sheet

## Momentum

\[
\boxed{

}
\]

---

## Force

\[
\boxed{
F=ma
}
\]

---

## Impulse

\[
\boxed{
J=Ft=\Delta p
}
\]

---

## Conservation of Momentum

\[
\boxed{
m_1u_1+m_2u_2
=

m_1v_1+m_2v_2
}
\]

---

# Solved Examples

---

## Example 1

A force of 10 N acts on a mass of 2 kg.

Find acceleration.

Given:

\[
F=10N
\]

\[
m=2kg
\]

Using:

\[
F=ma
\]

\[
10=2a
\]

\[
\boxed{a=5m/s^2}
\]

---

## Example 2

Find momentum of a 1000 kg car moving at 20 m/s.

\[
p=mv
\]

\[
=1000\times20
\]

\[
\boxed{20000\,kg\,m/s}
\]

---

## Example 3

Why does a gun recoil after firing?

Because:

- Bullet moves forward.
- Equal reaction force pushes gun backward.

Based on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Newton's Third Law}}
\]

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing mass and weight.
- Forgetting momentum is a vector quantity.
- Using wrong units for force and momentum.
- Applying Newton's third law without identifying action and reaction pairs.
- Forgetting impulse equals change in momentum.
- Mixing speed and velocity.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Force changes the state of motion of objects.
- Inertia is the resistance to change in motion.
- Newton's laws explain the relationship between force and motion.
- Momentum depends on mass and velocity.
- Impulse is the change in momentum.
- Total momentum remains conserved when no external force acts.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Newton's laws form the foundation of classical mechanics.}
}
\]

# Introduction

Optics is the branch of physics that deals with the study of light and its behaviour.

This chapter covers:

- Refraction of light
- Laws of refraction

- Image formation
- Lens formula
- Magnification
- Human eye

---

# PART A: LIGHT

---

# 1. Light

## Definition

Light is a form of energy that produces the sensation of vision.

---

Properties of light:

- Travels in straight lines
- Can be reflected
- Can be refracted
- Can be absorbed

---

# PART B: REFRACTION OF LIGHT

---

# 2. Refraction

## Definition

The bending of light when it passes from one transparent medium to another is called refraction.

---

Reason:

The speed of light changes when it enters a different medium.

---

Examples:

- Pencil appearing bent in water
- Bottom of a pond appearing raised

---

# 3. Laws of Refraction (Snell's Law)

---

## First Law

The incident ray, refracted ray and normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane.

---

## Second Law

For a given pair of media:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}=\mu
}
\]

---

Where:

- i = angle of incidence
- r = angle of refraction
- μ = refractive index

---

# 4. Refractive Index

## Definition

The ratio of speed of light in vacuum to the speed of light in a medium is called refractive index.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\mu=\frac{c}{v}
}
\]

---

Where:

c = speed of light in vacuum

v = speed of light in medium

---

# PART C: REFLECTION AND REFRACTION

---

# 5. Difference Between Reflection and Refraction

| Reflection                   | Refraction                  |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Light returns to same medium | Light enters another medium |
| Speed remains same           | Speed changes               |
| Direction changes            | Path bends                  |

---

# PART D: LENSES

---

# 6. Lens

## Definition

A transparent optical device bounded by two curved surfaces is called a lens.

---

Types:

1. Convex lens
2. Concave lens

---

# 7. Convex Lens

## Definition

A lens thicker at the centre and thinner at the edges is called a convex lens.

---

Also called:

\[
\boxed{\text{Converging lens}}
\]

---

Uses:

- Magnifying glass
- Camera
- Microscope

---

# 8. Concave Lens

## Definition

A lens thinner at the centre and thicker at the edges is called a concave lens.

---

Also called:

\[
\boxed{\text{Diverging lens}}
\]

---

Uses:

- Spectacles for myopia

---

# PART E: TERMS RELATED TO LENSES

---

# 9. Principal Axis

The straight line passing through the optical centre and centres of curvature of a lens is called principal axis.

---

# 10. Optical Centre

The central point of a lens through which light passes without deviation.

---

# 11. Principal Focus

The point where parallel rays meet after refraction is called principal focus.

---

# 12. Focal Length

The distance between optical centre and principal focus is called focal length.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
f=\text{distance between O and F}
}
\]

---

# PART F: IMAGE FORMATION BY CONVEX LENS

---

# 13. Convex Lens Cases

---

## Case 1: Object Beyond 2F

Image:

- Between F and 2F
- Real
- Inverted
- Smaller

---

## Case 2: Object at 2F

Image:

- At 2F
- Real
- Inverted
- Same size

---

## Case 3: Object Between F and 2F

Image:

- Beyond 2F
- Real
- Inverted
- Enlarged

---

## Case 4: Object Between Optical Centre and F

Image:

- Virtual
- Erect
- Magnified

---

# PART G: IMAGE FORMATION BY CONCAVE LENS

---

# 14. Concave Lens Image

Image is always:

- Virtual
- Erect
- Diminished

---

Image forms between:

\[
\boxed{O\text{ and }F}
\]

---

# PART H: LENS FORMULA

---

# 15. Lens Formula

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\frac1f=\frac1v-\frac1u
}
\]

---

Where:

f = focal length

v = image distance

u = object distance

---

# 16. Sign Convention

Using Cartesian sign convention:

- Distance measured in direction of incident light = positive
- Distance opposite to incident light = negative

---

# PART I: MAGNIFICATION

---

# 17. Magnification

## Definition

The ratio of height of image to height of object is called magnification.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
m=\frac{h_i}{h_o}
}
\]

---

For lenses:

\[
\boxed{
m=\frac vu
}
\]

---

Where:

hi = image height

ho = object height

---

# PART J: HUMAN EYE

---

# 18. Human Eye

The human eye is an optical organ that helps in vision.

---

Main parts:

- Cornea
- Iris
- Pupil
- Lens
- Retina
- Optic nerve

---

# 19. Functions of Eye Parts

---

## Cornea

Protects eye and refracts light.

---

## Iris

Controls amount of light entering eye.

---

## Pupil

Opening through which light enters.

---

## Lens

Focuses light on retina.

---

## Retina

Forms image.

---

## Optic Nerve

Carries signals to brain.

---

# PART K: DEFECTS OF VISION

---

# 20. Myopia (Short Sightedness)

## Definition

A person can see nearby objects clearly but distant objects appear blurred.

---

Cause:

Image forms before retina.

---

Correction:

\[
\boxed{\text{Concave lens}}
\]

---

# 21. Hypermetropia (Long Sightedness)

## Definition

A person can see distant objects clearly but nearby objects appear blurred.

---

Cause:

Image forms behind retina.

---

Correction:

\[
\boxed{\text{Convex lens}}
\]

---

# 22. Presbyopia

## Definition

Loss of ability to focus nearby objects due to ageing.

---

Cause:

Reduced flexibility of eye lens.

---

Correction:

- Convex lens
- Bifocal lens

---

- Forgetting lens formula sign convention.

- Confusing real and virtual images.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Refraction is bending of light due to change in speed.
- Lenses form images by refraction.

# Introduction

Thermal physics deals with heat, temperature and the behaviour of gases.

# PART A: HEAT AND TEMPERATURE

---

# 1. Heat

## Definition

Heat is a form of energy that flows from a hotter body to a colder body due to temperature difference.

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{Joule (J)}
\]

---

# 2. Temperature

## Definition

Temperature is the measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of a body.

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{Kelvin (K)}
\]

---

# 3. Difference Between Heat and Temperature

| Heat                           | Temperature           |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------- |
| Form of energy                 | Measure of hotness    |
| Depends on amount of substance | Independent of amount |
| Unit: Joule                    | Unit: Kelvin          |

---

# PART B: TEMPERATURE SCALES

---

# 4. Celsius Scale

Symbol:

\[
^\circ C
\]

Water freezes:

\[
0^\circ C
\]

Water boils:

\[
100^\circ C
\]

---

# 5. Kelvin Scale

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
K=^\circ C+273
}
\]

---

Example:

Convert:

\[
27^\circ C
\]

to Kelvin.

\[
K=27+273
\]

\[
\boxed{300K}
\]

---

# 6. Fahrenheit Scale

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{C}{5}
=

\frac{F-32}{9}
}
\]

---

# PART C: THERMAL EXPANSION

---

# 7. Thermal Expansion

## Definition

The increase in size of a substance when its temperature increases is called thermal expansion.

---

Types:

1. Linear expansion
2. Area expansion
3. Volume expansion

---

# 8. Linear Expansion

Expansion in length is called linear expansion.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta L=L\alpha\Delta T
}
\]

---

Where:

- ΔL = increase in length
- L = original length
- α = coefficient of linear expansion
- ΔT = temperature change

---

# 9. Area Expansion

Expansion in surface area.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta A=A\beta\Delta T
}
\]

---

# 10. Volume Expansion

Expansion in volume.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta V=V\gamma\Delta T
}
\]

---

# PART D: GAS LAWS

---

# 11. Gas Laws

The relationship between pressure, volume and temperature of gases is explained by gas laws.

---

# PART E: BOYLE'S LAW

---

# 12. Boyle's Law

## Statement

At constant temperature, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its volume.

---

Mathematical form:

\[
\boxed{
P\propto\frac1V
}
\]

---

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
PV=constant
}
\]

---

or:

\[
\boxed{
P_1V_1=P_2V_2
}
\]

---

# Example

A gas occupies 4 L at pressure 2 atm.

Find volume at 4 atm.

Given:

\[
P_1=2
\]

\[
V_1=4
\]

\[
P_2=4
\]

Using:

\[
P_1V_1=P_2V_2
\]

\[
2(4)=4V_2
\]

\[
V_2=2L
\]

Answer:

\[
\boxed{2L}
\]

---

# PART F: CHARLES'S LAW

---

# 13. Charles's Law

## Statement

At constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
V\propto T
}
\]

---

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\frac VT=constant
}
\]

---

or:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{V_1}{T_1}
=

\frac{V_2}{T_2}
}
\]

---

# PART G: PRESSURE LAW

---

# 14. Pressure Law

## Statement

At constant volume, pressure of a gas is directly proportional to absolute temperature.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
P\propto T
}
\]

---

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\frac PT=constant
}
\]

---

# PART H: IDEAL GAS EQUATION

---

# 15. Ideal Gas

An ideal gas is a hypothetical gas that obeys all gas laws perfectly.

---

# 16. Ideal Gas Equation

Combining gas laws:

\[
\boxed{
PV=nRT
}
\]

---

Where:

P = pressure

V = volume

n = number of moles

R = gas constant

T = temperature

---

# 17. Universal Gas Constant

\[
\boxed{
R=8.314\,J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}
}
\]

---

# PART I: KINETIC THEORY OF GASES

---

# 18. Kinetic Theory

It explains gases based on motion of molecules.

---

Main assumptions:

- Gas molecules are in continuous motion.
- Collisions are elastic.
- Molecules have negligible volume.
- Average kinetic energy depends on temperature.

---

# 19. Relation Between Temperature and Kinetic Energy

Higher temperature:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Higher molecular kinetic energy}
}
\]

---

---

## Thermometer

# PART A: ELECTRIC CHARGE

---

# 1. Electric Charge

## Definition

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical interactions.

---

Types of charges:

1. Positive charge
2. Negative charge

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Coulomb (C)}}
\]

---

# PART B: ELECTRIC CURRENT

---

# 2. Electric Current

## Definition

The rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor is called electric current.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac{Q}{t}
}
\]

---

Where:

I = current

Q = charge

t = time

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Ampere (A)}}
\]

---

# 3. Direction of Current

Conventional current flows from:

\[
\boxed{\text{Positive terminal to negative terminal}}
\]

---

Electron flow is opposite:

\[
\boxed{\text{Negative to positive}}
\]

---

# PART C: ELECTRIC POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE

---

# 4. Potential Difference

## Definition

The work done to move a unit charge from one point to another is called potential difference.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
V=\frac{W}{Q}
}
\]

---

Where:

V = potential difference

W = work done

Q = charge

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Volt (V)}}
\]

---

# PART D: OHM'S LAW

---

# 5. Ohm's Law

## Statement

At constant temperature, the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends.

---

Mathematically:

\[
\boxed{
V\propto I
}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
V=IR
}
\]

---

Where:

V = voltage

I = current

R = resistance

---

# 6. V-I Graph

For an ohmic conductor:

- V-I graph is a straight line.
- Slope gives resistance.

---

# PART E: RESISTANCE

---

# 7. Resistance

## Definition

The opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of electric current is called resistance.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
R=\frac VI
}
\]

---

SI Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Ohm }(\Omega)}
\]

---

# 8. Factors Affecting Resistance

Resistance depends on:

1. Length of conductor
2. Area of cross-section
3. Nature of material
4. Temperature

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
R\propto L
}
\]

---

\[
\boxed{
R\propto\frac1A
}
\]

---

# PART F: RESISTIVITY

---

# 9. Resistivity

## Definition

The resistance offered by a material of unit length and unit area of cross-section is called resistivity.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{

Environmental management is the process of protecting and managing natural resources to maintain ecological balance.

This chapter covers:

- Environment and ecosystem
- Renewable energy resources
- Soil erosion
- Conservation of wildlife
- Sustainable development

# PART A: ENVIRONMENT

---

# 1. Environment

## Definition

The surroundings in which living organisms exist and interact are called the environment.

---

Components:

1. Biotic components
2. Abiotic components

---

# 2. Biotic Components

## Definition

Living components of the environment are called biotic components.

Examples:

- Plants
- Animals
- Microorganisms

---

# 3. Abiotic Components

## Definition

Non-living components of the environment are called abiotic components.

Examples:

- Air
- Water
- Soil
- Temperature
- Sunlight

---

# PART B: ECOSYSTEM

---

# 4. Ecosystem

## Definition

A functional unit of nature where living organisms interact with each other and with the physical environment is called an ecosystem.

---

Components:

1. Producers
2. Consumers
3. Decomposers

---

# 5. Producers

## Definition

Organisms that prepare their own food by photosynthesis are called producers.

---

Example:

\[
\boxed{\text{Green plants}}
\]

---

# 6. Consumers

## Definition

Organisms that depend on other organisms for food are called consumers.

---

Types:

- Herbivores
- Carnivores
- Omnivores

---

# 7. Decomposers

## Definition

Organisms that break down dead organic matter are called decomposers.

---

Examples:

- Bacteria
- Fungi

---

- Biomass energy
