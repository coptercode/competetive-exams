# Introduction

Equilibrium is the state in which the forward and backward processes occur at equal rates.

It is important in:

- Chemical industries
- Acid-base reactions
- Solubility processes
- Biological systems

This chapter covers:

- Reversible reactions
- Dynamic equilibrium
- Equilibrium constants
- Le Chatelier's principle
- Factors affecting equilibrium

---

# PART A: EQUILIBRIUM

---

# 1. Equilibrium

## Definition

A state in which the macroscopic properties of a system remain constant with time is called equilibrium.

---

Examples:

- Evaporation and condensation
- Chemical reactions

---

# 2. Types of Equilibrium

---

## Physical Equilibrium

Equilibrium involving physical changes.

Examples:

- Solid ⇌ Liquid
- Liquid ⇌ Vapour

---

Example:

\[
H_2O(l)\rightleftharpoons H_2O(g)
\]

---

## Chemical Equilibrium

Equilibrium involving chemical reactions.

Example:

\[
H_2+I_2\rightleftharpoons2HI
\]

---

# PART B: REVERSIBLE REACTIONS

---

# 3. Reversible Reaction

## Definition

A reaction that can proceed in both forward and backward directions is called a reversible reaction.

---

Representation:

\[
\boxed{
A+B\rightleftharpoons C+D
}
\]

---

Forward reaction:

Reactants → Products

---

Backward reaction:

Products → Reactants

---

# 4. Irreversible Reaction

A reaction that proceeds only in one direction is called irreversible reaction.

---

Example:

\[
C+O_2\rightarrow CO_2
\]

---

# PART C: DYNAMIC EQUILIBRIUM

---

# 5. Dynamic Nature of Equilibrium

## Definition

At equilibrium, the forward reaction and backward reaction continue simultaneously at equal rates.

---

Characteristics:

1. Forward rate = Backward rate

2. Concentrations remain constant

3. Reaction does not stop

4. Equilibrium is dynamic

---

# 6. Equilibrium Constant

According to law of mass action:

For:

\[
aA+bB\rightleftharpoons cC+dD
\]

Equilibrium constant:

\[
\boxed{
K_c=
\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}
{[A]^a[B]^b}
}
\]

---

where:

[A], [B], [C], [D]

are equilibrium concentrations.

---

# 7. Meaning of Kc

---

## Large Kc

\[
K_c>>1
\]

Reaction favours products.

---

## Small Kc

\[
K_c<<1
\]

Reaction favours reactants.

---

# PART D: EQUILIBRIUM CONSTANT IN TERMS OF PRESSURE

---

# 8. Kp

For gaseous reactions, equilibrium constant can be expressed using partial pressures.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
K_p=
\frac{(P_C)^c(P_D)^d}
{(P_A)^a(P_B)^b}
}
\]

---

# 9. Relation Between Kp and Kc

\[
\boxed{
K_p=K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}
}
\]

---

where:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta n=
\text{moles of gaseous products}
-

\text{moles of gaseous reactants}
}
\]

---

Cases:

---

If:

\[
\Delta n=0
\]

Then:

\[
\boxed{
K_p=K_c
}
\]

---

# PART E: HOMOGENEOUS AND HETEROGENEOUS EQUILIBRIUM

---

# 10. Homogeneous Equilibrium

All reactants and products are in the same phase.

---

Example:

\[
H_2(g)+I_2(g)
\rightleftharpoons
2HI(g)
\]

---

# 11. Heterogeneous Equilibrium

Reactants and products are in different phases.

---

Example:

\[
CaCO_3(s)
\rightleftharpoons
CaO(s)+CO_2(g)
\]

---

Pure solids and liquids are not included in equilibrium expression.

---

# PART F: LE CHATELIER'S PRINCIPLE

---

# 12. Le Chatelier's Principle

## Statement

If a system at equilibrium is disturbed by changing concentration, temperature or pressure, the equilibrium shifts in a direction that reduces the effect of disturbance.

---

# PART G: EFFECT OF CONCENTRATION

---

# 13. Concentration Change

For:

\[
A+B\rightleftharpoons C+D
\]

---

Adding reactants:

Equilibrium shifts forward.

---

Adding products:

Equilibrium shifts backward.

---

Removing products:

Equilibrium shifts forward.

---

# PART H: EFFECT OF PRESSURE

---

# 14. Pressure Effect

Applicable mainly to gaseous equilibria.

---

Increasing pressure:

Equilibrium shifts towards side with fewer gas molecules.

---

Decreasing pressure:

Equilibrium shifts towards side with more gas molecules.

---

Example:

\[
N_2+3H_2
\rightleftharpoons
2NH_3
\]

Left side:

4 moles gas

Right side:

2 moles gas

---

Increasing pressure:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Formation of }NH_3\text{ increases}
}
\]

---

# PART I: EFFECT OF TEMPERATURE

---

# 15. Temperature Effect

Heat is treated as a reactant or product.

---

## Exothermic Reaction

\[
A+B\rightarrow C+Heat
\]

Increasing temperature:

Equilibrium shifts backward.

---

## Endothermic Reaction

\[
A+B+Heat\rightarrow C
\]

Increasing temperature:

Equilibrium shifts forward.

---

# PART J: CATALYST EFFECT

---

# 16. Effect of Catalyst

A catalyst:

- Increases rate of forward reaction
- Increases rate of backward reaction

It does not change:

\[
\boxed{
K_c
}
\]

or equilibrium position.

---

# PART K: IMPORTANT INDUSTRIAL APPLICATIONS

---

# 17. Haber Process

Preparation of ammonia:

\[
\boxed{
N_2+3H_2
\rightleftharpoons
2NH_3+Heat
}
\]

Conditions:

- High pressure
- Moderate temperature
- Iron catalyst

---

# 18. Contact Process

Preparation of sulphuric acid:

\[
2SO_2+O_2
\rightleftharpoons
2SO_3
\]

Catalyst:

\[
V_2O_5
\]

---

# Important Formula Sheet

## Equilibrium Constant

\[
\boxed{
K_c=
\frac{Products}
{Reactants}
}
\]

---

## Pressure Constant

\[
\boxed{
K_p=
\frac{Product\ pressures}
{Reactant\ pressures}
}
\]

---

## Relation Between Kp and Kc

\[
\boxed{
K_p=K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}
}
\]

---

## Change in Gas Moles

\[
\boxed{
\Delta n=
n_p-n_r
}
\]

---

# Solved Examples

---

## Example 1

For:

\[
H_2+I_2\rightleftharpoons2HI
\]

Write Kc expression.

Solution:

\[
\boxed{
K_c=
\frac{[HI]^2}
{[H_2][I_2]}
}
\]

---

## Example 2

If:

\[
K_c=1000
\]

Reaction favours:

Since:

\[
K_c>>1
\]

Answer:

\[
\boxed{\text{Products}}
\]

---

## Example 3

For reaction:

\[
N_2+3H_2\rightleftharpoons2NH_3
\]

Number of gaseous moles:

Products:

2

Reactants:

4

\[
\Delta n=2-4
\]

\[
\boxed{-2}
\]

---

# Common Mistakes

- Forgetting equilibrium is dynamic, not static.
- Including solids and liquids in Kc expression.
- Using Celsius instead of Kelvin in Kp relation.
- Confusing catalyst effect with equilibrium shift.
- Forgetting pressure affects only gaseous systems.
- Using wrong sign for Δn.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Equilibrium occurs when forward and backward reaction rates become equal.
- Chemical equilibrium is dynamic.
- Kc and Kp express equilibrium position.
- Large equilibrium constant favours products.
- Le Chatelier's principle predicts equilibrium shifts.
- Temperature, pressure and concentration affect equilibrium position.
- Catalysts only increase the speed of reaching equilibrium.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Equilibrium represents a balance between opposing processes.}
}
\]

# Introduction

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more components.

Solutions are important in:

- Chemical reactions
- Pharmaceutical preparations
- Industrial processes
- Biological systems

This chapter covers:

- Types of solutions
- Concentration terms
- Solubility
- Henry's law
- Raoult's law
- Colligative properties
- Osmotic pressure

---

# PART A: SOLUTIONS

---

# 1. Solution

## Definition

A homogeneous mixture of two or more substances is called a solution.

---

Components:

1. Solvent
2. Solute

---

# 2. Solvent

The component present in larger amount is called solvent.

Example:

Water in salt solution.

---

# 3. Solute

The component present in smaller amount is called solute.

Example:

Salt in salt solution.

---

# 4. Types of Solutions

Based on physical state:

| Solute | Solvent | Example          |
| ------ | ------- | ---------------- |
| Gas    | Gas     | Air              |
| Liquid | Liquid  | Alcohol in water |
| Solid  | Liquid  | Salt solution    |
| Gas    | Liquid  | CO₂ in water     |
| Solid  | Solid   | Alloys           |

---

# PART B: CONCENTRATION TERMS

---

# 5. Mass Percentage

The mass of solute present in 100 parts of solution.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\%w/w=
\frac{Mass\ of\ solute}
{Mass\ of\ solution}
\times100
}
\]

---

# 6. Volume Percentage

Volume of solute present in 100 parts of solution.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\%v/v=
\frac{Volume\ of\ solute}
{Volume\ of\ solution}
\times100
}
\]

---

# 7. Molarity (M)

## Definition

Number of moles of solute present in one litre of solution.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
M=
\frac{Number\ of\ moles\ of\ solute}
{Volume\ of\ solution\ in\ litre}
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{mol/L}
\]

---

# 8. Molality (m)

## Definition

Number of moles of solute present in one kilogram of solvent.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
m=
\frac{Moles\ of\ solute}
{Mass\ of\ solvent\ in\ kg}
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{mol/kg}
\]

---

# 9. Mole Fraction

## Definition

Ratio of moles of one component to total moles of all components.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
X_A=
\frac{n_A}
{n_A+n_B}
}
\]

---

For a binary solution:

\[
\boxed{
X_A+X_B=1
}
\]

---

# PART C: SOLUBILITY

---

# 10. Solubility

## Definition

The maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a specific temperature is called solubility.

---

# Factors Affecting Solubility

---

## 1. Nature of Solute and Solvent

"Like dissolves like"

---

## 2. Temperature

For most solids:

Temperature increases → Solubility increases

---

## 3. Pressure

Important for gases.

---

# PART D: SOLUBILITY OF GASES

---

# 11. Henry's Law

## Statement

At constant temperature, the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
P=K_Hx
}
\]

---

where:

- P = partial pressure of gas
- x = mole fraction of gas
- KH = Henry's law constant

---

# Applications of Henry's Law

---

## 1. Carbonated Drinks

CO₂ is dissolved under high pressure.

---

## 2. Scuba Diving

Nitrogen dissolves in blood at high pressure.

---

## 3. High Altitude

Lower oxygen pressure affects breathing.

---

# PART E: VAPOUR PRESSURE

---

# 12. Vapour Pressure

## Definition

The pressure exerted by vapour molecules above a liquid surface at equilibrium is called vapour pressure.

---

Factors affecting vapour pressure:

- Temperature
- Nature of liquid
- Intermolecular forces

---

# PART F: RAOULT'S LAW

---

# 13. Raoult's Law

## Statement

The partial vapour pressure of a volatile component in a solution is directly proportional to its mole fraction.

---

For component A:

\[
\boxed{
P_A=P_A^0X_A
}
\]

---

where:

- PA = partial vapour pressure
- PA° = vapour pressure of pure component
- XA = mole fraction

---

# 14. Total Vapour Pressure

For binary solution:

\[
\boxed{
P=P_A+P_B
}
\]

---

\[
P=P_A^0X_A+P_B^0X_B
\]

---

# PART G: COLLIGATIVE PROPERTIES

---

# 15. Colligative Properties

## Definition

Properties that depend only on the number of solute particles, not their nature.

---

Types:

1. Relative lowering of vapour pressure
2. Elevation of boiling point
3. Depression of freezing point
4. Osmotic pressure

---

# 16. Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{P^0-P}{P^0}=X_B
}
\]

---

where:

- P° = vapour pressure of pure solvent
- P = vapour pressure of solution

---

# 17. Elevation of Boiling Point

## Definition

Increase in boiling point of solvent due to addition of non-volatile solute.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta T_b=K_bm
}
\]

---

where:

- Kb = ebullioscopic constant
- m = molality

---

# 18. Depression of Freezing Point

## Definition

Decrease in freezing point of solvent due to dissolved solute.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta T_f=K_fm
}
\]

---

where:

- Kf = cryoscopic constant

---

# 19. Osmosis

## Definition

Movement of solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane from pure solvent to solution is called osmosis.

---

# 20. Osmotic Pressure

## Definition

The minimum pressure required to stop osmosis is called osmotic pressure.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\pi=CRT
}
\]

---

or:

\[
\boxed{
\pi=\frac{nRT}{V}
}
\]

---

where:

- π = osmotic pressure
- C = concentration
- R = gas constant
- T = temperature

---

# PART H: VAN'T HOFF FACTOR

---

# 21. Abnormal Molar Mass

Sometimes observed molar mass differs from calculated value due to:

- Association
- Dissociation

---

# 22. Van't Hoff Factor

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
i=
\frac{Observed\ colligative\ property}
{Calculated\ colligative\ property}
}
\]

---

For dissociation:

\[
i>1
\]

---

For association:

\[
i<1
\]

---

# Important Formula Sheet

## Molarity

\[
\boxed{
M=\frac{n}{V}
}
\]

---

## Molality

\[
\boxed{
m=\frac{n}{kg\ solvent}
}
\]

---

## Mole Fraction

\[
\boxed{
X_A=\frac{n_A}{n_A+n_B}
}
\]

---

## Henry's Law

\[
\boxed{
P=K_Hx
}
\]

---

## Raoult's Law

\[
\boxed{
P_A=P_A^0X_A
}
\]

---

## Boiling Point Elevation

\[
\boxed{
\Delta T_b=K_bm
}
\]

---

## Freezing Point Depression

\[
\boxed{
\Delta T_f=K_fm
}
\]

---

## Osmotic Pressure

\[
\boxed{
\pi=CRT
}
\]

---

---

## Example 1

1 mole solute in 2 L solution.

\[

\]

\[
=\frac12
\]

\[
\boxed{0.5M}
\]

---

Find mole fraction of solute if:

Solute moles = 2

Solvent moles = 8

\[
X_{solute}
=

\frac2{2+8}
\]

\]

---

## Example 3

A solution has:

\[
K_b=0.5
\]

and molality:

\[
m=2
\]

\]

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing molarity and molality.
- Using kg instead of litre in molarity.
- Forgetting temperature effect on solubility.
- Using Kelvin in osmotic pressure calculations.
- Confusing Raoult's law and Henry's law.
- Ignoring Van't Hoff factor for association/dissociation.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Solutions are homogeneous mixtures.
- Concentration can be expressed using molarity, molality and mole fraction.
- Henry's law explains gas solubility.
- Raoult's law explains vapour pressure of solutions.
- Colligative properties depend on number of solute particles.
- Osmotic pressure is useful for determining molecular mass.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Solutions connect molecular behaviour with measurable properties.}
}

# Introduction

Chemical bonding explains how atoms combine to form molecules and compounds.

Atoms form bonds to achieve:

- Stability
- Lower energy state
- Noble gas electronic configuration

This chapter covers:

- Lewis theory
- Octet rule
- Ionic and covalent bonds
- VSEPR theory
- Valence bond theory
- Hybridization
- Molecular shapes

---

# PART A: CHEMICAL BOND

---

# 1. Chemical Bond

## Definition

The attractive force that holds atoms together in a molecule or compound is called a chemical bond.

---

# 2. Types of Chemical Bonds

Main types:

1. Ionic bond
2. Covalent bond
3. Coordinate bond
4. Metallic bond

---

# PART B: LEWIS THEORY

---

# 3. Lewis Concept

Atoms combine by:

- Losing electrons
- Gaining electrons
- Sharing electrons

to achieve stable electronic configuration.

---

# 4. Lewis Symbols

The valence electrons of an element are represented by dots around its symbol.

Example:

Hydrogen:

\[
H\cdot
\]

---

# PART C: OCTET RULE

---

# 5. Octet Rule

## Statement

Atoms tend to gain, lose or share electrons to achieve eight electrons in their valence shell.

---

Examples:

### Sodium

Electronic configuration:

\[
2,8,1
\]

Loses one electron:

\[
Na\rightarrow Na^+ +e^-
\]

---

### Chlorine

\[
2,8,7
\]

Gains one electron:

\[
Cl+e^-\rightarrow Cl^-
\]

---

# 6. Limitations of Octet Rule

Octet rule fails for:

1. Incomplete octet compounds

Example:

\[
BeCl_2
\]

2. Expanded octet compounds

Example:

\[
SF_6
\]

3. Odd electron molecules

Example:

\[
NO
\]

---

# PART D: IONIC BOND

---

# 7. Ionic Bond

## Definition

A bond formed by transfer of electrons from one atom to another is called ionic bond.

---

Example:

Formation of NaCl:

\[
Na\rightarrow Na^++e^-
\]

\[
Cl+e^-\rightarrow Cl^-
\]

\[
Na^++Cl^-\rightarrow NaCl
\]

---

# 8. Properties of Ionic Compounds

- High melting point
- High boiling point
- Soluble in water
- Conduct electricity in molten state
- Hard crystalline solids

---

# PART E: COVALENT BOND

---

# 9. Covalent Bond

## Definition

A bond formed by sharing of electron pairs between atoms is called covalent bond.

---

Examples:

Hydrogen:

\[
H-H
\]

Oxygen:

\[
O=O
\]

Nitrogen:

\[
N\equiv N
\]

---

# 10. Types of Covalent Bonds

---

## Single Bond

One electron pair shared.

Example:

\[
H-H
\]

---

## Double Bond

Two electron pairs shared.

Example:

\[
O=O
\]

---

## Triple Bond

Three electron pairs shared.

Example:

\[
N\equiv N
\]

---

---

# 13. Order of Repulsion

\boxed{

}
\]

---

where:

LP = Lone pair

BP = Bond pair

# PART I: VALENCE BOND THEORY

---

# 19. Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

## Statement

A covalent bond forms by overlap of atomic orbitals containing unpaired electrons.

---

Types of overlap:

1. Sigma bond (σ)
2. Pi bond (π)

---

# 20. Sigma Bond

## Definition

Bond formed by head-on overlap of orbitals.

---

Properties:

- Strong bond
- Symmetrical
- Allows free rotation

---

# 21. Pi Bond

## Definition

Bond formed by sideways overlap of orbitals.

---

Properties:

- Weaker than sigma bond
- Restricts rotation

---

# Introduction

Organic reactions involve the breaking and formation of covalent bonds in carbon compounds.

# PART A: ELECTRON DISPLACEMENT EFFECTS

---

# 1. Electron Displacement

## Definition

The movement of electrons within an organic molecule due to the influence of atoms or groups is called electron displacement.

---

Types:

1. Inductive effect
2. Electromeric effect
3. Resonance effect
4. Hyperconjugation

---

# PART B: INDUCTIVE EFFECT

---

# 2. Inductive Effect

## Definition

The permanent displacement of sigma (σ) electrons along a carbon chain due to electronegativity differences is called inductive effect.

---

Characteristics:

- Operates through sigma bonds.
- Decreases with distance.
- Permanent effect.

---

# 3. Types of Inductive Effect

---

## +I Effect

Groups that release electrons through sigma bonds show +I effect.

---

Examples:

\[
-C(CH_3)_3 > -CH(CH_3)_2 > -C_2H_5 > -CH_3
\]

---

Electron donating groups:

- Alkyl groups

---

## -I Effect

Groups that withdraw electrons show -I effect.

---

Examples:

\[
-NO_2,\ -CN,\ -COOH,\ -F
\]

---

Electron withdrawing groups:

- Halogens
- Nitro group
- Carbonyl groups

---

# 4. Applications of Inductive Effect

---

## 1. Stability of Carbocations

+I groups increase carbocation stability.

---

## 2. Acidity of Organic Compounds

Electron withdrawing groups increase acidity.

Example:

\[
ClCH_2COOH > CH_3COOH
\]

---

# PART C: ELECTROMERIC EFFECT

---

# 5. Electromeric Effect

## Definition

The complete transfer of shared pi electrons to one atom in the presence of an attacking reagent is called electromeric effect.

---

Characteristics:

- Temporary effect
- Occurs only in compounds with multiple bonds
- Disappears after removal of reagent

---

# 6. Types of Electromeric Effect

---

## +E Effect

Pi electrons move towards the atom to which the reagent attaches.

---

Example:

Addition of H⁺ to alkene.

---

## -E Effect

Pi electrons move away from the atom where reagent attacks.

---

Example:

Carbonyl compounds.

---

# PART D: RESONANCE

---

# 7. Resonance

## Definition

The delocalization of pi electrons over two or more atoms is called resonance.

---

Characteristics:

- Molecule exists as resonance hybrid.
- Increases stability.
- Electrons are spread over the molecule.

---

# 8. Resonance Structures

Examples:

## Benzene

\[
C_6H_6
\]

has delocalized electrons.

---

## Carbonate Ion

\[
CO_3^{2-}
\]

has resonance structures.

---

# 9. Stability Due to Resonance

Greater resonance:

\[
\boxed{\text{Higher stability}}
\]

---

Examples:

Benzene is stable due to resonance.

---

# PART E: HYPERCONJUGATION

---

# 10. Hyperconjugation

## Definition

The delocalization of sigma electrons of C-H bonds adjacent to a double bond or positive charge is called hyperconjugation.

---

Also called:

\[
\boxed{\text{No bond resonance}}

# 1. Haloalkanes

## Definition

Organic compounds in which halogen atom is attached to an sp³ hybridised carbon atom of an alkyl group are called haloalkanes.

---

General formula:

\[
\boxed{R-X}
\]

where:

- R = alkyl group
- X = halogen

---

Examples:

Methyl chloride:

\[
CH_3Cl
\]

Ethyl bromide:

\[
C_2H_5Br
\]

---

# 2. Haloarenes

## Definition

Organic compounds in which halogen is directly attached to an aromatic ring are called haloarenes.

---

Example:

Chlorobenzene:

\[
C_6H_5Cl
\]

---

# PART B: CLASSIFICATION OF HALOALKANES

---

# 3. Based on Number of Halogen Atoms

---

## Monohaloalkanes

Contains one halogen atom.

Example:

\[
CH_3Cl
\]

---

## Dihaloalkanes

Contains two halogen atoms.

Example:

\[
CH_2Cl_2
\]

---

## Polyhaloalkanes

Contains more than two halogen atoms.

Example:

\[
CHCl_3
\]

---

# 4. Based on Carbon Attached to Halogen

---

## Primary Haloalkane (1°)

Halogen attached to carbon connected with one other carbon.

Example:

\[
CH_3CH_2Cl
\]

---

## Secondary Haloalkane (2°)

Halogen attached to carbon connected with two carbons.

Example:

\[
(CH_3)_2CHCl
\]

---

## Tertiary Haloalkane (3°)

Halogen attached to carbon connected with three carbons.

Example:

\[
(CH_3)_3CCl
\]

---

# PART F: NUCLEOPHILIC SUBSTITUTION REACTIONS

# 10. Nucleophile

## Definition

A species that donates an electron pair is called a nucleophile.

---

Examples:

\[
OH^-,CN^-,NH_3
\]

---

# 11. Nucleophilic Substitution

## Definition

A reaction in which a nucleophile replaces a halogen atom in haloalkane is called nucleophilic substitution.

---

General reaction:

\[
\boxed{
R-X+Nu^-
\rightarrow
R-Nu+X^-
}
\]

---

# PART G: SN1 REACTION

---

# 12. SN1 Mechanism

SN1 means:

\[
\boxed{\text{Substitution Nucleophilic Unimolecular}}
\]

---

Characteristics:

- Two-step mechanism
- Rate depends only on haloalkane concentration
- Carbocation intermediate formed

---

Rate equation:

\[
\boxed{
Rate=k[R-X]
}
\]

---

# Mechanism

Step 1:

Formation of carbocation:

\[
R-X\rightarrow R^++X^-
\]

---

Step 2:

Attack by nucleophile:

\[
R^++Nu^-
\rightarrow R-Nu
\]

---

# SN1 Reactivity Order

\[
\boxed{
3^\circ>2^\circ>1^\circ
}
\]

---

Reason:

Tertiary carbocations are more stable.

---

# PART H: SN2 REACTION

---

# 13. SN2 Mechanism

SN2 means:

\[
\boxed{\text{Substitution Nucleophilic Bimolecular}}
\]

---

Characteristics:

- One-step mechanism
- No intermediate
- Rate depends on both reactants

---

Rate equation:

\[
\boxed{
Rate=k[R-X][Nu^-]
}
\]

---

SN2 Reactivity Order:

\[
\boxed{
1^\circ>2^\circ>3^\circ
}
\]

---

Reason:

Less steric hindrance.

---

# PART I: COMPARISON OF SN1 AND SN2

| SN1                          | SN2                         |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Two steps                    | One step                    |
| Carbocation intermediate     | No intermediate             |
| First order reaction         | Second order reaction       |
| Favoured by tertiary halides | Favoured by primary halides |
| Racemization possible        | Inversion occurs            |

---

# Solved Examples

---

## Example 1

Identify type of haloalkane:

\[
(CH_3)_3CCl
\]

The carbon attached to chlorine is tertiary.

Answer:

\[
\boxed{\text{Tertiary haloalkane}}
\]

---

## Example 2

Which mechanism is preferred by CH₃Br?

Methyl bromide has minimum steric hindrance.

Answer:

\[
\boxed{SN2}
\]

---

## Example 3

Why does tert-butyl chloride undergo SN1 easily?

Because it forms a stable tertiary carbocation.

Answer:

\[
\boxed{\text{Stable }3^\circ\text{ carbocation}}
\]

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing haloalkanes and haloarenes.
- Forgetting SN1 involves carbocation formation.
- Applying SN1 order to SN2 reactions.
- Ignoring steric hindrance in SN2.
- Forgetting chlorobenzene is resonance stabilized.
- Confusing Sandmeyer and Finkelstein reactions.

---

# Chapter Summary

- Haloalkanes contain halogen attached to alkyl groups.
- Haloarenes contain halogen attached directly to aromatic rings.
- Nucleophilic substitution replaces halogen with nucleophile.
- SN1 occurs through carbocation intermediate.
- SN2 occurs through one-step backside attack.
- Haloarenes undergo electrophilic substitution due to aromatic stability.

\[
\boxed{
\text{The behaviour of halo compounds depends on carbon-halogen bond and reaction mechanism.}
}
\]

Environmental chemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies chemical processes occurring in the environment and their effects on living organisms.

It deals with:

- Air pollution
- Water pollution
- Soil pollution
- Greenhouse effect
- Acid rain
- Ozone depletion
