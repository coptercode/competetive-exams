# Introduction

Electrostatics is the branch of physics that studies **electric charges at rest**.

It deals with:

- Electric charge
- Force between charges
- Electric fields
- Electric potential
- Capacitors
- Energy stored in electric fields

Electrostatics plays an important role in:

- Electrical devices
- Electronics
- Communication systems
- Medical instruments

---

# PART A: ELECTRIC CHARGES

---

# 1. Electric Charge

## Definition

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical interactions.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{q}
\]

Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Coulomb (C)}}
\]

---

# Types of Charges

There are two types:

## Positive Charge

Example:

\[
\text{Proton}
\]

---

## Negative Charge

Example:

\[
\text{Electron}
\]

---

# Properties of Electric Charge

---

## 1. Additivity of Charge

Total charge is the algebraic sum of individual charges.

\[
\boxed{
Q=q_1+q_2+q_3+...
}
\]

---

## 2. Conservation of Charge

Electric charge can neither be created nor destroyed.

It can only be transferred.

---

## 3. Quantisation of Charge

Charge exists in discrete packets.

\[
\boxed{
q=ne
}
\]

where:

- n = integer
- e = charge of electron

\[
\boxed{
e=1.6\times10^{-19}C
}
\]

---

# 2. Coulomb's Law

## Statement

The electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of distance between them.

---

If two charges:

\[
q_1,q_2
\]

are separated by distance:

\[
r
\]

then:

\[
\boxed{
F\propto\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}
}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
F=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}
}
\]

---

Where:

\[
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
=

9\times10^9 Nm^2/C^2
\]

---

# Nature of Force

Like charges:

\[
\boxed{\text{Repel}}
\]

Unlike charges:

\[
\boxed{\text{Attract}}
\]

---

# 3. Principle of Superposition

## Statement

The net force on a charge is the vector sum of forces due to all other charges.

---

For several charges:

\[
\boxed{
\vec F=\vec F_1+\vec F_2+\vec F_3+...
}
\]

---

# PART B: ELECTRIC FIELD

---

# 4. Electric Field

## Definition

The region around a charge in which another charge experiences force is called electric field.

---

Electric field intensity:

\[
\boxed{
E=\frac{F}{q}
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{N/C}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{V/m}
\]

---

# Electric Field Due to Point Charge

For charge:

\[
Q
\]

at distance:

\[
r
\]

\[
\boxed{
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{Q}{r^2}
}
\]

---

# Direction of Electric Field

Positive charge:

\[
\boxed{\text{Away from charge}}
\]

Negative charge:

\[
\boxed{\text{Towards charge}}
\]

---

# 5. Electric Field Lines

Properties:

1. Start from positive charge.
2. End at negative charge.
3. Never intersect each other.
4. Density represents field strength.
5. Tangent gives field direction.

---

# 6. Electric Dipole

## Definition

A pair of equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance is called an electric dipole.

---

Charges:

\[
+q,-q
\]

Distance:

\[
2a
\]

---

# Dipole Moment

\[
\boxed{
p=q(2a)
}
\]

---

Direction:

From:

\[
-q \rightarrow +q
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{Cm}
\]

---

# Electric Field of Dipole

## Axial Position

\[
\boxed{
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{2p}{r^3}
}
\]

---

## Equatorial Position

\[
\boxed{
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{p}{r^3}
}
\]

(Direction opposite to dipole moment)

---

# 7. Torque on Electric Dipole

When placed in uniform electric field:

\[
\boxed{
\tau=pE\sin\theta
}
\]

where:

- p = dipole moment
- E = electric field
- θ = angle between p and E

---

Maximum torque:

\[
\boxed{
\tau_{max}=pE
}
\]

---

# PART C: ELECTRIC POTENTIAL

---

# 8. Electric Potential

## Definition

Electric potential at a point is the work done per unit charge in bringing a positive test charge from infinity to that point.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
V=\frac{W}{q}
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Volt (V)}}
\]

---

# Potential Due to Point Charge

\[
\boxed{
V=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{Q}{r}
}
\]

---

# 9. Potential Difference

The difference in potential between two points is:

\[
\boxed{
V_B-V_A
}
\]

---

Work done:

\[
\boxed{
W=q(V_B-V_A)
}
\]

---

# 10. Relation Between Electric Field and Potential

Electric field is the negative gradient of potential:

\[
\boxed{
E=-\frac{dV}{dr}
}
\]

---

# 11. Equipotential Surface

## Definition

A surface where every point has the same electric potential is called an equipotential surface.

---

Properties:

- No work is done moving charge along it.
- Electric field is perpendicular to it.
- Two equipotential surfaces never intersect.

---

# PART D: GAUSS'S LAW

---

# 12. Electric Flux

Electric flux measures the electric field passing through a surface.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Phi=EA\cos\theta
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
Nm^2/C
\]

---

# 13. Gauss's Law

## Statement

The total electric flux through a closed surface is equal to:

\[
\frac{1}{\epsilon_0}
\]

times the charge enclosed.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\Phi=
\frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}
}
\]

---

# Applications of Gauss Law

---

# 1. Infinite Line Charge

Electric field:

\[
\boxed{
E=
\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0r}
}
\]

where:

\[
\lambda
\]

is charge per unit length.

---

# 2. Infinite Plane Sheet

Electric field:

\[
\boxed{
E=
\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}
}
\]

---

# 3. Spherical Shell

Outside:

\[
\boxed{
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{Q}{r^2}
}
\]

Inside:

\[
\boxed{
E=0
}
\]

---

# PART E: CAPACITORS

---

# 14. Capacitor

## Definition

A device used to store electric charge is called a capacitor.

---

A capacitor consists of:

- Two conductors
- Insulating material between them

---

# Capacitance

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
C=\frac{Q}{V}
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Farad (F)}}
\]

---

# 15. Parallel Plate Capacitor

Capacitance:

\[
\boxed{
C=
\frac{\epsilon_0A}{d}
}
\]

where:

- A = area of plates
- d = separation

---

# With Dielectric

\[
\boxed{
C=
\frac{K\epsilon_0A}{d}
}
\]

where K is dielectric constant.

---

# 16. Combination of Capacitors

---

# Parallel Combination

Same potential:

\[
V=V_1=V_2
\]

Equivalent capacitance:

\[
\boxed{
C=C_1+C_2+C_3
}
\]

---

# Series Combination

Same charge:

\[
Q=Q_1=Q_2
\]

Equivalent capacitance:

\[
\boxed{
\frac1C=
\frac1{C_1}
+\frac1{C_2}
+\frac1{C_3}
}
\]

---

# 17. Energy Stored in Capacitor

Energy:

\[
\boxed{
U=\frac12CV^2
}
\]

Also:

\[
\boxed{
U=\frac12QV
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
U=\frac{Q^2}{2C}
}
\]

---

# Energy Density

Energy per unit volume:

\[
\boxed{
u=\frac12\epsilon_0E^2
}
\]

---

# Important Formula Sheet

## Coulomb's Law

\[
F=
\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}
\]

---

## Electric Field

\[
E=\frac Fq
\]

---

## Point Charge Field

\[
E=
\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac Q{r^2}
\]

---

## Dipole Moment

\[

\]

---

## Dipole Torque

\]

---

## Potential

\[

\]

---

## Capacitance

\[
C=\frac QV
\]

---

## Capacitor Energy

\[

\]

---

Two charges:

\[
q_1=2\mu C
\]

\[
q_2=3\mu C
\]

are separated by:

\[
r=2m
\]

Find force.

\[
F=
9\times10^9

\]

}
\]

---

## Example 2

Find capacitance if:

\[
Q=4C
\]

and:

\[

\]

\[
=\frac42
\]

\]

---

## Example 3

C=5F,\ V=10V

=\frac12(5)(100)

---

# Common Mistakes

- Forgetting that charge is quantised.
- Using scalar addition instead of vector addition for forces.
- Confusing electric field and potential.
- Forgetting the negative sign in:

\[
E=-\frac{dV}{dr}
\]

- Using wrong capacitance formula for series and parallel combinations.
- Confusing dielectric constant with permittivity.
- Forgetting that electric field inside a conductor is zero.

---

# Unit Summary

- Electrostatics deals with charges at rest.
- Coulomb's law gives force between charges.
- Electric field describes force per unit charge.
- Dipoles produce characteristic electric fields and torque.
- Potential represents electrical energy per unit charge.
- Gauss's law simplifies field calculations for symmetric charge distributions.
- Capacitors store electrical energy and charge.

\[
\boxed{

}

# Introduction

Current electricity deals with the **flow of electric charges through conductors**.

This unit explains:

- Electric current
- Drift velocity
- Ohm's law
- Resistance
- Kirchhoff's laws
- Electrical circuits
- Electrical power
- Potentiometer
- Thermoelectric effects

Applications include:

- Household circuits
- Electrical instruments
- Batteries
- Measuring devices

---

# PART A: ELECTRIC CURRENT

---

# 1. Electric Current

## Definition

The rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor is called electric current.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac{Q}{t}
}
\]

where:

- I = current
- Q = charge
- t = time

---

# SI Unit

\[
\boxed{\text{Ampere (A)}}
\]

---

# Direction of Current

Conventional current flows:

\[
\boxed{
\text{From positive terminal to negative terminal}
}
\]

Electron flow is opposite:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Negative to positive}
}
\]

---

# 2. Current Density

## Definition

Current flowing per unit cross-sectional area of conductor is called current density.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
J=\frac IA
}
\]

Unit:

\[
\boxed{A/m^2}
\]

---

# 3. Drift Velocity

## Definition

The average velocity acquired by free electrons in a conductor due to an applied electric field is called drift velocity.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{v_d}
\]

---

Without electric field:

Electrons move randomly.

With electric field:

Electrons acquire a small average velocity.

---

# Relation Between Current and Drift Velocity

\[
\boxed{
I=neAv_d
}
\]

where:

- n = number density of electrons
- e = charge of electron
- A = area of conductor

---

# 4. Mobility

## Definition

Mobility is the drift velocity produced per unit electric field.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\mu=\frac{v_d}{E}
}
\]

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
v_d=\mu E
}
\]

---

# PART B: OHM'S LAW AND RESISTANCE

---

# 5. Ohm's Law

## Statement

At constant temperature, current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it.

\[
\boxed{
V\propto I
}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
V=IR
}
\]

---

where:

- V = potential difference
- I = current
- R = resistance

---

# V-I Graph

For an ohmic conductor:

- Straight line
- Passes through origin

---

# 6. Resistance

## Definition

Resistance is the opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of current.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
R=\frac VI
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{\Omega}
\]

---

# 7. Resistivity

## Definition

Resistance of a conductor having unit length and unit area of cross-section is called resistivity.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\rho=\frac{RA}{l}
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{\Omega m}
\]

---

# Factors Affecting Resistance

\[
\boxed{
R=\rho\frac lA
}
\]

Resistance increases with:

- Length
- Temperature

Resistance decreases with:

- Larger area

---

# 8. Temperature Dependence of Resistance

For small temperature changes:

\[
\boxed{
R_t=R_0(1+\alpha\Delta T)
}
\]

where:

- \(R_0\) = resistance at 0°C
- α = temperature coefficient

---

# Temperature Coefficient

\[
\boxed{
\alpha=
\frac{R_t-R_0}{R_0T}
}
\]

---

# PART C: CELLS AND CIRCUITS

---

# 9. Electromotive Force (EMF)

## Definition

The energy supplied by a cell per unit charge is called EMF.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
E=\frac WQ
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{Volt}
\]

---

# 10. Internal Resistance

A cell has resistance inside it called internal resistance.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed r
\]

---

# Terminal Voltage

When current flows:

\[
\boxed{
V=E-Ir
}
\]

---

# Current in Circuit

\[
\boxed{
I=\frac E{R+r}
}
\]

where:

- R = external resistance
- r = internal resistance

---

# PART D: KIRCHHOFF'S LAWS

---

# 11. Kirchhoff's First Law

## Junction Rule

## Statement

The algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero.

\[
\boxed{
\sum I=0
}
\]

---

Based on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Conservation of charge}}
\]

---

# 12. Kirchhoff's Second Law

## Loop Rule

## Statement

The algebraic sum of potential differences in a closed loop is zero.

\[
\boxed{
\sum V=0
}
\]

---

Based on:

\[
\boxed{\text{Conservation of energy}}
\]

---

# Sign Convention

Moving through a resistor:

- Along current:

\[
-I R
\]

- Opposite current:

\[
+IR
\]

---

# PART E: WHEATSTONE BRIDGE

---

# 13. Wheatstone Bridge

A device used to measure unknown resistance.

Condition for balance:

\[
\boxed{
\frac PR=\frac QS
}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{
P S=Q R
}
\]

---

At balance:

\[
\boxed{
I_g=0
}
\]

No current flows through galvanometer.

---

# 14. Meter Bridge

A practical form of Wheatstone bridge.

Balance condition:

\[
\boxed{
\frac XR=
\frac l{100-l}
}
\]

where:

- X = unknown resistance
- R = known resistance
- l = balancing length

---

# PART F: POTENTIOMETER

---

# 15. Principle of Potentiometer

The potential drop across a uniform wire is directly proportional to its length.

\[
\boxed{
V\propto l
}
\]

---

# Potential Gradient

\[
\boxed{
k=\frac Vl
}
\]

---

# Comparison of EMF

For two cells:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{E_1}{E_2}
=

\frac{l_1}{l_2}
}
\]

---

# Applications

1. Comparing EMF of cells.
2. Measuring internal resistance.
3. Measuring potential difference.

---

# PART G: ELECTRIC POWER

---

Rate at which electrical energy is consumed.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
P=VI
}
\]

---

Using Ohm's law:

\[
\boxed{
P=I^2R
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
P=\frac{V^2}{R}
}
\]

---

# Electrical Energy

\[
\boxed{
W=Pt
}
\]

---

Commercial Unit:

\[
\boxed{kWh}
\]

---

# 17. Joule's Heating Law

## Statement

Heat produced in a resistor is directly proportional to:

- Square of current
- Resistance
- Time

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
H=I^2Rt
}
\]

---

# PART H: THERMOELECTRIC EFFECT

---

# 18. Thermoelectric Effect

---

# Seebeck Effect

When two dissimilar metals form a closed circuit and their junctions are maintained at different temperatures, an EMF is produced.

---

# Thermocouple

A device based on Seebeck effect.

Applications:

- Temperature measurement
- Thermal sensors

# Introduction

Magnetism is the branch of physics that studies magnetic fields and their effects on moving charges and current-carrying conductors.

This unit covers:

- Magnetic fields
- Bar magnets
- Magnetic dipoles
- Magnetic properties of materials
- Biot-Savart law
- Ampere's law
- Lorentz force
- Moving coil galvanometer

Applications include:

- Electric motors
- Generators
- Speakers
- Measuring instruments
- Magnetic storage devices

---

# PART A: MAGNETIC FIELD

---

# 1. Magnetic Field

## Definition

The region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor where magnetic force can be experienced is called a magnetic field.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{B}
\]

Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Tesla (T)}}
\]

---

# Magnetic Field Lines

Properties:

1. They emerge from the north pole and enter the south pole outside a magnet.
2. Inside the magnet, they move from south to north.
3. They form closed loops.
4. Two magnetic field lines never intersect.
5. Density of lines represents field strength.

---

# 2. Magnetic Dipole

A pair of equal and opposite magnetic poles separated by a distance is called a magnetic dipole.

Examples:

- Bar magnet
- Current loop

---

# Magnetic Dipole Moment

For a bar magnet:

\[
\boxed{
M=m(2l)
}
\]

where:

- m = pole strength
- 2l = magnetic length

Unit:

\[
\boxed{Am^2}
\]

---

# 3. Magnetic Field Due to Bar Magnet

---

# Axial Position

Magnetic field:

\[
\boxed{
B=
\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}
\frac{2M}{r^3}
}
\]

---

# Equatorial Position

Magnetic field:

\[
\boxed{
B=
\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}
\frac{M}{r^3}
}
\]

Direction:

Opposite to magnetic moment.

---

# 4. Torque on Magnetic Dipole

When a magnetic dipole is placed in a uniform magnetic field:

\[
\boxed{
\tau=MB\sin\theta
}
\]

where:

- M = magnetic moment
- B = magnetic field
- θ = angle between M and B

---

Maximum torque:

\[
\boxed{
\tau_{max}=MB
}
\]

---

# Potential Energy of Magnetic Dipole

\[
\boxed{
U=-MB\cos\theta
}
\]

---

# PART B: MAGNETIC MATERIALS

---

# 5. Magnetic Properties of Materials

Materials are classified into:

1. Diamagnetic
2. Paramagnetic
3. Ferromagnetic

---

# 6. Diamagnetic Materials

## Definition

Materials weakly repelled by magnetic fields are called diamagnetic materials.

Examples:

- Copper
- Silver
- Gold
- Water

---

Properties:

- Magnetic susceptibility is negative.

\[
\boxed{
\chi<0
}
\]

- Weak repulsion
- No permanent magnetism

---

# 7. Paramagnetic Materials

## Definition

Materials weakly attracted by magnetic fields are called paramagnetic materials.

Examples:

- Aluminium
- Oxygen
- Platinum

---

Properties:

\[
\boxed{
\chi>0
}
\]

- Weak attraction
- Lose magnetism when field is removed

---

# 8. Ferromagnetic Materials

## Definition

Materials strongly attracted by magnetic fields and capable of retaining magnetism are called ferromagnetic materials.

Examples:

- Iron
- Cobalt
- Nickel

---

Properties:

- Large positive susceptibility.

\[
\boxed{
\chi\gg0
}
\]

- Form magnetic domains.
- Can become permanent magnets.

---

\[

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing displacement current with conduction current.
- Forgetting that EM waves can travel through vacuum.
- Mixing wavelength and frequency order in spectrum.
- Forgetting:

\[
E=cB
\]

- Confusing X-rays and gamma rays.
- Forgetting that EM waves are transverse.
- Using incorrect radiation pressure formula.

# Unit Summary

- Maxwell showed that changing electric and magnetic fields create electromagnetic waves.
- Displacement current completed Ampere's law.
- Electromagnetic waves do not require a material medium.
- They travel at the speed of light.
- The electromagnetic spectrum ranges from radio waves to gamma rays.
- Different regions have different frequencies, wavelengths, and applications.

\[
\boxed{
c=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}
}
\]

is the fundamental equation describing electromagnetic wave propagation.
