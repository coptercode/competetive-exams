# Introduction

Ray optics deals with the study of light as a collection of rays travelling in straight lines.

It explains:

- Reflection of light
- Refraction of light
- Mirrors
- Lenses
- Prisms
- Optical instruments
- Total internal reflection

Applications:

- Cameras
- Microscopes
- Telescopes
- Optical fibres
- Spectacles

---

# PART A: REFLECTION OF LIGHT

---

# 1. Reflection

## Definition

The phenomenon in which light returns back into the same medium after striking a surface is called reflection.

---

# Laws of Reflection

## First Law

The incident ray, reflected ray and normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane.

---

## Second Law

The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.

\[
\boxed{
i=r
}
\]

---

# 2. Spherical Mirrors

A spherical mirror is a reflecting surface forming part of a sphere.

Types:

1. Concave mirror
2. Convex mirror

---

# Important Terms

## Pole (P)

Centre point of mirror surface.

---

## Centre of Curvature (C)

Centre of the sphere from which mirror is formed.

---

## Radius of Curvature (R)

Distance between P and C.

\[
\boxed{
R=PC
}
\]

---

## Principal Axis

Line passing through P and C.

---

## Focus (F)

Point where parallel rays meet or appear to diverge.

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
f=\frac R2
}
\]

---

# 3. Mirror Formula

For spherical mirrors:

\[
\boxed{
\frac1f=
\frac1v+\frac1u
}
\]

where:

- f = focal length
- v = image distance
- u = object distance

---

# 4. Magnification of Mirror

\[
\boxed{
m=-\frac vu
}
\]

---

Interpretation:

\[
m>1
\]

Magnified image

\[
m<1
\]

Diminished image

---

# Sign Convention (Cartesian)

- All distances measured from pole.
- Direction of incident light is positive reference.
- Distances opposite to light direction are negative.

---

# PART B: REFRACTION OF LIGHT

---

# 5. Refraction

## Definition

The bending of light when it passes from one medium to another is called refraction.

Reason:

Change in speed of light.

---

# 6. Refractive Index

## Definition

Refractive index measures how much a medium slows down light.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\mu=\frac cv
}
\]

where:

- c = speed of light in vacuum
- v = speed in medium

---

# Relative Refractive Index

\[
\boxed{
\mu_{21}
=

\frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1}
}
\]

---

# 7. Snell's Law

## Statement

For refraction:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}=\mu
}
\]

---

# 8. Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

## Definition

The complete reflection of light back into a denser medium when it attempts to enter a rarer medium is called total internal reflection.

---

# Conditions for TIR

1. Light must travel from denser to rarer medium.

2. Angle of incidence must be greater than critical angle.

\[
\boxed{
i>C
}
\]

---

# Critical Angle

The angle of incidence in denser medium for which angle of refraction becomes 90°.

\[
\boxed{
\sin C=\frac1\mu
}
\]

---

# Applications of TIR

- Optical fibres
- Diamond brilliance
- Periscopes
- Binoculars

---

# PART C: LENSES

---

# 9. Lens

A transparent optical device bounded by two surfaces is called a lens.

Types:

1. Convex lens
2. Concave lens

---

# Convex Lens

- Converging lens
- Positive focal length

---

# Concave Lens

- Diverging lens
- Negative focal length

---

# 10. Lens Formula

\[
\boxed{
\frac1f=
\frac1v-\frac1u
}
\]

where:

- f = focal length
- v = image distance
- u = object distance

---

# 11. Magnification of Lens

\[
\boxed{
m=\frac vu
}
\]

---

# 12. Power of Lens

## Definition

The ability of a lens to converge or diverge light is called power.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
P=\frac1f
}
\]

where f is in metres.

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Dioptre (D)}}
\]

---

For lenses in contact:

\[
\boxed{
P=P_1+P_2
}
\]

---

# 13. Lens Maker's Formula

The relation between focal length and curvature of lens surfaces is:

\[
\boxed{
\frac1f
=

(\mu-1)
\left(
\frac1{R_1}
-

\frac1{R_2}
\right)
}
\]

where:

- μ = refractive index
- R₁, R₂ = radii of curvature

---

# PART D: PRISMS

---

# 14. Prism

A prism is a transparent refracting medium bounded by two plane surfaces inclined at an angle.

---

# 15. Angle of Prism

The angle between two refracting surfaces is called angle of prism.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed A
\]

---

# 16. Deviation of Light

When light passes through a prism, it bends from its original path.

Angle of deviation:

\[
\boxed{\delta}
\]

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
\delta=i_1+i_2-A
}
\]

---

# 17. Minimum Deviation

At minimum deviation:

\[
\boxed{
i_1=i_2
}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{
r_1=r_2=\frac A2
}
\]

---

# Refractive Index of Prism

\[
\boxed{
\mu=
\frac{
\sin\frac{A+\delta_m}{2}
}
{
\sin\frac A2
}
}
\]

where:

- δₘ = minimum deviation

---

# PART E: OPTICAL FIBRE

---

# 18. Optical Fibre

A thin transparent fibre that transmits light using total internal reflection is called optical fibre.

---

# Structure

1. Core
2. Cladding
3. Protective coating

---

# Applications

- Communication
- Medical endoscopy
- Internet data transfer

> **Class:** 12

---

# Introduction

Wave optics deals with the **wave nature of light**.

It explains phenomena that cannot be explained by ray optics:

- Interference
- Diffraction
- Polarisation

Wave optics proves that light behaves as a transverse wave.

Applications:

- Optical instruments
- Holography
- Polarising devices
- Spectroscopy
- Communication technology

---

# PART A: WAVE THEORY OF LIGHT

---

# 1. Huygens' Principle

## Statement

Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets which spread in all directions with the speed of light.

---

# Wavefront

## Definition

A surface joining all points having the same phase of vibration is called a wavefront.

---

# Types of Wavefronts

## 1. Spherical Wavefront

Produced by:

- Point source of light

---

## 2. Plane Wavefront

Produced when:

- Source is very far away

---

## 3. Cylindrical Wavefront

Produced by:

- Line source

---

# PART B: INTERFERENCE OF LIGHT

---

# 2. Interference

## Definition

The phenomenon of redistribution of intensity due to superposition of two light waves is called interference.

---

# Principle of Superposition

When two waves overlap:

\[
\boxed{
y=y_1+y_2
}
\]

---

# Conditions for Sustained Interference

Two sources must be:

1. Coherent
2. Same frequency
3. Constant phase difference
4. Nearly equal intensity

---

# Coherent Sources

## Definition

Sources having the same frequency and constant phase difference are called coherent sources.

---

# Types of Interference

## 1. Constructive Interference

When two waves meet in same phase.

Condition:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta=n\lambda
}
\]

Intensity is maximum.

---

## 2. Destructive Interference

When two waves meet in opposite phase.

Condition:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta=(2n+1)\frac{\lambda}{2}
}
\]

Intensity is minimum.

---

# PART C: YOUNG'S DOUBLE SLIT EXPERIMENT (YDSE)

---

# 3. Young's Double Slit Experiment

Performed by:

\[
\boxed{\text{Thomas Young}}
\]

It proved the wave nature of light.

---

# Arrangement

Two coherent sources:

\[
S_1,S_2
\]

are separated by distance:

\[
d
\]

Screen distance:

\[
D
\]

where:

\[
D>>d
\]

---

# Path Difference

For any point on screen:

\[
\boxed{
\Delta=\frac{dx}{D}
}
\]

---

# 4. Fringe Width

## Definition

The distance between two successive bright or dark fringes is called fringe width.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{\beta}
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\beta=\frac{\lambda D}{d}
}
\]

where:

- λ = wavelength
- D = distance between screen and slit
- d = slit separation

---

# 5. Position of Bright Fringes

Condition:

\[
\boxed{
x_n=n\beta
}
\]

where:

\[
n=0,1,2,...
\]

---

# 6. Position of Dark Fringes

Condition:

\[
\boxed{
x_n=
(2n+1)\frac{\beta}{2}
}
\]

---

# 7. Intensity in Interference

Intensity:

\[
\boxed{
I=I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\phi
}
\]

---

For equal intensities:

Maximum intensity:

\[
\boxed{
I_{max}=4I_0
}
\]

Minimum intensity:

\[
\boxed{
I_{min}=0
}
\]

---

# PART D: DIFFRACTION OF LIGHT

---

# 8. Diffraction

## Definition

The bending of light around obstacles and spreading into the region of shadow is called diffraction.

---

Diffraction is significant when:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Obstacle size}\approx\lambda
}
\]

---

# 9. Single Slit Diffraction

When light passes through a narrow slit, diffraction pattern is formed.

---

# Condition for Minima

\[
\boxed{
a\sin\theta=n\lambda
}
\]

where:

- a = slit width
- n = order of minima

---

# 10. Width of Central Maximum

Angular width:

\[
\boxed{
\theta=\frac{2\lambda}{a}
}
\]

---

Linear width:

\[
\boxed{
W=
\frac{2D\lambda}{a}
}
\]

---

# Comparison: Interference vs Diffraction

| Interference                        | Diffraction                          |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Due to two coherent sources         | Due to bending around obstacle       |
| Bright fringes equally spaced       | Central maximum is widest            |
| Fringes have nearly equal intensity | Intensity decreases away from centre |

---

# PART E: POLARISATION

---

# 11. Polarisation

## Definition

The phenomenon of restricting vibrations of light waves to one direction is called polarisation.

---

It proves:

\[
\boxed{\text{Light is a transverse wave}}
\]

---

# 12. Unpolarised Light

Light vibrations occur in all directions perpendicular to propagation.

---

# 13. Plane Polarised Light

Light vibrations occur only in one direction.

---

# 14. Polaroids

## Definition

A polaroid is a material that allows vibrations only in one direction.

---

Applications:

- Sunglasses
- Camera filters
- LCD displays
- Stress analysis

---

# 15. Malus' Law

## Statement

The intensity of plane polarised light transmitted through an analyser is proportional to the square of cosine of angle between transmission axes.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
I=I_0\cos^2\theta
}
\]

---

# 16. Brewster's Law

## Statement

At a particular angle of incidence, the reflected light becomes completely polarised.

This angle is called polarising angle.

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\mu=\tan i_p
}
\]

where:

- μ = refractive index
- \(i_p\) = polarising angle

---

# Important Formula Sheet

## Interference Condition

\[
\Delta=n\lambda
\]

---

Destructive:

\[
\Delta=(2n+1)\frac{\lambda}{2}
\]

---

## Fringe Width

\[
\boxed{

}
\]

---

## Bright Fringe Position

\[
x_n=n\beta
\]

---

## Dark Fringe Position

\[
x_n=(2n+1)\frac{\beta}{2}
\]

---

## Diffraction Minimum

\[
a\sin\theta=n\lambda
\]

---

## Central Maximum Width

\[

\]

---

## Malus Law

\[
I=I_0\cos^2\theta
\]

---

## Brewster Law

\]

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing interference and diffraction.
- Forgetting that interference requires coherent sources.
- Using wrong fringe width formula.
- Confusing bright and dark fringe conditions.
- Forgetting that polarisation proves transverse nature of light.
- Applying Malus law without considering angle between axes.
- Mixing wavelength units.

---

# PART A: DUAL NATURE OF RADIATION

---

# 1. Nature of Light

Light shows two types of behaviour:

## Wave Nature

Explains:

- Interference
- Diffraction
- Polarisation

---

## Particle Nature

Explains:

- Photoelectric effect
- Compton effect

---

# 2. Quantum Theory of Light

Proposed by:

\[
\boxed{\text{Max Planck}}
\]

---

According to quantum theory:

Light energy is emitted or absorbed in packets called photons.

---

# Photon

## Definition

A photon is a packet of electromagnetic energy.

---

Energy of photon:

\[
\boxed{
E=h\nu
}
\]

where:

- h = Planck's constant
- ν = frequency

---

Also:

\[
\boxed{
E=\frac{hc}{\lambda}
}
\]

# PART B: PHOTOELECTRIC EFFECT

---

# 3. Photoelectric Effect

## Definition

The phenomenon of emission of electrons from a metal surface when light of suitable frequency falls on it is called photoelectric effect.

---

Emitted electrons:

\[
\boxed{\text{Photoelectrons}}
\]

---

# 4. Experimental Arrangement

Main parts:

- Photosensitive metal plate
- Light source
- Vacuum tube
- Collector electrode
- Battery

---

# 5. Hertz and Hallwachs Observations

---

## Hertz's Observation

Hertz discovered that ultraviolet light falling on metal surfaces produced sparks more easily.

---

## Hallwachs Observation

Hallwachs showed that:

- Zinc plate loses charge when exposed to UV light.
- Emission of electrons occurs.

---

# 6. Important Observations of Photoelectric Effect

---

## 1. Threshold Frequency

The minimum frequency required to eject electrons from a metal surface is called threshold frequency.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{\nu_0}
\]

---

If:

\[
\nu<\nu_0
\]

No photoelectric emission occurs.

---

## 2. Effect of Intensity

Increasing intensity increases:

\[
\boxed{\text{Number of photoelectrons}}
\]

but does not increase their kinetic energy.

---

## 3. Effect of Frequency

Increasing frequency increases:

\[
\boxed{\text{Kinetic energy of electrons}}
\]

---

## 4. Instantaneous Emission

Photoelectric emission occurs immediately without measurable delay.

---

# 7. Work Function

## Definition

Minimum energy required to remove an electron from a metal surface is called work function.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{\phi}
\]

---

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\phi=h\nu_0
}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\boxed{J}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{eV}
\]

---

# 8. Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

Einstein explained photoelectric effect using conservation of energy.

---

Energy of photon:

\[
h\nu
\]

is used for:

1. Removing electron:

\[
\phi
\]

2. Providing kinetic energy:

\[
K_{max}
\]

---

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
h\nu=\phi+K_{max}
}
\]

---

Since:

\[
K_{max}=\frac12mv^2
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
h\nu=\phi+\frac12mv^2
}
\]

---

# 9. Stopping Potential

## Definition

The minimum negative potential applied to stop photoelectrons is called stopping potential.

Symbol:

\[
\boxed{V_0}
\]

---

Relation:

\[
\boxed{
K_{max}=eV_0
}
\]

---

Einstein equation:

\[
\boxed{
h\nu=\phi+eV_0
}
\]

---

# 10. Graphs in Photoelectric Effect

---

## Kinetic Energy vs Frequency

Straight line:

\[
K_{max}=h\nu-\phi
\]

---

Slope:

\[
\boxed{h}
\]

---

## Photocurrent vs Intensity

Photocurrent increases with intensity.

# PART C: MATTER WAVES

---

# 11. de Broglie Hypothesis

## Statement

Every moving particle is associated with a wave called matter wave.

Proposed by:

\[
\boxed{\text{Louis de Broglie}}
\]

---

# 12. de Broglie Wavelength

Formula:

\[
\boxed{
\lambda=\frac hp
}
\]

where:

- h = Planck's constant
- p = momentum

---

Since:

\[
p=mv
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\lambda=\frac h{mv}
}
\]

---

# 13. Electron Accelerated Through Potential Difference

If electron is accelerated through voltage V:

\[
\boxed{
\lambda=
\frac h{\sqrt{2meV}}
}
\]

---

Practical formula:

\[
\boxed{
\lambda=
\frac{12.27}{\sqrt V}
\text{ Å}
}
\]

---

# 14. Properties of Matter Waves

- Associated with moving particles.
- Wavelength depends on momentum.
- Not electromagnetic waves.
- Significant for microscopic particles.

---

# PART D: DAVISSON-GERMER EXPERIMENT

---

# 15. Davisson-Germer Experiment

## Purpose

To prove the wave nature of electrons.

---

# Experiment

Electrons are accelerated and made to strike a nickel crystal.

The scattered electrons show diffraction pattern.

---

# Result

The measured wavelength agrees with de Broglie wavelength.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Electrons have wave nature}
}
\]

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing nanometre with micrometre.

Remember:

\[
1nm=10^{-9}m
\]

- Thinking nanomaterials have only smaller size, not different properties.
- Confusing quantum dots with ordinary semiconductor particles.
- Forgetting carbon nanotubes are made of carbon atoms.
- Mixing top-down and bottom-up approaches.
- Assuming all nanoparticles are naturally occurring.

# Unit Summary

- Nanotechnology studies materials at atomic and molecular scales.
- Nanomaterials have unique properties due to quantum effects and high surface area.
- Quantum dots are size-dependent semiconductor nanoparticles.
- Carbon nanotubes have exceptional strength and electrical properties.
- Nanotechnology has applications in medicine, electronics, energy and environment.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Nanotechnology controls matter at the scale of atoms and molecules.}
}
