# Introduction

The **Goods and Services Tax (GST)** is an indirect tax introduced in India on **1 July 2017**. It replaced multiple indirect taxes such as VAT, Excise Duty, Service Tax, and Entry Tax with a single tax system.

GST is collected at every stage of the supply chain, but the burden of tax is ultimately borne by the **final consumer**. Businesses can reduce their tax liability through the **Input Tax Credit (ITC)** mechanism.

---

# 1. Goods and Services Tax (GST)

## Definition

**GST (Goods and Services Tax)** is a destination-based indirect tax imposed on the supply of goods and services.

---

## Features of GST

- Single indirect tax system
- Eliminates cascading (tax on tax)
- Collected at every stage of supply
- Final tax burden is on the consumer
- Promotes transparency
- Supports Input Tax Credit (ITC)

---

## Components of GST

GST is divided into three parts:

### CGST (Central Goods and Services Tax)

Collected by the Central Government on **intra-state** transactions.

---

### SGST (State Goods and Services Tax)

Collected by the State Government on **intra-state** transactions.

---

### IGST (Integrated Goods and Services Tax)

Collected by the Central Government on **inter-state** transactions.

---

When the seller and buyer are located in the **same state**, GST is divided equally into:

- CGST

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **CGST/SGST** with **IGST**.
- Applying **CGST and SGST** to inter-state transactions instead of **IGST**.
- Forgetting to divide the GST rate equally for intra-state sales.
- Calculating GST on the invoice value instead of the taxable value.
- Ignoring the **Input Tax Credit (ITC)** while computing the net tax payable.
- Adding GST twice in multi-stage supply chain problems.
- Forgetting that the **final consumer cannot claim ITC**.
- Using incorrect GST percentages in calculations.

---

# Formula Sheet

| Formula                                  | Description      |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| **GST = Taxable Value × GST Rate / 100** | GST Amount       |
| **Invoice Value = Taxable Value + GST**  | Final Price      |
| **Taxable Value = Invoice Value − GST**  | Before Tax       |
| **CGST = GST ÷ 2**                       | Intra-State Tax  |
| **SGST = GST ÷ 2**                       | Intra-State Tax  |
| **IGST = Entire GST Amount**             | Inter-State Tax  |
| **Net GST = Output GST − Input GST**     | Tax Liability    |
| **ITC = GST Paid on Purchases**          | Input Tax Credit |

---

# Chapter Summary

- **GST (Goods and Services Tax)** is a destination-based indirect tax levied on the supply of goods and services.
- **Intra-state transactions** attract **CGST and SGST**, each charged at half of the total GST rate.
- **Inter-state transactions** attract **IGST**, which is equal to the full GST rate.
- **Input Tax Credit (ITC)** enables businesses to deduct the GST paid on purchases from the GST collected on sales.
- The **Net Tax Liability** is calculated by subtracting the **Input Tax Credit** from the **Output GST**.
- In a **multi-stage supply chain**, each dealer pays GST only on the value added, while the **final consumer bears the entire tax burden**.
- Correct use of GST formulas is essential for preparing invoices and solving board examination problems.
- GST simplifies taxation, reduces cascading taxes, and improves transparency in the tax system.

# Introduction

A **Recurring Deposit (RD)** is a savings scheme offered by banks and post offices in which a fixed amount is deposited every month for a specified period. The bank pays **simple interest** on each monthly installment for the remaining period until maturity.

Recurring Deposits encourage regular savings and help individuals accumulate money with interest over time.

---

# 1. Recurring Deposit (RD)

## Definition

A **Recurring Deposit (RD)** is a bank account in which a customer deposits a fixed amount every month for a fixed period and earns interest on those deposits.

---

## Features of RD

- Fixed monthly installment
- Fixed tenure
- Fixed rate of interest
- Interest is calculated using Simple Interest
- Safe investment
- Guaranteed maturity amount

---

## Terms Used in RD

### Monthly Installment (P)

The fixed amount deposited every month.

---

### Time Period (n)

The total duration of the deposit in months.

---

### Rate of Interest (r)

Annual rate of simple interest offered by the bank.

---

### Interest (I)

The extra money earned from the bank.

---

### Maturity Value (MV)

The total amount received at the end of the RD period.

```
Maturity Value

=

Total Deposits

+

Interest
```

---

# 2. Interest Formula for Recurring Deposit

The interest on an RD account is calculated using the following formula.

## Formula

```
I

=

P × n(n+1)

────────────── × r
   2 × 12      ───
                100
```

or

```
I

=

P × n(n+1) × r

──────────────────

2400
```

where

- **P** = Monthly installment
- **n** = Number of months
- **r** = Annual rate of interest

---

## Total Deposit

```
Total Deposit

=

P × n
```

---

## Maturity Value

```
MV

=

P × n

+

I
```

---

# 3. Finding the Monthly Installment (P)

Sometimes the maturity value or interest is given, and the monthly installment must be found.

---

## Formula

From

```
I

=

P × n(n+1) × r

──────────────────

2400
```

Rearranging,

```
P

=

I × 2400

────────────────

n(n+1) × r
```

---

## Using Maturity Value

If maturity value is given,

Step 1

```
Interest

=

MV

−

Total Deposits
```

Step 2

Use the interest formula to calculate **P**.

---

# 4. Finding the Time Period (n)

If the monthly installment, maturity value, and interest rate are known, the time period can be determined.

---

## Procedure

1. Find the interest.

```
I = MV − Total Deposit
```

2. Substitute the values into the interest formula.

3. Simplify to obtain a quadratic equation.

4. Solve the equation to find **n**.

---

## Note

The value of **n** must always be a positive whole number representing the number of months.

---

# 5. Finding the Rate of Interest (r)

If the monthly installment, number of months, and interest earned are known,

the rate of interest is

```
r

=

I × 2400

────────────────

P × n(n+1)
```

---

- Emergency funds
- Vacation planning
- Purchasing vehicles
- Future investments

# Common Mistakes

- Using **Compound Interest** instead of the **Recurring Deposit Interest Formula**.
- Forgetting that **n** is measured in **months**, not years.
- Using the annual installment instead of the monthly installment.
- Calculating the maturity value without adding the interest.
- Making errors while simplifying **n(n + 1)**.
- Using the wrong value of **r** (write it as a percentage, not a decimal).
- Confusing **Total Deposit** with **Maturity Value**.
- Forgetting to convert the given information correctly before applying the formula.

---

# Formula Sheet

| Formula                           | Description                          |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| **I = P × n(n+1) × r / 2400**     | Interest on Recurring Deposit        |
| **Total Deposit = P × n**         | Total Amount Deposited               |
| **MV = P × n + I**                | Maturity Value                       |
| **P = I × 2400 / [n(n+1) × r]**   | Monthly Installment                  |
| **r = I × 2400 / [P × n(n+1)]**   | Rate of Interest                     |
| **Interest = MV − Total Deposit** | Finding Interest from Maturity Value |

---

# Chapter Summary

- A **Recurring Deposit (RD)** is a banking scheme where a fixed amount is deposited every month for a fixed period.
- Interest on an RD is calculated using the **Simple Interest-based RD formula**, not the compound interest formula.
- The **interest** earned depends on the **monthly installment (P)**, **number of months (n)**, and **annual rate of interest (r)**.
- The **Maturity Value (MV)** is the sum of the **total deposits** and the **interest earned**.
- The RD formula can be rearranged to find the **monthly installment**, **time period**, or **rate of interest** when other values are known.
- Recurring Deposits promote disciplined savings and are widely used for future financial goals.
- Mastering the RD formulas and careful substitution of values are essential for solving ICSE Class 10 examination problems accurately.

# Chapter Summary

- A **share** represents a unit of ownership in a company, and a **shareholder** earns a portion of the company's profits as **dividend**.
- **Face Value** is the nominal value of a share, whereas **Market Value** is the current trading price in the stock market.
- Shares may be issued **at par**, **at premium**, or **at discount**, depending on the relationship between the market value and face value.
- The **investment** required to purchase shares depends on the **market value**, while the **dividend** is always calculated using the **face value**.
- The **rate of return (yield)** measures the profitability of an investment as a percentage of the amount invested.
- In **share interchange** problems, the proceeds from selling one company's shares are used to purchase another company's shares.
- Accurate use of formulas and a clear understanding of the difference between **face value** and **market value** are essential for solving ICSE Class 10 examination questions.

# 2. Rules for Solving Linear Inequations

## Rule 1: Addition

Adding the same number to both sides does **not** change the inequality.

```

```


Subtracting the same number from both sides does **not** change the inequality.

Example

Subtract 8.

Multiplying both sides by a **positive number** does **not** change the inequality.


Multiply by 3.

```
x > 12
```

---

## Rule 4: Division

Dividing both sides by a **positive number** does **not** change the inequality.

Example

```
4x ≥ 20
```

Divide by 4.

```
x ≥ 5
```

# 1. Polynomial

## Definition

A **Polynomial** is an algebraic expression consisting of variables and constants combined using addition, subtraction, and multiplication, where the exponents of the variables are non-negative integers.

---

## General Form

```
P(x)

=

aₙxⁿ + aₙ₋₁xⁿ⁻¹ + ... + a₁x + a₀
```

where

- **aₙ ≠ 0**
- **n** is the degree of the polynomial.

---

## Examples

```
2x² + 5x − 3
```

```
x³ − 4x + 6
```

```
5x⁴ + 2x² − 9
```

---

# 2. Remainder Theorem

## Statement

If a polynomial

```
P(x)
```

is divided by

```
(x − a)
```

then the remainder is

```
P(a)
```

---

## Formula

If

```
P(x)

÷

(x − a)
```

then

```
Remainder

=

P(a)
```

---

## Example

Find the remainder when

```
P(x)

=

x² + 5x + 6
```

is divided by

```
(x − 2)
```

### Solution

```
P(2)

=

2² + 5(2) + 6

=

4 + 10 + 6

=

20
```

### Answer

```
Remainder = 20
```

---

# 3. Factor Theorem

## Statement

A polynomial

```
P(x)
```

has

```
(x − a)
```

as a factor **if and only if**

```
P(a) = 0
```

---

## Formula

If

```
P(a)=0
```

then

```
(x − a)
```

is a factor.

---

## Example

Check whether

```
(x − 3)
```

is a factor of

```
x² − 5x + 6
```

### Solution

```
P(3)

=

9 −15 +6

=

0
```

Since

```
P(3)=0
```

Therefore,

```
(x−3)
```

is a factor.

---

# 4. Finding Unknown Constants

The Remainder and Factor Theorems help determine unknown coefficients in a polynomial.

---

## Procedure

1. Substitute the given value of

```
x
```

into the polynomial.

2. Use

```
P(a)=0
```

or the given remainder.

3. Solve the resulting linear equation.

---

## Example

Find

```
k
```

if

```
(x−2)
```

is a factor of

```
x² + kx − 6
```

### Solution

```
P(2)

=

4 +2k −6

=

0
```

```
2k−2=0
```

```
k=1
```

# 5. Complete Factorization

Higher-degree polynomials can be factorized completely using the Factor Theorem.

---

## Steps

1. Find one factor using the Factor Theorem.
2. Divide the polynomial by that factor.
3. Obtain the quotient.
4. Factorize the quotient further.

---

## Example

Factorize

```
x³ −6x² +11x −6
```

### Step 1

Check

```
P(1)
```

```
1−6+11−6

=

0
```

Hence,

```
(x−1)
```

is a factor.

---

### Step 2

Divide by

```
(x−1)
```

Quotient

```
x²−5x+6
```

---

### Step 3

Factorize

```
x²−5x+6
```

```
(x−2)(x−3)
```

---

### Final Answer

```
(x−1)(x−2)(x−3)
```

---

# Synthetic Division (Brief Overview)

Synthetic division is a shortcut method for dividing a polynomial by a linear factor of the form

```
(x−a)
```

It is commonly used after identifying a factor using the Factor Theorem.

---

# Relationship Between the Two Theorems

| Remainder Theorem                          | Factor Theorem                        |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| Gives the remainder when divided by (x−a). | Determines whether (x−a) is a factor. |
| Remainder = P(a)                           | If P(a)=0, then (x−a) is a factor.    |

---

# Applications

The Remainder and Factor Theorems are used in:

- Polynomial factorization
- Solving polynomial equations
- Finding unknown coefficients
- Algebraic simplification
- Curve analysis
- Computer algorithms
- Engineering and physics calculations

# Common Mistakes

- Substituting the wrong value of **x**. Remember that dividing by **(x − a)** means substitute **x = a**, while dividing by **(x + a)** means substitute **x = −a**.
- Confusing the **Remainder Theorem** with the **Factor Theorem**.
- Forgetting that **P(a) = 0** is necessary for **(x − a)** to be a factor.
- Making arithmetic mistakes while evaluating the polynomial.
- Incorrectly factorizing the quotient polynomial after division.
- Ignoring negative signs during substitution.
- Assuming a polynomial has a factor without verification.
- Using synthetic division incorrectly after identifying a factor.

# Chapter Summary

- A **Polynomial** is an algebraic expression with variables raised to non-negative integer powers.
- The **Remainder Theorem** states that the remainder obtained when dividing a polynomial **P(x)** by **(x − a)** is **P(a)**.
- The **Factor Theorem** states that **(x − a)** is a factor of **P(x)** if and only if **P(a) = 0**.
- These theorems help determine unknown coefficients, verify factors, and simplify polynomial division.
- Higher-degree polynomials can be completely factorized by first identifying one factor using the **Factor Theorem**, then dividing to obtain the remaining factors.
- Synthetic division provides a quicker method for dividing a polynomial by a linear factor.
- The Remainder and Factor Theorems are fundamental tools in algebra and are frequently tested in ICSE Class 10 board examinations.

# Introduction

A **Matrix** is a rectangular arrangement of numbers, variables, or algebraic expressions arranged in rows and columns. Matrices are widely used in mathematics, computer science, engineering, economics, graphics, and data analysis. They simplify solving systems of equations and handling large sets of numerical data.

## Definition

A **Matrix** is a rectangular array of elements arranged in rows and columns and enclosed within brackets.

- **n** = Number of columns

# Common Mistakes

- Adding or subtracting matrices of **different orders**.
- Multiplying matrices without checking the compatibility condition (**columns of the first = rows of the second**).
- Assuming **AB = BA** for all matrices.
- Confusing the **order** of a matrix with the number of elements.
- Multiplying corresponding elements instead of using the row-by-column rule.
- Incorrectly identifying an identity matrix.
- Forgetting to multiply every element during scalar multiplication.
- Making arithmetic errors while performing matrix operations.

# Chapter Summary

- A **Matrix** is a rectangular arrangement of numbers in rows and columns.
- The **order** of a matrix is expressed as **m × n**, where **m** is the number of rows and **n** is the number of columns.
- Matrices of the same order can be **added** or **subtracted** by performing operations on corresponding elements.
- In **scalar multiplication**, every element of the matrix is multiplied by the given constant.
- Matrix multiplication is possible only when the **number of columns of the first matrix equals the number of rows of the second matrix**.
- Matrix multiplication is **associative** and **distributive**, but **not commutative** in general.
- The **Identity Matrix** acts like the number 1 in ordinary multiplication, satisfying **AI = IA = A**.
- Matrices have wide applications in computer science, engineering, artificial intelligence, data science, economics, and many other fields.

# Introduction

A **Progression** is a sequence of numbers that follows a specific pattern. The two most common types are **Arithmetic Progression (AP)** and **Geometric Progression (GP)**.

- In an **Arithmetic Progression**, the difference between consecutive terms is constant.
- In a **Geometric Progression**, the ratio between consecutive terms is constant.

Progressions are widely used in finance, banking, population growth, depreciation, investments, engineering, and scientific calculations.

---

# 1. Sequence

## Definition

A **Sequence** is an ordered arrangement of numbers that follows a particular rule.

### Examples

```
2, 4, 6, 8, 10, ...
```

```
3, 9, 27, 81, ...
```

---

# 2. Arithmetic Progression (AP)

## Definition

An **Arithmetic Progression (AP)** is a sequence in which the difference between any two consecutive terms is constant.

---

## General Form

```
a,

a+d,

a+2d,

a+3d,

...
```

where

- **a** = First Term
- **d** = Common Difference

---

### Formula

```
d

=

Second Term

−

First Term
```

```

```

### Example

```

=

8−5

=

3
```

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing the **common difference (d)** in an AP with the **common ratio (r)** in a GP.
- Using the AP formulas for GP problems and vice versa.
- Forgetting that the **nth term** starts with **(n − 1)** in the exponent or multiplier.
- Applying the GP sum formula incorrectly when **r = 1**.
- Using the wrong version of the GP sum formula without considering the sign of **r**.
- Incorrectly calculating powers while finding GP terms.
- Forgetting to identify the first term correctly.
- Making arithmetic errors while simplifying expressions.

| **tₙ = a + (n−1)d**        | nth Term of AP         |
| **Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n−1)d]** | Sum of AP              |
| **Sₙ = n/2 (a + l)**       | Alternative AP Sum     |
| **r = t₂ / t₁**            | Common Ratio (GP)      |
| **tₙ = ar⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾**           | nth Term of GP         |
| **Sₙ = a(rⁿ−1)/(r−1)**     | Sum of GP (r ≠ 1)      |
| **Sₙ = a(1−rⁿ)/(1−r)**     | Alternative GP Sum     |
| **Sₙ = na**                | GP Sum when r = 1      |
| **S∞ = a/(1−r)**           | Infinite GP (          | r   | < 1) |

---

# Chapter Summary

- A **Progression** is a sequence of numbers that follows a specific pattern.
- In an **Arithmetic Progression (AP)**, consecutive terms differ by a constant **common difference (d)**.
- The **nth term** of an AP is given by **tₙ = a + (n − 1)d**, and the **sum of the first n terms** is **Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d]**.
- In a **Geometric Progression (GP)**, consecutive terms are related by a constant **common ratio (r)**.
- The **nth term** of a GP is **tₙ = ar⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾**, and the **sum of the first n terms** is **Sₙ = a(rⁿ − 1)/(r − 1)** for **r ≠ 1**.
- When **|r| < 1**, an infinite GP has the finite sum **S∞ = a/(1 − r)**.
- Arithmetic and Geometric Progressions have numerous applications in finance, economics, population studies, engineering, and scientific modeling.
- A clear understanding of the formulas and careful substitution of values are essential for solving ICSE Class 10 examination questions accurately.

# Introduction

**Reflection** is a type of geometric transformation in which a figure is flipped over a line called the **line of reflection** to produce its mirror image. During reflection, the **shape and size of the figure remain unchanged**, but its orientation is reversed. Reflection is widely used in geometry, architecture, engineering, computer graphics, robotics, and image processing.

---

# 1. Reflection

## Definition

A **Reflection** is a transformation that produces the mirror image of a figure about a fixed line called the **Mirror Line** or **Line of Reflection**.

---

## Properties of Reflection

- Shape remains unchanged.
- Size remains unchanged.
- Corresponding angles remain equal.
- Corresponding sides remain equal.
- Image is the mirror image of the original figure.
- Distance from the mirror line remains the same.

---

# 2. Reflection in the X-axis

## Rule

# Introduction

**Similarity** is an important concept in Geometry that deals with figures having the **same shape but not necessarily the same size**. Similar figures have equal corresponding angles and proportional corresponding sides. Similarity is widely used in map drawing, architecture, engineering, photography, surveying, and computer graphics.

---

# 1. Similar Figures

## Definition

Two figures are said to be **Similar** if

- Their corresponding angles are equal.
- Their corresponding sides are proportional.

---

## Symbol

```
∆ABC ∼ ∆DEF
```

is read as

```
Triangle ABC is similar to Triangle DEF.
```

---

## Properties of Similar Figures

- Corresponding angles are equal.
- Corresponding sides are proportional.
- Same shape.
- Different sizes are possible.
- Corresponding medians, angle bisectors, and altitudes are proportional.

---

# 2. Similar Triangles

## Definition

Two triangles are **Similar** if they satisfy any one of the similarity criteria.

---

# 3. Criteria for Similarity of Triangles

## (i) AA (Angle-Angle) Criterion

### Statement

If two angles of one triangle are respectively equal to two angles of another triangle, then the triangles are similar.

---

### Example

```
∠A = ∠D

∠B = ∠E
```

Then

```
∆ABC ∼ ∆DEF
```

---

## (ii) SAS (Side-Angle-Side) Criterion

### Statement

If one angle of a triangle is equal to one angle of another triangle and the including sides are proportional, then the triangles are similar.

---

### Condition

```
AB      AC
──  =  ──
DE      DF
```

and

```
∠A = ∠D
```

---

## (iii) SSS (Side-Side-Side) Criterion

### Statement

If the corresponding sides of two triangles are proportional, then the triangles are similar.

---

### Condition

```
AB      BC      AC
──  =  ──  =  ──
DE      EF      DF
```

---

# 4. Ratio of Areas of Similar Triangles

## Theorem

The ratio of the areas of two similar triangles is equal to the square of the ratio of their corresponding sides.

---

## Formula

```
Area₁      AB²
─────  =  ─────
Area₂      DE²
```

or

```
Area₁      (Corresponding Side)²
─────  =  -----------------------
Area₂      (Corresponding Side)²
```

---

## Example

If

```
AB : DE

=

2 : 5
```

Then

```
Area Ratio

=

4 : 25
```

---

# 5. Ratio of Perimeters

For similar triangles,

## Formula

```
Perimeter₁      Side₁
──────────  =  ─────
Perimeter₂      Side₂
```

---

### Example

If

```
AB : DE

=

3 : 4
```

Then

```
Perimeter Ratio

=

3 : 4
```

---

# 6. Basic Proportionality Theorem (BPT)

## Statement

If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides, then it divides those two sides in the same ratio.

---

### Figure

In

```
∆ABC
```

If

```
DE || BC
```

then

```
AD      AE
──  =  ──
DB      EC
```

---

# Converse of BPT

## Statement

If a line divides two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, then that line is parallel to the third side.

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **similar** figures with **congruent** figures.
- Writing the corresponding vertices in the wrong order.
- Using the **AA Criterion** when corresponding angles are not correctly matched.

- Incorrectly identifying corresponding sides in similar triangles.
- Making arithmetic mistakes while simplifying ratios.

# Introduction

A **Circle** is one of the most important geometrical figures. It is the set of all points in a plane that are at a constant distance from a fixed point called the **centre**. Circles are widely used in engineering, architecture, astronomy, transportation, construction, and computer graphics. Understanding the properties and theorems related to circles helps in solving many geometrical problems.

---

# 1. Circle

## Definition

A **Circle** is the collection of all points in a plane that are at an equal distance from a fixed point called the **Centre**.

---

## Parts of a Circle

- Centre
- Radius
- Diameter
- Chord
- Arc
- Sector
- Segment
- Tangent
- Secant

---

# 2. Radius

## Definition

A **Radius** is the line segment joining the centre of the circle to any point on the circle.

---

## Symbol

```
r
```

---

# 3. Diameter

## Definition

A **Diameter** is a chord that passes through the centre of the circle.

---

## Formula

```
Diameter = 2 × Radius
```

or

```
d = 2r
```

---

# 4. Chord

## Definition

A **Chord** is a line segment joining any two points on the circumference of the circle.

### Important Fact

The **Diameter** is the longest chord of a circle.

---

# 5. Arc

## Definition

An **Arc** is a part of the circumference of a circle.

### Types

- Minor Arc
- Major Arc
- Semicircular Arc

---

# 6. Sector

## Definition

A **Sector** is the region enclosed by two radii and the intercepted arc.

### Types

- Minor Sector
- Major Sector

---

# 7. Segment

## Definition

A **Segment** is the region enclosed by a chord and its corresponding arc.

### Types

- Minor Segment
- Major Segment

---

# 8. Tangent

## Definition

A **Tangent** is a line that touches the circle at exactly one point.

This point is called the **Point of Contact**.

---

## Theorem 1

The tangent at any point of a circle is **perpendicular** to the radius through the point of contact.

### Statement

If

```
PT
```

is a tangent at

```
P
```

then

```
OP ⟂ PT
```

where

```
O
```

is the centre.

---

# 9. Secant

## Definition

A **Secant** is a line that intersects the circle at two distinct points.

---

# 10. Equal Chords Theorem

## Statement

Equal chords of a circle are equidistant from the centre.

---

## Converse

Chords equidistant from the centre are equal.

---

# 11. Perpendicular from Centre to Chord

## Theorem

The perpendicular drawn from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord.

---

### Converse

The line joining the centre to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to the chord.

---

# 12. Angles in the Same Segment

## Theorem

Angles subtended by the same chord in the same segment are equal.

---

### Formula

If

```
∠ABC
```

and

```
∠ADC
```

stand on the same chord,

then

```
∠ABC = ∠ADC
```

---

# 13. Angle in a Semicircle

## Theorem

The angle subtended by a diameter at the circumference is always a right angle.

---

### Formula

If

```
AB
```

is a diameter,

then

```
∠ACB = 90°
```

---

# 14. Cyclic Quadrilateral

## Definition

A quadrilateral whose four vertices lie on the same circle is called a **Cyclic Quadrilateral**.

---

## Theorem

The opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary.

---

## Formula

```
∠A + ∠C = 180°
```

```
∠B + ∠D = 180°
```

---

# 15. Tangents from an External Point

## Theorem

The lengths of tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.

---

### Formula

If

```
PA

and

PB
```

are tangents,

then

```
PA = PB
```

- A **Circle** is the set of all points equidistant from a fixed point called the **centre**.
- The **Radius** joins the centre to a point on the circle, while the **Diameter** is the longest chord and equals **2r**.
