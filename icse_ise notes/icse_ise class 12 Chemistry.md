# Introduction

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more components.

A solution consists of:

```
Solution

   │

 ┌─┴─────────┐

 ▼           ▼

Solvent    Solute
```

---

# 1. Components of Solution

## Solvent

The component present in larger quantity that dissolves another substance.

Example:

In salt water:

```
Water → Solvent
```

---

## Solute

The component present in smaller quantity that gets dissolved.

Example:

```
Salt → Solute
```

---

# 2. Types of Solutions

Based on physical state:

| Solute | Solvent | Example          |
| ------ | ------- | ---------------- |
| Gas    | Gas     | Air              |
| Gas    | Liquid  | CO₂ in water     |
| Liquid | Liquid  | Alcohol in water |
| Solid  | Liquid  | Salt in water    |
| Solid  | Solid   | Alloys           |

---

# 3. Concentration of Solutions

Concentration represents the amount of solute present in a given amount of solution.

---

# 1. Mass Percentage

## Definition

Mass of solute present in 100 g of solution.

Formula:

\[
Mass\% =
\frac{Mass\ of\ solute}{Mass\ of\ solution}
\times100
\]

---

Example:

10 g salt + 90 g water

Mass of solution:

= 100 g

Mass percentage:

\[
=\frac{10}{100}\times100
\]

= 10%

---

# 2. Volume Percentage

## Definition

Volume of solute present in 100 mL solution.

Formula:

\[
Volume\%=
\frac{Volume\ of\ solute}{Volume\ of\ solution}
\times100
\]

---

Example:

20 mL alcohol in 100 mL solution:

Volume %

= 20%

---

# 3. Mass by Volume Percentage

Formula:

\[
Mass/Volume\%=
\frac{Mass\ of\ solute}{Volume\ of\ solution}
\times100
\]

---

Example:

5 g glucose in 100 mL solution

= 5% (w/v)

---

# 4. Parts Per Million (ppm)

Used for very dilute solutions.

Formula:

\[
ppm=
\frac{Number\ of\ parts\ of\ component}
{Total\ parts\ of\ solution}
\times10^6
\]

---

Applications:

- Pollution analysis
- Water quality testing

---

# 5. Mole Fraction

## Definition

Ratio of number of moles of a component to total moles of all components.

Formula:

\[
X_A=
\frac{n_A}{n_A+n_B}
\]

---

For binary solution:

\[
X_A+X_B=1
\]

---

# 6. Molarity (M)

## Definition

Number of moles of solute present in one litre of solution.

Formula:

\[
M=
\frac{Number\ of\ moles\ of\ solute}
{Volume\ of\ solution\ in\ litre}
\]

---

Unit:

```
mol L⁻¹
```

---

# 7. Molality (m)

## Definition

Number of moles of solute present in one kilogram of solvent.

Formula:

\[
m=
\frac{Moles\ of\ solute}
{Mass\ of\ solvent\ in\ kg}
\]

---

Unit:

```
mol kg⁻¹
```

---

# Difference Between Molarity and Molality

| Molarity                    | Molality                 |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Based on volume of solution | Based on mass of solvent |
| Temperature dependent       | Temperature independent  |
| Unit: mol/L                 | Unit: mol/kg             |

---

# 4. Solubility

## Definition

Maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a specific temperature.

---

# Factors Affecting Solubility

## 1. Nature of Solute and Solvent

"Like dissolves like"

Polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents.

---

## 2. Temperature

For solids:

Solubility usually increases with temperature.

---

For gases:

Solubility decreases with temperature.

---

## 3. Pressure

Mainly affects gases.

Higher pressure:

→ Higher gas solubility

---

# 5. Henry's Law

## Statement

At constant temperature, solubility of a gas in liquid is directly proportional to partial pressure of gas.

---

Formula:

\[
p=K_Hx
\]

Where:

- p = Partial pressure of gas
- KH = Henry's constant
- x = Mole fraction of dissolved gas

---

# Applications

## 1. Carbonated Drinks

CO₂ is dissolved under high pressure.

---

## 2. Scuba Diving

Controls nitrogen dissolution in blood.

---

## 3. High Altitude Problems

Reduced oxygen solubility causes altitude sickness.

---

# 6. Vapour Pressure of Liquid Solutions

The pressure exerted by vapour molecules above a liquid surface is called vapour pressure.

---

# Raoult's Law

## Statement

The partial vapour pressure of any volatile component is directly proportional to its mole fraction in solution.

---

Formula:

\[
P_A=P_A^0X_A
\]

Where:

- PA = Partial vapour pressure
- PA° = Vapour pressure of pure component
- XA = Mole fraction

---

For binary solution:

\[
P_{total}=P_A+P_B
\]

---

# 7. Ideal Solutions

A solution that obeys Raoult's law at all concentrations.

---

# Characteristics

- ΔHmix = 0
- ΔVmix = 0
- Similar intermolecular forces

---

Examples:

- Benzene + Toluene

---

# 8. Non-Ideal Solutions

Solutions that do not obey Raoult's law.

---

Types:

```
Non-Ideal Solution

        │

 ┌──────┴──────┐

 ▼             ▼

Positive     Negative
Deviation    Deviation
```

---

# Positive Deviation

Vapour pressure is higher than expected.

Cause:

Weak attraction between molecules.

Example:

Ethanol + Acetone

---

# Negative Deviation

Vapour pressure is lower than expected.

Cause:

Strong intermolecular attraction.

Example:

Chloroform + Acetone

---

# 9. Azeotropes

## Definition

Mixtures that boil at constant temperature with same composition in liquid and vapour phases.

---

# Types

## 1. Minimum Boiling Azeotrope

Shows positive deviation.

Example:

Ethanol-water mixture

---

## 2. Maximum Boiling Azeotrope

Shows negative deviation.

Example:

Nitric acid-water mixture

---

# 10. Colligative Properties

## Definition

Properties that depend only on number of solute particles, not their nature.

---

# Four Colligative Properties

```
Colligative Properties

        │

 ┌──────┼──────┐

 ▼      ▼      ▼

Relative   Elevation   Depression

Vapour     of Boiling  of Freezing

Pressure   Point       Point

        │

        ▼

 Osmotic Pressure
```

---

# 1. Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure

Formula:

\[
\frac{P^0-P}{P^0}=X_B
\]

---

# 2. Elevation in Boiling Point

Formula:

\[
\Delta T_b=K_bm
\]

Where:

- Kb = Molal elevation constant
- m = Molality

---

# 3. Depression in Freezing Point

Formula:

\[
\Delta T_f=K_fm
\]

Where:

- Kf = Molal depression constant

---

# 4. Osmotic Pressure

## Definition

Pressure required to stop osmosis.

---

Formula:

\[
\pi=CRT
\]

Where:

- C = Molar concentration
- R = Gas constant
- T = Temperature

---

# 11. Osmosis

## Definition

Movement of solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane from dilute solution to concentrated solution.

---

# Types

## Reverse Osmosis

Pressure applied greater than osmotic pressure.

Applications:

- Water purification
- Desalination

---

# 12. Van't Hoff Factor (i)

## Definition

Correction factor used for abnormal colligative properties.

---

Formula:

\[
i=
\frac{Observed\ colligative\ property}
{Calculated\ colligative\ property}
\]

---

# For Dissociation

\[
i>1
\]

Example:

NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻

---

# For Association

\[
i<1
\]

Example:

Acetic acid dimer formation

---

# Modified Equations

## Elevation in boiling point:

\[
\Delta T_b=iK_bm
\]

---

## Depression in freezing point:

\[
\Delta T_f=iK_fm
\]

---

## Osmotic pressure:

\[
\pi=iCRT
\]

---

## 1. State Henry's law.

**Answer:**

At constant temperature, the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas.

\[
p=K_Hx
\]

## 2. Differentiate ideal and non-ideal solutions.

| Ideal Solution     | Non-Ideal Solution         |
| ------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Obeys Raoult's law | Deviates from Raoult's law |
| ΔHmix = 0          | ΔHmix ≠ 0                  |

## 3. What are colligative properties?

**Answer:**

Properties depending only on the number of solute particles present in solution are called colligative properties.

---

## 4. Why is molality preferred over molarity?

**Answer:**

Molality does not change with temperature because it depends on mass, while molarity depends on volume.

# Introduction

**Electrochemistry** is the branch of chemistry that deals with the relationship between:

- Chemical energy
- Electrical energy

It involves reactions where electrons are transferred.

---

# 1. Redox Reactions

Electrochemical reactions are based on oxidation and reduction.

---

# Oxidation

## Definition

Loss of electrons is called oxidation.

Example:

\[
Zn \rightarrow Zn^{2+}+2e^-
\]

Zinc loses electrons.

---

# Reduction

## Definition

Gain of electrons is called reduction.

Example:

\[
Cu^{2+}+2e^- \rightarrow Cu
\]

Copper ions gain electrons.

---

# 2. Electrochemical Cells

## Definition

A device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy or electrical energy into chemical energy.

---

# Types of Electrochemical Cells

```
             Electrochemical Cells

                    │

        ┌───────────┴───────────┐

        ▼                       ▼

  Galvanic Cell          Electrolytic Cell
```

---

# 3. Galvanic Cell

## Definition

A cell that converts chemical energy into electrical energy through a spontaneous redox reaction.

---

# Example:

## Daniell Cell

Reaction:

\[
Zn + Cu^{2+} \rightarrow Zn^{2+}+Cu
\]

---

# Structure of Daniell Cell

```
      Zn Electrode              Cu Electrode

          │                         │

       ZnSO₄                     CuSO₄

          │                         │

          └──── Salt Bridge ────────┘
```

---

# Components

## 1. Anode

Electrode where oxidation occurs.

In Daniell cell:

```
Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
```

Zinc electrode acts as anode.

---

## 2. Cathode

Electrode where reduction occurs.

Reaction:

```
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
```

Copper electrode acts as cathode.

---

# 4. Salt Bridge

## Definition

A U-shaped tube containing electrolyte that connects two half cells.

---

# Functions

### 1. Maintains electrical neutrality

### 2. Completes electrical circuit

### 3. Prevents direct mixing of solutions

---

# 5. Cell Notation

Representation of an electrochemical cell.

---

Daniell Cell:

\[
Zn|Zn^{2+}||Cu^{2+}|Cu
\]

---

Meaning:

```
Anode | Anode solution || Cathode solution | Cathode
```

---

# 6. Electrode Potential

## Definition

The tendency of an electrode to lose or gain electrons when placed in electrolyte.

---

# Standard Electrode Potential (E°)

Measured under standard conditions:

- Temperature = 298 K
- Pressure = 1 bar
- Concentration = 1 M

---

# Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

Reference electrode.

Its potential:

\[
E^\circ = 0V
\]

---

# 7. EMF of Cell

## Definition

The maximum potential difference between two electrodes of an electrochemical cell.

---

Formula:

\[
E_{cell}=E_{cathode}-E_{anode}
\]

---

# For spontaneous cell:

\[
E_{cell}>0
\]

---

# 8. Electrochemical Series

## Definition

Arrangement of electrodes according to their standard reduction potentials.

---

# Applications

Used to predict:

- Oxidising strength
- Reducing strength
- Feasibility of reactions

---

# 9. Gibbs Energy and Cell Potential

Relationship:

\[
\Delta G=-nFE_{cell}
\]

Where:

- n = Number of electrons
- F = Faraday constant
- Ecell = Cell potential

---

# Conditions

If:

\[
E_{cell}>0
\]

Then:

\[
\Delta G<0
\]

Reaction is spontaneous.

---

# 10. Nernst Equation

## Definition

Equation used to calculate electrode potential under non-standard conditions.

---

General equation:

\[
E=E^\circ-\frac{0.0591}{n}\log Q
\]

(at 298 K)

---

Where:

- E = Cell potential
- E° = Standard potential
- n = Number of electrons
- Q = Reaction quotient

---

# For Daniell Cell:

Reaction:

\[
Zn+Cu^{2+}\rightarrow Zn^{2+}+Cu
\]

Reaction quotient:

\[
Q=\frac{[Zn^{2+}]}{[Cu^{2+}]}
\]

---

# 11. Relation Between E° and Equilibrium Constant

At equilibrium:

\[
E_{cell}=0
\]

Equation:

\[
E^\circ_{cell}
=

\frac{0.0591}{n}\log K
\]

---

Where:

K = Equilibrium constant

---

# 12. Electrolytic Cells

## Definition

A cell where electrical energy is converted into chemical energy.

---

Example:

Electrolysis of molten NaCl.

---

# Electrolysis

## Definition

Chemical decomposition caused by passing electricity through an electrolyte.

---

# Components

```
Electrolytic Cell

        │

 ┌──────┴──────┐

 ▼             ▼

Cathode      Anode
Reduction    Oxidation
```

---

# 13. Conductance of Electrolytic Solutions

Resistance:

\[
R=\frac{V}{I}
\]

---

Conductance:

\[
G=\frac{1}{R}
\]

---

Unit:

```
Siemens (S)
```

---

# 14. Specific Conductivity (κ)

## Definition

Conductance of a solution placed between electrodes of unit length and unit area.

---

Formula:

\[
\kappa=G\frac{l}{A}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
S\,cm^{-1}
\]

---

# 15. Molar Conductivity (Λm)

## Definition

Conductance of all ions produced by one mole of electrolyte.

---

Formula:

\[
\Lambda_m=
\frac{\kappa \times1000}{M}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}
\]

---

# 16. Variation of Conductivity with Dilution

## Strong Electrolytes

Examples:

- HCl
- NaCl

Conductivity increases due to increased ion mobility.

---

## Weak Electrolytes

Examples:

- CH₃COOH

Conductivity increases greatly due to increased ionisation.

---

# 17. Kohlrausch's Law

## Statement

At infinite dilution, each ion contributes independently to total molar conductivity.

---

Formula:

\[
\Lambda_m^\circ=
\lambda^\circ_+ + \lambda^\circ_-
\]

---

# Applications

## 1. Calculate conductivity of weak electrolytes.

## 2. Determine degree of dissociation.

## 3. Calculate solubility of sparingly soluble salts.

---

# 18. Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

---

# First Law

## Statement

The amount of substance deposited is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed.

---

Formula:

\[
m \propto Q
\]

Since:

\[
Q=It
\]

Therefore:

\[
m=ZIt
\]

---

Where:

- m = Mass deposited
- I = Current
- t = Time
- Z = Electrochemical equivalent

---

# Second Law

## Statement

When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes, deposited masses are proportional to their equivalent masses.

---

Formula:

\[
\frac{m_1}{m_2}
=

\frac{E_1}{E_2}
\]

---

# 19. Faraday Constant

One Faraday:

\[
96500C
\]

is the charge required to deposit one gram equivalent of a substance.

---

# 20. Batteries

# Introduction

**Chemical kinetics** is the branch of chemistry that deals with:

- Rate of chemical reactions
- Factors affecting reaction rate
- Mechanism of reactions

---

# 1. Rate of Reaction

## Definition

The change in concentration of reactants or products per unit time is called the rate of reaction.

---

For a reaction:

\[
A \rightarrow B
\]

Rate:

\[
Rate=-\frac{\Delta[A]}{\Delta t}
\]

or

\[
Rate=\frac{\Delta[B]}{\Delta t}
\]

---

# 2. Average Rate of Reaction

## Definition

Rate measured over a particular time interval.

Formula:

\[
Average\ Rate=
-\frac{Change\ in\ concentration}{Time}
\]

---

Example:

If concentration decreases from 1M to 0.5M in 10 seconds:

\[
Rate=\frac{1-0.5}{10}
\]

\[
=0.05M/s
\]

---

# 3. Instantaneous Rate

## Definition

Rate of reaction at a specific moment of time.

---

It is obtained by:

```
Slope of concentration-time graph
```

---

# 4. Factors Affecting Rate of Reaction

```
          Rate of Reaction

                  │

 ┌────────────────┼────────────────┐

 ▼                ▼                ▼

Concentration Temperature Catalyst

                  │

                  ▼

            Surface Area
```

---

# 1. Effect of Concentration

According to collision theory:

Higher concentration:

→ More collisions

→ Faster reaction

---

# 2. Effect of Temperature

Increase in temperature:

- Increases kinetic energy
- Increases effective collisions

Therefore:

Reaction rate increases.

---

# 3. Effect of Catalyst

Catalyst increases reaction rate by lowering activation energy.

---

# 4. Effect of Surface Area

Powdered substances react faster than large pieces.

Reason:

Greater surface area provides more collision sites.

---

# 5. Rate Law

## Definition

The relationship between reaction rate and reactant concentration is called rate law.

---

For reaction:

\[
aA+bB\rightarrow Products
\]

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k[A]^m[B]^n
\]

---

Where:

- k = Rate constant
- m,n = Order with respect to reactants

---

# 6. Rate Constant (k)

## Definition

Rate constant is the proportionality constant in rate law.

---

It depends on:

- Temperature
- Nature of reaction
- Catalyst

---

# 7. Order of Reaction

## Definition

Sum of powers of concentration terms in rate law.

---

For:

\[
Rate=k[A]^m[B]^n
\]

Order:

\[
=m+n
\]

---

# Types of Reaction Order

```
Reaction Order

      │

 ┌────┼────┐

 ▼    ▼    ▼

Zero First Second
```

---

# 8. Zero Order Reaction

## Definition

Rate is independent of reactant concentration.

---

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k[A]^0
\]

Therefore:

\[
Rate=k
\]

---

# Integrated Rate Equation

\[
[A]_t=[A]_0-kt
\]

---

Where:

- [A]₀ = Initial concentration
- [A]t = Concentration after time t

---

# Half-Life of Zero Order Reaction

\[
t_{1/2}
=

\frac{[A]_0}{2k}
\]

---

# Graph

```
[A]

│\
│ \
│  \
│___\________

     Time
```

---

# 9. First Order Reaction

## Definition

Rate depends on concentration of one reactant.

---

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k[A]
\]

---

# Integrated Rate Equation

\[
k=
\frac{2.303}{t}
\log
\frac{[A]_0}{[A]_t}
\]

---

# Half-Life

\[
t_{1/2}
=

\frac{0.693}{k}
\]

---

# Important Point

Half-life is independent of initial concentration.

---

# Graph

```
[A]

│\
│ \
│  \
│   \_____

      Time
```

---

# 10. Second Order Reaction

## Definition

Rate depends on square of concentration or two reactants.

---

Rate law:

\[
Rate=k[A]^2
\]

---

# Integrated Rate Equation

\[
\frac{1}{[A]_t}
=

\frac{1}{[A]_0}+kt
\]

---

# Half-Life

\[
t_{1/2}
=

\frac{1}{k[A]_0}
\]

# Exam Important Questions

## 1. Why are haloarenes less reactive than haloalkanes?

**Answer:**

Because the C-X bond in haloarenes has partial double bond character due to resonance, making it stronger.

---

## 2. Explain SN1 reaction.

**Answer:**

SN1 reaction occurs in two steps involving carbocation formation followed by nucleophile attack. Its rate depends only on haloalkane concentration.

---

## 3. Explain SN2 reaction.

**Answer:**

SN2 reaction is a one-step bimolecular substitution involving backside attack of nucleophile with inversion of configuration.

---

## 4. State Wurtz reaction.

**Answer:**

Alkyl halides react with sodium metal in dry ether to form higher alkanes.

# 10. Structure of Proteins

Protein structure exists at four levels.

```

        │

 ┌──────┼──────┐

 ▼      ▼      ▼

              │

              ▼

          Quaternary
```

# 1. Primary Structure

## Definition

Sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain.

---

Example:

\[
Ala-Gly-Val-Leu
\]

---

# 2. Secondary Structure

Arrangement of polypeptide chain due to hydrogen bonding.

Types:

## α-Helix

Spiral structure.

---

## β-Pleated Sheet

Folded sheet structure.

---

# 3. Tertiary Structure

Three-dimensional folding of a protein chain.

Maintained by:

- Hydrogen bonds
- Disulfide bonds
- Ionic interactions

---

# 4. Quaternary Structure

Arrangement of multiple polypeptide chains.

Example:

Haemoglobin

---

# 11. Denaturation of Proteins

## Definition

Loss of biological activity due to destruction of secondary and tertiary structures is called denaturation.

Causes:

- Heat
- pH change
- Chemicals

---

Example:

Boiling of egg white.

# Exam Important Questions

## 1. What are amino acids?

**Answer:**

Amino acids are organic compounds containing amino (-NH₂) and carboxyl (-COOH) groups that act as building blocks of proteins.

---

## 2. Explain peptide bond.

**Answer:**

The bond formed between two amino acids by elimination of water is called a peptide bond.

\[
-CO-NH-
\]

---

## 3. What is protein denaturation?

**Answer:**

Loss of biological activity due to destruction of secondary and tertiary structures of proteins is called denaturation.

---

## 4. Differentiate DNA and RNA.

| DNA                        | RNA                     |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Double helix               | Single strand           |
| Thymine present            | Uracil present          |
| Stores genetic information | Helps protein synthesis |

---

## 5. What is a nucleotide?

**Answer:**

A nucleotide is the basic unit of nucleic acids consisting of sugar, nitrogen base, and phosphate group.
