# Introduction

**Electrostatics** is the branch of physics that deals with:

- Electric charges at rest
- Electric fields
- Electric potential
- Capacitance

---

# 1. Electric Charge

## Definition

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical interactions.

---

# Types of Charges

```
Electric Charge

      │

 ┌────┴────┐

 ▼         ▼

Positive  Negative
Charge    Charge
```

---

# Properties of Electric Charge

## 1. Additivity of Charge

Total charge:

\[
q=q_1+q_2+q_3+...
\]

---

## 2. Conservation of Charge

Charge can neither be created nor destroyed.

It can only be transferred.

---

## 3. Quantisation of Charge

Charge exists in discrete packets.

Formula:

\[
q=ne
\]

Where:

- n = Integer
- e = Charge of electron

\[
e=1.6\times10^{-19}C
\]

---

# 2. Coulomb's Law

## Statement

The force of attraction or repulsion between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of distance between them.

---

Formula:

\[
F=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}
\]

---

Where:

- F = Electrostatic force
- q₁, q₂ = Charges
- r = Distance between charges
- ε₀ = Permittivity of free space

---

# In Vacuum:

\[
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}=9\times10^9 Nm^2/C^2
\]

---

# Nature of Force

Like charges:

```
Repulsion
```

Unlike charges:

```
Attraction
```

---

# Coulomb's Law in Vector Form

\[
\vec{F}
=

\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}
\hat{r}
\]

---

# 3. Principle of Superposition

## Statement

The net force on a charge due to several charges is the vector sum of individual forces.

---

Formula:

\[
\vec{F}
=

\vec{F_1} +
\vec{F_2} +
\vec{F_3}
+...
\]

---

# 4. Electric Field

## Definition

The region around a charged particle where another charge experiences force is called electric field.

---

Electric field intensity:

\[
E=\frac{F}{q_0}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
NC^{-1}
\]

or

\[
Vm^{-1}
\]

---

# Electric Field Due to Point Charge

\[
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q}{r^2}
\]

---

Direction:

Positive charge:

```
Away from charge
```

Negative charge:

```
Towards charge
```

---

# 5. Electric Field Lines

## Properties

1. Field lines start from positive charge.
2. End at negative charge.
3. Never intersect.
4. Density represents field strength.

---

Diagram:

```
      +q

    ↗ ↑ ↖

  ←     →

    ↘ ↓ ↙
```

---

# 6. Electric Dipole

## Definition

A pair of equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance.

---

Representation:

\[
(+q)\quad\quad(-q)
\]

Distance:

\[
2a
\]

---

# Dipole Moment

Formula:

\[
p=q(2a)
\]

---

Direction:

From negative charge to positive charge.

---

Unit:

\[
Cm
\]

---

# Electric Field Due to Dipole

## On Axial Position

\[
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{2p}{r^3}
\]

---

## On Equatorial Position

\[
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{p}{r^3}
\]

Direction opposite to dipole moment.

---

# 7. Electric Flux

## Definition

Measure of electric field passing through a surface.

---

Formula:

\[
\phi=E.A
\]

For angle θ:

\[
\phi=EA\cos\theta
\]

---

Unit:

\[
Nm^2/C
\]

---

# 8. Gauss's Law

## Statement

The total electric flux through a closed surface is equal to the charge enclosed divided by permittivity.

---

Formula:

\[
\phi=\frac{q}{\epsilon_0}
\]

---

Mathematical form:

\[
\oint E.dA
=

\frac{q}{\epsilon_0}
\]

---

# Applications of Gauss's Law

---

# 1. Electric Field Due to Infinite Line Charge

Linear charge density:

\[
\lambda=\frac{q}{L}
\]

Electric field:

\[
E=
\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0r}
\]

---

# 2. Electric Field Due to Infinite Plane Sheet

\[
E=
\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}
\]

Where:

\[
\sigma=\frac{q}{A}
\]

---

# 3. Electric Field Inside a Charged Sphere

Inside conductor:

\[
E=0
\]

---

Outside:

\[
E=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{Q}{r^2}
\]

---

# 9. Electric Potential

## Definition

Work done per unit positive charge in bringing a test charge from infinity to a point.

---

Formula:

\[
V=\frac{W}{q}
\]

---

Unit:

Volt (V)

---

# Potential Due to Point Charge

\[
V=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q}{r}
\]

---

# Relation Between Electric Field and Potential

\[
E=-\frac{dV}{dr}
\]

---

Meaning:

Electric field acts from higher potential to lower potential.

---

# 10. Potential Energy

## Definition

Energy possessed by charges due to their positions.

---

Two Charge System:

\[
U=
\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q_1q_2}{r}
\]

---

# Electric Potential Energy of Dipole

\[
U=-pE\cos\theta
\]

---

# 11. Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium

Properties:

---

## 1. Electric Field Inside Conductor

\[
E=0
\]

---

## 2. Charge Distribution

Charge resides on outer surface.

---

## 3. Potential

Potential remains constant throughout conductor.

---

# 12. Capacitance

## Definition

Ability of a conductor to store electric charge.

---

Formula:

\[
C=\frac{Q}{V}
\]

---

Unit:

Farad (F)

---

# 13. Parallel Plate Capacitor

Capacitance:

\[
C=
\frac{\epsilon_0A}{d}
\]

Where:

- A = Area of plates
- d = Distance between plates

---

# With Dielectric

\[
C=
\frac{K\epsilon_0A}{d}
\]

Where:

K = Dielectric constant

---

# 14. Combination of Capacitors

---

# Parallel Combination

Same potential difference.

Total capacitance:

\[
C=C_1+C_2+C_3
\]

---

# Series Combination

Same charge.

Formula:

\[
\frac{1}{C}
=

\frac1{C_1} +
\frac1{C_2} +
\frac1{C_3}
\]

---

# 15. Energy Stored in Capacitor

Formula:

\[
U=\frac12CV^2
\]

---

Other forms:

\[
U=\frac{Q^2}{2C}
\]

\[
U=\frac12QV
\]

---

# Energy Density

\[
u=\frac12\epsilon_0E^2
\]

---

# 16. Dielectrics

## Definition

Insulating materials placed between capacitor plates.

Examples:

- Glass
- Mica
- Plastic

---

# Polarisation

Alignment of electric dipoles inside dielectric.

---

# Effect of Dielectric

Capacitance increases:

\[
C'=KC
\]

# Board Important Questions

## 1. State Coulomb's law.

**Answer:**

The electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to square of distance between them.

---

## 2. State Gauss's law.

**Answer:**

The total electric flux through a closed surface is equal to charge enclosed divided by permittivity of free space.

---

**Answer:**

If electric field existed, free electrons would move, violating electrostatic equilibrium.

**Answer:**

Capacitance is the ratio of charge stored to potential difference.

\[
C=\frac{Q}{V}
\]

# Introduction

**Current Electricity** deals with the flow of electric charges through conductors.

When free electrons move through a conductor due to an applied electric field, electric current is produced.

---

# 1. Electric Current

## Definition

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a cross-sectional area of a conductor.

---

Formula:

\[
I=\frac{Q}{t}
\]

Where:

- I = Current
- Q = Charge
- t = Time

---

# Unit

\[
Ampere(A)
\]

---

# Direction of Current

By convention:

```
Positive charge flow direction
```

Actual electron flow:

```
Opposite to current direction
```

---

# 2. Types of Current

## 1. Direct Current (DC)

Current flows in one direction.

Example:

Battery current

---

## 2. Alternating Current (AC)

Current changes direction periodically.

Example:

Household electricity

---

# 3. Current Density

## Definition

Current flowing per unit cross-sectional area of conductor.

Formula:

\[
J=\frac{I}{A}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
Am^{-2}
\]

---

# 4. Drift Velocity

## Definition

The average velocity acquired by free electrons in a conductor due to an applied electric field is called drift velocity.

Symbol:

\[
v_d
\]

---

Without electric field:

```
Random motion of electrons
```

With electric field:

```
Net drift motion
```

---

# Relation Between Current and Drift Velocity

\[
I=nAe v_d
\]

Where:

- n = Number of free electrons per unit volume
- A = Area of conductor
- e = Charge of electron
- vd = Drift velocity

---

# 5. Mobility of Electron

## Definition

Mobility is the drift velocity acquired per unit electric field.

Formula:

\[
\mu=\frac{v_d}{E}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
m^2V^{-1}s^{-1}
\]

---

# 6. Ohm's Law

## Statement

At constant temperature, the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it.

---

Formula:

\[
V\propto I
\]

Therefore:

\[
V=IR
\]

---

Where:

- V = Potential difference
- I = Current
- R = Resistance

---

# V-I Graph

For ohmic conductor:

```
V

│
│       /
│      /
│     /
│____/________ I
```

Slope:

\[
R=\frac{V}{I}
\]

---

# 7. Resistance

## Definition

Resistance is the opposition offered by a conductor to the flow of current.

---

Formula:

\[
R=\rho\frac{l}{A}
\]

---

Where:

- ρ = Resistivity
- l = Length
- A = Area

---

# Unit

\[
Ohm(\Omega)
\]

---

# 8. Resistivity

## Definition

Resistance of a material having unit length and unit area.

---

Formula:

\[
\rho=\frac{RA}{l}
\]

---

Unit:

\[
\Omega m
\]

---

# Factors Affecting Resistance

Resistance depends on:

1. Length

\[
R\propto l
\]

2. Area

\[
R\propto\frac1A
\]

3. Material

4. Temperature

---

# 9. Combination of Resistors

---

# Series Combination

Same current flows.

```
R₁ ─ R₂ ─ R₃
```

Equivalent resistance:

\[
R=R_1+R_2+R_3
\]

---

# Parallel Combination

Same voltage across resistors.

```
 ┌─R₁─┐
 ├─R₂─┤
 └─R₃─┘
```

Equivalent resistance:

\[
\frac1R=
\frac1{R_1}
+\frac1{R_2}
+\frac1{R_3}
\]

---

# 10. Temperature Dependence of Resistance

Resistance changes with temperature.

Formula:

\[
R_t=R_0(1+\alpha\Delta T)
\]

---

Where:

- R₀ = Resistance at initial temperature
- Rt = Resistance at final temperature
- α = Temperature coefficient

---

# Metals

Resistance increases with temperature.

\[
\alpha>0
\]

---

# Semiconductors

Resistance decreases with temperature.

\[
\alpha<0
\]

---

# 11. Electrical Energy and Power

---

# Electrical Energy

\[
W=VIt
\]

Using Ohm's law:

\[
W=I^2Rt
\]

\[
W=\frac{V^2t}{R}
\]

---

# Electrical Power

## Definition

Rate of consumption of electrical energy.

Formula:

\[
P=\frac{W}{t}
\]

---

Therefore:

\[
P=VI
\]

Also:

\[
P=I^2R
\]

\[
P=\frac{V^2}{R}
\]

---

# Unit

\[
Watt(W)
\]

---

# 12. Cells and EMF

## EMF

The energy supplied by a cell per unit charge is called electromotive force.

Formula:

\[
E=\frac{W}{Q}
\]

---

# Internal Resistance

Every cell has resistance inside it called internal resistance.

---

Terminal Voltage:

\[
V=E-Ir
\]

Where:

- E = EMF
- r = Internal resistance

---

# 13. Combination of Cells

---

# Cells in Series

Equivalent EMF:

\[
E=nE
\]

Equivalent resistance:

\[
r=nr
\]

---

# Cells in Parallel

For identical cells:

\[
E_{eq}=E
\]

\[
r_{eq}=\frac rn
\]

---

# 14. Kirchhoff's Laws

Used to solve complex electrical circuits.

---

# Kirchhoff's First Law

## Junction Rule

## Statement

The algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero.

---

Formula:

\[
\sum I=0
\]

Based on:

```
Conservation of charge
```

---

# Kirchhoff's Second Law

## Loop Rule

## Statement

The algebraic sum of potential differences around a closed loop is zero.

---

Formula:

\[
\sum V=0
\]

Based on:

```
Conservation of energy
```

---

# Sign Convention

Moving:

Positive to negative:

\[
-V
\]

Negative to positive:

\[
+V
\]

---

# 15. Wheatstone Bridge

## Definition

A network used to measure unknown resistance.

---

Circuit:

```
       R₁       R₂

        |       |

        G

        |       |

       R₃       R₄
```

---

# Balance Condition

When galvanometer shows zero current:

\[
\frac{R_1}{R_2}
=

\frac{R_3}{R_4}
\]

---

# Applications

- Measuring unknown resistance
- Sensor circuits

---

# 16. Metre Bridge

## Definition

A practical form of Wheatstone bridge using a 1 metre resistance wire.

---

Balance condition:

\[
\frac{R}{S}
=

\frac{l}{100-l}
\]

---

Where:

- R = Unknown resistance
- S = Known resistance
- l = Balancing length

---

# Uses

- Find unknown resistance
- Compare resistances

---

# 17. Potentiometer

## Definition

A device used to measure potential difference without drawing current.

---

# Principle

Potential drop across a uniform wire is directly proportional to length.

\[
V\propto l
\]

---

# Potential Gradient

\[
k=\frac Vl
\]

---

# 18. Measurement of EMF Using Potentiometer

At balance point:

\[
E=kl
\]

---

Comparison of EMF:

\[
\frac{E_1}{E_2}
=

\frac{l_1}{l_2}
\]

---

# 19. Finding Internal Resistance of Cell

Formula:

\[
r=
R
\frac{l_1-l_2}{l_2}
\]

Where:

- R = External resistance
- l₁ = Open circuit length
- l₂ = Closed circuit length

# Introduction

When electric charges move, they produce a magnetic field.

**Magnetic effects of current** deals with:

- Magnetic field produced by current
- Force on moving charges
- Motion of charged particles in magnetic fields
- Electrical measuring instruments

---

# 1. Magnetic Field

## Definition

The region around a magnet or current carrying conductor where magnetic force can be experienced is called a magnetic field.

---

# Magnetic Field Vector

Symbol:

\[
\vec{B}
\]

Unit:

\[
Tesla(T)
\]

---

# 2. Magnetic Field Lines

## Properties

1. Magnetic field lines form closed loops.
2. Outside a magnet they move from North to South.
3. Inside a magnet they move from South to North.
4. Two magnetic field lines never intersect.
5. Density of lines represents field strength.

---

# 3. Oersted Experiment

## Observation

A current carrying wire deflects a nearby compass needle.

---

## Conclusion

Electric current produces a magnetic field.

---

# 4. Biot-Savart Law

## Statement

The magnetic field due to a small current element is directly proportional to:

- Current
- Length element
- Sine of angle

and inversely proportional to square of distance.

---

# Formula

\[
dB=
\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}
\frac{Idl\sin\theta}{r^2}
\]

---

Where:

- dB = Small magnetic field
- I = Current
- dl = Current element
- r = Distance
- μ₀ = Permeability of free space

---

# Direction

Given by:

```
Right Hand Thumb Rule
```

---

# 5. Magnetic Field Due to Straight Current Carrying Conductor

For a long straight wire:

\[
B=
\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}
\]

---

Where:

- I = Current
- r = Distance from wire

---

# Variation

\[
B\propto\frac1r
\]

Magnetic field decreases with distance.

---

# 6. Magnetic Field Due to Circular Current Loop

At centre:

\[
B=
\frac{\mu_0 NI}{2R}
\]

---

Where:

- N = Number of turns
- R = Radius of coil

---

# Direction

Using:

```
Right Hand Rule
```

---

# Magnetic Field on Axis of Circular Loop

\[
B=
\frac{\mu_0NIR^2}
{2(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}
\]

---

# 7. Ampere's Circuital Law

## Statement

The line integral of magnetic field around a closed path is equal to μ₀ times the current enclosed.

---

Formula:

\[
\oint B.dl=\mu_0I
\]

---

# Applications

Used to find magnetic fields in:

- Solenoid
- Toroid
- Infinite wire

---

# 8. Magnetic Field Due to Solenoid

## Definition

A long coil consisting of many turns of wire is called a solenoid.

---

Magnetic field inside solenoid:

\[
B=\mu_0 nI
\]

---

Where:

- n = Number of turns per unit length
- I = Current

---

# Properties

Inside solenoid:

```
Uniform magnetic field
```

Outside:

```
Almost zero
```

---

# 9. Toroid

## Definition

A solenoid bent into circular form is called a toroid.

---

Magnetic field:

\[
B=
\frac{\mu_0NI}{2\pi r}
\]

---

# 10. Force on a Moving Charge in Magnetic Field

A moving charge experiences magnetic force.

---

# Lorentz Force

\[
F=q(\vec{v}\times\vec{B})
\]

---

Magnitude:

\[
F=qvB\sin\theta
\]

---

Cases:

## θ = 0°

\[
F=0
\]

Charge moves parallel to field.

---

## θ = 90°

Maximum force.

\[
F=qvB
\]

---

# 11. Motion of Charged Particle in Magnetic Field

---

# Case 1: Velocity Perpendicular to Magnetic Field

Particle moves in circular path.

---

Magnetic force provides centripetal force:

\[
qvB=\frac{mv^2}{r}
\]

---

Radius:

\[
r=\frac{mv}{qB}
\]

# 1. Magnetic Flux

## Definition

The amount of magnetic field passing through a surface is called magnetic flux.

# 2. Faraday's Laws of Electromagnetic Induction

---

# First Law

## Statement

Whenever the magnetic flux linked with a circuit changes, an induced emf is produced in the circuit.

---

# Second Law

## Statement

The magnitude of induced emf is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux.

---

Formula:

\[
e\propto\frac{d\Phi}{dt}
\]

## Statement

The induced current always flows in such a direction that it opposes the cause producing it.

Mathematical representation:

\[
e=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt}
\]

The negative sign represents Lenz's law.

# Introduction

**Electromagnetic waves** are waves consisting of oscillating electric and magnetic fields that can travel through vacuum.

They were theoretically predicted by:

```
James Clerk Maxwell
```

and experimentally verified by:

```
Heinrich Hertz
```

---

# 1. Maxwell's Theory of Electromagnetic Waves

Maxwell proposed that:

1. A changing electric field produces a magnetic field.
2. A changing magnetic field produces an electric field.
3. These fields travel together as electromagnetic waves.

---

# 2. Production of Electromagnetic Waves

Electromagnetic waves are produced by:

```
Accelerated charges
```

Example:

- Oscillating electrons in antennas

---

# 3. Nature of Electromagnetic Waves

Electromagnetic waves are:

- Transverse waves
- Non-mechanical waves
- Do not require a medium

They can travel through:

```
Vacuum
```

---

# 4. Structure of Electromagnetic Wave

An electromagnetic wave consists of:

```
Electric Field (E)

        ↑

        │

        │

        ─────────→ Direction of propagation

        │

        ↓

Magnetic Field (B)
```

---

# Properties:

Electric field and magnetic field are:

- Mutually perpendicular
- Perpendicular to direction of propagation

Therefore:

\[
E \perp B \perp Direction
\]

\[

\]

- μ₀ = Permeability of free space

- Interference
- Diffraction
- Polarisation

These phenomena prove the wave nature of light.

Light also behaves as particles called:

```

```

# 3. Photon

## Definition

A photon is a packet of electromagnetic energy.

---

Energy of photon:

\[
E=h\nu
\]

Where:

- h = Planck's constant
- ν = Frequency

---

Using:

\[
\nu=\frac c\lambda
\]

Energy becomes:

\[
E=\frac{hc}{\lambda}
\]

---

# Properties of Photon

1. Zero rest mass.
2. Travels with speed of light.
3. Has energy and momentum.
4. Cannot exist at rest.

---

# 4. Photoelectric Effect

## Definition

The phenomenon of emission of electrons from a metal surface when electromagnetic radiation of suitable frequency falls on it is called photoelectric effect.

---

Emitted electrons:

```
Photoelectrons
```

---

# 6. Important Terms in Photoelectric Effect

---

# 1. Photoelectron

Electrons emitted from metal surface due to light.

---

# 2. Photoelectric Current

Current produced due to flow of photoelectrons.

---

# 3. Threshold Frequency

## Definition

Minimum frequency of incident radiation required to emit electrons from a metal surface.

Symbol:

\[
\nu_0
\]

---

# 4. Work Function

## Definition

Minimum energy required to remove an electron from a metal surface.

---

Formula:

\[
\phi=h\nu_0
\]

---

Unit:

\[
eV
\]

---

# 5. Stopping Potential

## Definition

Minimum negative potential applied to stop the most energetic photoelectrons.

Symbol:

\[
V_0
\]

---

# 7. Laws of Photoelectric Effect

---

## Law 1

For a given metal, photoelectric emission occurs only if frequency is greater than threshold frequency.

\[
\nu>\nu_0
\]

---

## Law 2

Photoelectric current is directly proportional to intensity of incident light.

---

## Law 3

Maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons depends on frequency, not intensity.

---

## Law 4

Photoelectric emission is instantaneous.

---

# 8. Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

Einstein explained photoelectric effect using photon theory.

---

Energy conservation:

\[
Photon\ Energy=
Work\ Function+
Maximum\ Kinetic\ Energy
\]

---

Formula:

\[
h\nu=\phi+K_{max}
\]

---

Since:

\[
K_{max}=\frac12mv^2
\]

Therefore:

\[
h\nu=\phi+\frac12mv^2
\]

---

# In Terms of Stopping Potential

\[
K_{max}=eV_0
\]

Therefore:

\[
h\nu=\phi+eV_0
\]

---

# 11. Matter Waves

## de Broglie Hypothesis

According to de Broglie:

Every moving particle has an associated wave.

---

Matter has:

```
Particle nature + Wave nature
```

# 12. de Broglie Wavelength

Formula:

\[
\lambda=\frac hp
\]

---

Since:

\[
p=mv
\]

Therefore:

\[
\lambda=\frac h{mv}
\]

---

Where:

- λ = Wavelength
- h = Planck constant
- m = Mass
- v = Velocity

---

# 13. de Broglie Wavelength for Electron

For electron accelerated through potential V:

\[
\lambda=
\frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}
\]

---

Approximate formula:

\[
\lambda=
\frac{12.27}{\sqrt V}
\mathring{A}
\]

Electrons show wave nature

# 2. Thomson's Atomic Model

Known as:

Plum Pudding Model
```

---

# Limitation

Could not explain:

- Rutherford scattering results
- Stability of atom

# 3. Rutherford's Atomic Model

Based on:

```
Alpha particle scattering experiment
```

---

# Observations

1. Most particles passed straight through.
2. Few particles were deflected.
3. Very few bounced back.

---

# Conclusions

1. Atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus.
2. Most of the atom is empty space.
3. Electrons revolve around nucleus.

---

# Limitations

According to classical theory:

Moving electrons should radiate energy and collapse into nucleus.

Could not explain:

- Stability of atom
- Line spectra

---

# 4. Bohr's Atomic Model

Bohr proposed:

Electrons revolve around nucleus only in certain allowed orbits.

---

---

Electrons revolve without radiating energy.

---

## 2. Quantisation of Angular Momentum

\[

# 6. Radioactive Decay Law

## Statement

The rate of radioactive decay is directly proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present.

---

Formula:

\[
-\frac{dN}{dt}\propto N
\]

---

Therefore:

\[
N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}
\]

---

Where:

- N = Number of nuclei after time t
- N₀ = Initial number of nuclei
- λ = Decay constant

---

---

## Definition

The boundary formed when p-type and n-type semiconductors are joined is called p-n junction.

# Formation of Depletion Region

When p and n regions join:

- Electrons diffuse from n-side to p-side.
- Holes diffuse from p-side to n-side.

A region without free charge carriers is formed.

Called:

```

A Zener diode is a diode designed to operate in reverse breakdown region and maintain constant voltage.

A transistor amplifies signals by controlling collector current using a small base current.
