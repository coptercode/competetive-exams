# Introduction

Biology is the branch of science that deals with the study of **living organisms** and their interactions with one another and the environment. The Earth is home to millions of different organisms that vary in size, shape, habitat, structure, and function.

To study this enormous diversity systematically, scientists classify organisms into groups based on their similarities, differences, and evolutionary relationships. This chapter introduces the **characteristics of living organisms**, **taxonomy**, **systematics**, **binomial nomenclature**, **taxonomic hierarchy**, and **taxonomical aids**.

---

# 1. What is Life?

## Definition

**Life** is the characteristic that distinguishes living organisms from non-living matter by the presence of biological processes such as metabolism, growth, reproduction, and response to stimuli.

---

# 2. Characteristics of Living Organisms

## A. Growth

Growth is a permanent increase in the size, volume, or mass of an organism.

### In Plants

- Growth occurs throughout life.
- Due to **meristematic tissues**.

### In Animals

- Growth occurs only up to a certain age.

---

## B. Reproduction

Reproduction is the process by which organisms produce offspring of their own kind.

### Types

#### Asexual Reproduction

One parent produces offspring.

Examples

- Amoeba → Binary fission
- Hydra → Budding
- Yeast → Budding

---

#### Sexual Reproduction

Two parents produce genetically different offspring.

Examples

- Humans
- Flowering plants
- Birds

---

## C. Metabolism

### Definition

Metabolism is the sum total of all biochemical reactions occurring inside living cells.

It consists of

```
Metabolism
      │
 ┌────┴────┐
 ▼         ▼
Anabolism  Catabolism
```

---

### Anabolism

- Building reactions
- Energy is consumed

Examples

- Photosynthesis
- Protein synthesis

---

### Catabolism

- Breakdown reactions
- Energy is released

Examples

- Respiration
- Digestion

---

### Importance

Metabolism is considered the **most important defining characteristic of life**.

---

# 10. Binomial Nomenclature

Developed by

**Carolus Linnaeus**

---

## Rules

1. Two words are used.
2. First word is the **Genus**.
3. Second word is the **Species**.
4. Genus begins with a capital letter.
5. Species begins with a small letter.
6. Printed in _italics_.
7. If handwritten, both words are underlined separately.
8. Author's name is written after the scientific name.

---

## Examples

| Common Name | Scientific Name    |
| ----------- | ------------------ |
| Human       | _Homo sapiens_     |
| Mango       | _Mangifera indica_ |
| Tiger       | _Panthera tigris_  |
| Lion        | _Panthera leo_     |
| Rice        | _Oryza sativa_     |

# Chapter Summary

- The **Living World** introduces the fundamental characteristics that distinguish living organisms from non-living matter, emphasizing **metabolism**, **cellular organization**, and **response to stimuli** as defining features.
- **Taxonomy** provides a systematic method for identifying, naming, and classifying organisms, while **systematics** extends this by incorporating evolutionary relationships and phylogeny.
- The **binomial nomenclature** system developed by **Carolus Linnaeus** ensures that every organism has a unique and universally accepted scientific name.
- Organisms are arranged in a **taxonomic hierarchy** from **Kingdom** down to **Species**, enabling easy identification and comparison.
- **Taxonomical aids** such as **herbaria, botanical gardens, museums, zoological parks, and identification keys** are essential for the study, preservation, and classification of biodiversity.

# Introduction

The enormous diversity of living organisms makes their study difficult without proper classification. **Biological Classification** is the scientific arrangement of organisms into groups based on similarities and differences in their structure, nutrition, reproduction, and evolutionary relationships.

One of the most widely accepted systems is **R.H. Whittaker's Five-Kingdom Classification (1969)**, which classifies all living organisms into **Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia**.

This chapter also introduces **Viruses, Viroids, Prions, and Lichens**, which do not fit perfectly into the five-kingdom system.

---

# 1. Need for Biological Classification

Classification helps to

- Identify organisms correctly.
- Study biodiversity systematically.
- Understand evolutionary relationships.
- Avoid confusion due to local names.
- Predict characteristics of unknown organisms.

---

# 2. Basis of Classification

Whittaker classified organisms using the following criteria:

- Cell structure
- Cell organization
- Mode of nutrition
- Reproduction
- Evolutionary relationships (Phylogeny)

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Biological Classification** organizes the vast diversity of living organisms into meaningful groups based on cellular organization, nutrition, reproduction, and evolutionary relationships.
- **R. H. Whittaker's Five-Kingdom Classification** divides organisms into **Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia**, providing a systematic framework for studying life.
- **Kingdom Monera** includes prokaryotes such as archaebacteria, eubacteria, cyanobacteria, and mycoplasma, while **Protista** comprises diverse unicellular eukaryotes like diatoms, dinoflagellates, euglenoids, slime moulds, and protozoans.
- **Fungi** are heterotrophic organisms with chitinous cell walls and reproduce through various spores, whereas **Plantae** and **Animalia** include multicellular autotrophic and heterotrophic organisms respectively.
- **Viruses, viroids, prions, and lichens** represent unique biological entities that highlight the diversity and complexity of life beyond the traditional kingdoms.
- Understanding biological classification is fundamental for studying **biodiversity, evolution, ecology, medicine, agriculture, biotechnology, and conservation biology**.

# Introduction

The **Plant Kingdom (Kingdom Plantae)** comprises a vast group of multicellular, eukaryotic, autotrophic organisms capable of performing **photosynthesis** due to the presence of chlorophyll. Plants play a vital role in maintaining ecological balance by producing oxygen, fixing carbon dioxide, and serving as the primary producers in food chains.

Based on the **degree of differentiation, vascular tissue, seed formation, and reproductive structures**, the plant kingdom is classified into:

- Algae
- Bryophytes
- Pteridophytes
- Gymnosperms
- Angiosperms

---

# 1. Characteristics of Kingdom Plantae

- Multicellular and eukaryotic.
- Cell wall made of cellulose.
- Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll.
- Autotrophic mode of nutrition.

# Chapter Summary

- The **Plant Kingdom** consists of multicellular, eukaryotic, autotrophic organisms that carry out photosynthesis and exhibit **alternation of generations**.
- Plants are classified into **Algae, Bryophytes, Pteridophytes, Gymnosperms, and Angiosperms** based on body organization, vascular tissues, seed formation, and reproductive structures.
- **Algae** are simple thalloid plants classified into **green, brown, and red algae** based on pigments, storage products, and cell wall composition.

# Introduction

The **Kingdom Animalia** consists of multicellular, eukaryotic, heterotrophic organisms that lack cell walls. Animals exhibit a high degree of organization, specialized tissues, locomotion, and complex organ systems. They occupy diverse habitats ranging from deep oceans to deserts and play essential roles in ecosystems.

- Body symmetry

# Introduction

**Plant Anatomy** is the branch of botany that deals with the **internal structure and organization of plant tissues and organs**. It explains how different tissues are arranged within roots, stems, and leaves and how these tissues function together.

# 2. Meristematic Tissue

## Definition

# Introduction

The **cell** is the **basic structural, functional, and biological unit of life**. Every living organism is composed of one or more cells, and all life processes such as growth, metabolism, reproduction, and inheritance occur within cells.

The discovery of the cell revolutionized biology and led to the formulation of the **Cell Theory**, which states that all living organisms are composed of cells and that cells arise only from pre-existing cells.

This chapter explains the structure and functions of **prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells**, the organization of cell organelles, the endomembrane system, the cytoskeleton, and the nucleus.

| Robert Brown            | Discovered nucleus                                              |
| Schleiden               | Plants are made of cells                                        |

# Introduction

The **cell cycle** is the sequence of events through which a cell grows, duplicates its genetic material, and divides to produce new daughter cells. Cell division is essential for **growth, repair, reproduction, development, and maintenance** of living organisms.

There are two major types of cell division:

- **Mitosis** (Equational Division)
- **Meiosis** (Reduction Division)

Proper regulation of the cell cycle ensures genetic stability and normal functioning of organisms.

---

# 1. Cell Cycle

## Definition

The **cell cycle** is the ordered sequence of events that occurs from the formation of a cell until it divides into two daughter cells.

---

## Importance

- Growth of organisms
- Replacement of worn-out cells
- Tissue repair
- Asexual reproduction
- Formation of gametes (through meiosis)
- Maintenance of chromosome number

---

# Phases of Cell Cycle

```
              CELL CYCLE

      ┌────────────┴────────────┐

                                │

                         Cell Division
```

# 2. Interphase

## Definition

Interphase is the longest phase of the cell cycle during which the cell grows and prepares for division.

It occupies about **90–95%** of the total cell cycle.

## B. S Phase (Synthesis Phase)

### Characteristics

- DNA replication occurs.
- Chromosomes duplicate.
- Histone proteins are synthesized.
- DNA content doubles.

---

## C. G₂ Phase (Gap 2)

### Characteristics

- Final growth phase.
- Protein synthesis continues.
- Cell prepares for mitosis.
- Spindle proteins are produced.

# Introduction

Plants require a continuous supply of **water, minerals, and organic food** for growth, metabolism, and survival. The transport system in plants consists of specialized tissues—**xylem** and **phloem**—which transport water, minerals, and food to different parts of the plant.

Transport in plants mainly involves:

- Diffusion
- Facilitated Diffusion
- Active Transport
- Osmosis
- Water Absorption
- Ascent of Sap
- Transpiration
- Translocation of Food

---

Plants transport substances through both passive and active mechanisms.

```

               │

    ▼          ▼          ▼

```

---

# 2. Diffusion

## Definition

**Diffusion** is the movement of molecules from a region of **higher concentration to lower concentration** until equilibrium is reached.

---

## Characteristics

- Passive process
- No ATP required
- Occurs in gases, liquids, and solids
- Continues until equilibrium

---

## Examples

- Exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide through stomata
- Diffusion of gases during respiration

---

# Factors Affecting Diffusion

- Concentration gradient
- Temperature
- Surface area
- Size of molecules
- Distance

---

# 3. Facilitated Diffusion

## Definition

Movement of molecules across membranes with the help of **carrier proteins** or **channel proteins**.

---

## Characteristics

- Passive process
- No ATP required
- Specific transport proteins involved

---

## Examples

- Glucose transport
- Ion channels

---

# Difference Between Diffusion and Facilitated Diffusion

| Diffusion             | Facilitated Diffusion    |
| --------------------- | ------------------------ |
| No transport proteins | Requires proteins        |
| Simple movement       | Carrier/channel mediated |
| Less specific         | Highly specific          |

---

# 4. Active Transport

## Definition

Active transport is the movement of substances **against the concentration gradient** using metabolic energy (ATP).

## Characteristics

- Carrier proteins involved
- Highly selective

## Examples

- Mineral absorption by root hairs
- Ion transport

# Introduction

**Respiration** is the biochemical process by which living cells release energy from food molecules. Plants continuously respire throughout their life to obtain **ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate)**, which powers cellular activities such as growth, transport, synthesis, and repair.

Unlike photosynthesis, respiration occurs in **all living cells**, both during the day and night.

---

# Definition

Respiration is the **oxidation of organic food (mainly glucose)** to release energy in the form of ATP.

General Equation (Aerobic Respiration)

```
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

↓

6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy (ATP)
```

---

# Importance of Respiration

- Releases ATP for cellular activities.
- Supports growth and development.
- Provides intermediates for biosynthesis.
- Maintains metabolism.
- Essential for active transport.
- Produces metabolic water.

---

 Aerobic                 Anaerobic

# 1. Aerobic Respiration

## Definition

Respiration occurring **in the presence of oxygen**, resulting in the complete oxidation of glucose.

---

## Characteristics

- Requires oxygen.
- Complete oxidation of glucose.
- Produces maximum ATP.
- Occurs mainly in mitochondria.
- End products:
  - Carbon dioxide
  - Water

---

## Overall Equation

```
Glucose + Oxygen

↓

Carbon Dioxide + Water + ATP
```

---

# 2. Anaerobic Respiration

## Definition

Respiration occurring **without oxygen**, leading to partial breakdown of glucose.

---

## Characteristics

- Oxygen absent.
- Less ATP produced.
- Occurs in cytoplasm.
- Incomplete oxidation.

---

## Alcoholic Fermentation (Yeast)

```
Glucose

↓

Ethanol + CO₂ + ATP
```

---

## Lactic Acid Fermentation

Occurs in

- Muscle cells
- Certain bacteria

```
Glucose

↓

Lactic Acid + ATP
```

# Chapter Summary

- **Respiration in Plants** is the process by which glucose is oxidized to release energy in the form of ATP, supporting all cellular activities.
- Aerobic respiration consists of **glycolysis, oxidative decarboxylation, the Krebs cycle, and the Electron Transport Chain**, resulting in the complete oxidation of glucose.
- **Glycolysis** occurs in the cytoplasm and produces pyruvate, while the **Krebs cycle** and **ETC** occur in mitochondria, generating the majority of ATP.
- The **Chemiosmotic Theory**, proposed by **Peter Mitchell**, explains ATP synthesis through a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane.
- The **Respiratory Quotient (RQ)** helps identify the type of respiratory substrate being metabolized by comparing CO₂ released to O₂ consumed.
- Respiration is considered an **amphibolic pathway** because it functions in both the breakdown of biomolecules for energy and the synthesis of essential cellular compounds, making it central to plant metabolism, growth, and survival.

# Introduction

**Plant Growth and Development** is the process by which plants increase in size, undergo differentiation, and attain maturity. Growth is a permanent increase in size and dry mass, while development includes all the changes from seed germination to senescence.

Plant growth is regulated by **internal factors (plant hormones)** and **external factors (light, temperature, water, oxygen, and nutrients).**

---

# 1. Plant Growth

## Definition

Plant growth is a **permanent and irreversible increase in the size, volume, dry weight, and number of cells** of a plant.

---

# Characteristics of Plant Growth

- Permanent and irreversible.
- Increases cell number and size.
- Requires energy.
- Occurs throughout the life of the plant.
- Controlled by genes and hormones.

---

# Phases of Growth

```
              Plant Growth
                    │
      ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
      ▼             ▼             ▼
 Meristematic   Elongation   Maturation
     Phase         Phase         Phase
```

---

## A. Meristematic Phase

### Characteristics

- Active cell division.
- Cells are small and thin-walled.
- Dense cytoplasm.
- Large nucleus.
- No vacuoles.

---

## B. Elongation Phase

### Characteristics

- Cells enlarge rapidly.
- Vacuoles increase in size.
- Cell wall loosens.
- Maximum increase in length occurs.

---

## C. Maturation Phase

### Characteristics

- Cells attain final shape.
- Differentiation occurs.
- Specialized functions develop.

---

# Growth Rate

## Definition

Growth rate is the increase in size or mass per unit time.

---

# Types of Growth

### Arithmetic Growth

Growth occurs at a constant rate.

Formula

```
Lt = L₀ + rt
```

Where

- Lt = Final length
- L₀ = Initial length
- r = Growth rate
- t = Time

---

### Geometric Growth

Growth is proportional to existing size.

Formula

```
Wt = W₀eʳᵗ
```

# Grand Period of Growth

The complete period from the beginning of growth until maturity.

# 2. Development

# Chapter Summary

- **Plant Growth** is a permanent and irreversible increase in size, while **development** includes growth, differentiation, maturation, and senescence.
- Growth occurs in three phases: **meristematic**, **elongation**, and **maturation**, and follows characteristic growth patterns such as arithmetic and geometric growth.
- **Plant Growth Regulators (PGRs)** coordinate growth and development. **Auxins, gibberellins, and cytokinins** promote growth, whereas **abscisic acid (ABA)** and **ethylene** mainly regulate inhibition, dormancy, stress responses, and fruit ripening.
- **Photoperiodism** controls flowering based on day length, while **vernalisation** promotes flowering through prolonged exposure to low temperatures.
- External factors such as **light, water, temperature, oxygen, and minerals**, together with internal genetic and hormonal factors, regulate plant growth.
- Understanding plant growth and development is essential for **modern agriculture, horticulture, plant breeding, tissue culture, crop improvement, and sustainable food production**.

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

### Question

Which gland is known as the master gland?

### Solution

The pituitary gland controls the activity of several other endocrine glands.

### Answer

Pituitary gland.

---

## Example 2

### Question

Which hormone lowers blood glucose level?

### Solution

Insulin promotes the uptake and storage of glucose, thereby reducing blood glucose concentration.

### Answer

Insulin.

---

## Example 3

### Question

Name the hormone secreted by the adrenal medulla.

### Solution

The adrenal medulla secretes adrenaline and noradrenaline, which prepare the body for emergency situations.

### Answer

Adrenaline (Epinephrine).

---

## Example 4

### Question

What is the cause of goitre?

### Solution

Goitre commonly develops due to iodine deficiency, which reduces thyroxine synthesis and enlarges the thyroid gland.

### Answer

Iodine deficiency.

---

## Example 5

### Question

Differentiate between insulin and glucagon.

### Solution

Insulin decreases blood glucose levels, whereas glucagon increases blood glucose levels.

### Answer

Insulin → Lowers blood glucose; Glucagon → Raises blood glucose.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing endocrine glands with exocrine glands.
- Mixing up the functions of insulin and glucagon.
- Assuming calcitonin and parathyroid hormone have the same effect.
- Confusing gigantism with acromegaly.
- Forgetting that the posterior pituitary stores and releases ADH and oxytocin.
- Mixing up hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism.

# Chapter Summary

- The **endocrine system** regulates body functions through **hormones**, which are chemical messengers released by ductless glands into the bloodstream.
- Major endocrine glands include the **pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, adrenal glands, pancreas, pineal gland, thymus, testes, and ovaries**, each secreting specific hormones with distinct physiological roles.
- Hormones regulate **growth, metabolism, reproduction, calcium balance, stress responses, blood glucose levels, water balance, and immunity**, helping maintain internal stability.
- Hormone secretion is controlled by **feedback mechanisms**, especially **negative feedback**, which maintains homeostasis.
- Disorders such as **diabetes mellitus, goitre, gigantism, acromegaly, dwarfism, Addison's disease, and Cushing's syndrome** result from hormonal imbalance.
- Understanding chemical coordination is fundamental for **medicine, endocrinology, biotechnology, and maintaining overall physiological homeostasis**.
