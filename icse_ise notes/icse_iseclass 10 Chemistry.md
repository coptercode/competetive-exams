# Introduction

The **Periodic Table** is a systematic arrangement of all known elements based on their **atomic numbers**. Elements are arranged in such a way that those with similar chemical properties are placed in the same group. The modern periodic table helps predict the physical and chemical properties of elements based on periodic trends such as **atomic radius, ionisation potential, electron affinity, and electronegativity**.

---

# 1. Modern Periodic Table

## Definition

The **Modern Periodic Table** is the arrangement of elements in increasing order of their **atomic numbers**.

---

## Features

- Contains **118 elements**.
- Arranged into **7 periods** (horizontal rows).
- Arranged into **18 groups** (vertical columns).
- Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties.
- Properties repeat periodically due to similar electronic configurations.

---

## Periods

| Period | Number of Elements |
| ------ | -----------------: |
| 1      |                  2 |
| 2      |                  8 |
| 3      |                  8 |
| 4      |                 18 |
| 5      |                 18 |
| 6      |                 32 |
| 7      |         Incomplete |

---

## Groups

- Group 1 – Alkali Metals
- Group 2 – Alkaline Earth Metals
- Groups 3–12 – Transition Metals
- Group 17 – Halogens
- Group 18 – Noble Gases

---

# 2. Periodic Properties

## Definition

**Periodic Properties** are the properties of elements that change in a regular pattern across periods and down groups.

---

# 3. Atomic Radius

## Definition

**Atomic Radius** is the distance from the centre of the nucleus to the outermost electron shell.

---

## Trend Across a Period

- **Decreases from left to right.**

### Reason

The nuclear charge increases while the number of electron shells remains the same, pulling electrons closer to the nucleus.

---

## Trend Down a Group

- **Increases from top to bottom.**

### Reason

Each successive element has an additional electron shell, increasing the size of the atom.

---

# 4. Ionisation Potential (Ionisation Energy)

## Definition

**Ionisation Potential (IP)** is the minimum energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom.

---

## Trend Across a Period

- **Increases from left to right.**

### Reason

The atomic size decreases and nuclear attraction increases, making it harder to remove an electron.

---

## Trend Down a Group

- **Decreases from top to bottom.**

### Reason

The outermost electron is farther from the nucleus and is less strongly attracted.

---

# 5. Electron Affinity

## Definition

**Electron Affinity (EA)** is the amount of energy released when a neutral gaseous atom gains an electron.

---

## Trend Across a Period

- Generally **increases** from left to right.

---

## Trend Down a Group

- Generally **decreases** from top to bottom.

---

## Note

Halogens have **high electron affinity** because they readily gain one electron to complete their octet.

---

# 6. Electronegativity

## Definition

**Electronegativity** is the tendency of an atom to attract the shared pair of electrons towards itself in a covalent bond.

---

## Trend Across a Period

- **Increases from left to right.**

---

## Trend Down a Group

- **Decreases from top to bottom.**

---

## Most Electronegative Element

```
Fluorine (F)
```

---

# 7. Metallic and Non-Metallic Character

## Metallic Character

The tendency of an element to lose electrons and form positive ions.

### Trend

- Decreases across a period.
- Increases down a group.

---

## Non-Metallic Character

The tendency of an element to gain electrons.

### Trend

- Increases across a period.
- Decreases down a group.

---

# 8. Valency

## Definition

**Valency** is the combining capacity of an atom.

---

## Across a Period

```
1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 → 0
```

---

## Down a Group

Valency generally remains **constant**.

---

# 9. Periodic Trends Summary

| Property               | Across a Period     | Down a Group        |
| ---------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------- |
| Atomic Radius          | Decreases           | Increases           |
| Ionisation Potential   | Increases           | Decreases           |
| Electron Affinity      | Generally Increases | Generally Decreases |
| Electronegativity      | Increases           | Decreases           |
| Metallic Character     | Decreases           | Increases           |
| Non-metallic Character | Increases           | Decreases           |

---

# Difference Between Atomic Radius and Ionic Radius

| Atomic Radius            | Ionic Radius                         |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| Size of a neutral atom   | Size of an ion                       |
| Depends on atomic shells | Depends on gain or loss of electrons |
| Used for neutral atoms   | Used for cations and anions          |

### Question

### Solution

### Answer

### Answer

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **atomic number** with **atomic mass**.
- Mixing up **electron affinity** and **electronegativity**.
- Assuming atomic radius increases across a period (it decreases).
- Forgetting that **fluorine** has the highest electronegativity.
- Confusing ionisation potential with electron affinity.
- Assuming metallic character increases across a period.
- Forgetting that valency generally remains constant within a group.

---

# Quick Revision

- Modern Periodic Table → Based on **atomic number**.
- Periods → **7**
- Groups → **18**
- Atomic Radius → ↓ Across, ↑ Down
- Ionisation Potential → ↑ Across, ↓ Down
- Electron Affinity → Generally ↑ Across, ↓ Down
- Electronegativity → ↑ Across, ↓ Down
- Metallic Character → ↓ Across, ↑ Down
- Non-metallic Character → ↑ Across, ↓ Down
- Most electronegative element → **Fluorine**

---

# Chapter Summary

- The **Modern Periodic Table** arranges elements according to increasing **atomic number**, with **7 periods** and **18 groups**.
- **Periodic properties** show regular trends due to changes in atomic structure and electronic configuration.
- **Atomic radius** decreases across a period because of increasing nuclear charge and increases down a group due to the addition of electron shells.
- **Ionisation potential**, **electron affinity**, and **electronegativity** generally increase across a period and decrease down a group.
- **Metallic character** decreases across a period but increases down a group, while **non-metallic character** shows the opposite trend.
- Understanding these periodic trends helps predict the **reactivity, bonding behaviour, and chemical properties** of elements, making the periodic table a powerful tool in chemistry.

# Introduction

**Chemical Bonding** is the force of attraction that holds atoms together to form molecules or compounds. Atoms combine to achieve a **stable electronic configuration**, usually by completing their outermost shell (octet or duplet). The three main types of chemical bonds studied at the ICSE Class 10 level are **Electrovalent (Ionic) Bonds**, **Covalent Bonds**, and **Coordinate (Dative Covalent) Bonds**.

---

# 1. Chemical Bond

## Definition

A **Chemical Bond** is the attractive force that holds atoms or ions together in a molecule or compound.

---

## Why Do Atoms Form Bonds?

Atoms combine to achieve:

- Stable electronic configuration.
- Noble gas configuration.
- Lower energy state.

---

# 2. Octet Rule

## Definition

The **Octet Rule** states that atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to acquire **eight electrons** in their outermost shell.

### Exception

Hydrogen and Helium follow the **Duplet Rule**, requiring only **two electrons**.

---

# 3. Electrovalent (Ionic) Bond

## Definition

An **Electrovalent (Ionic) Bond** is formed by the **complete transfer of electrons** from one atom to another.

---

## Formation of Sodium Chloride (NaCl)

### Electronic Configuration

- Sodium (Na): **2,8,1**
- Chlorine (Cl): **2,8,7**

### Electron Transfer

```
Na  →  Na⁺ + e⁻

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
```

### Bond Formation

```
Na⁺  +  Cl⁻  →  NaCl
```

---

## Electron Dot Structure

```
Na•      :Cl:

↓

Na⁺ [:Cl:]⁻
```

---

## Formation of Magnesium Chloride (MgCl₂)

### Electronic Configuration

- Magnesium: **2,8,2**
- Chlorine: **2,8,7**

```
Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻

2Cl + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻

Mg²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → MgCl₂
```

---

## Properties of Ionic Compounds

- High melting point.
- High boiling point.
- Hard and brittle.
- Conduct electricity in molten or aqueous state.
- Soluble in water.
- Insoluble in organic solvents.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Chemical bonding** enables atoms to achieve a stable electronic configuration by following the **octet or duplet rule**.
- **Electrovalent (ionic) bonds** are formed through the complete transfer of electrons, producing oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction.
- **Covalent bonds** are formed by the sharing of electron pairs between non-metal atoms and may be single, double, or triple bonds.
- **Coordinate (dative covalent) bonds** are a special type of covalent bond in which both shared electrons are donated by a single atom.
- **Lewis electron dot structures** help represent the arrangement of valence electrons and the formation of chemical bonds.
- Ionic compounds generally have **high melting and boiling points**, conduct electricity in the molten or aqueous state, and are soluble in water, whereas covalent compounds generally have **lower melting points**, do not conduct electricity, and are often insoluble in water.
- Understanding chemical bonding is essential for explaining the **structure, properties, and behaviour** of compounds in chemistry.

# Introduction

**Acids, Bases, and Salts** are important classes of chemical compounds found in everyday life. They play a vital role in industries, agriculture, medicine, food preservation, and laboratory reactions. According to the **Arrhenius Theory**, acids produce **hydrogen ions (H⁺)** or **hydronium ions (H₃O⁺)** in aqueous solution, while bases produce **hydroxide ions (OH⁻)**. Their properties can be studied using indicators, pH values, and chemical reactions.

---

# 1. Acids

## Definition (Arrhenius Theory)

An **Acid** is a substance that produces **H⁺ ions** (or **H₃O⁺ ions**) when dissolved in water.

---

## Examples

- Hydrochloric Acid (HCl)
- Sulfuric Acid (H₂SO₄)
- Nitric Acid (HNO₃)
- Acetic Acid (CH₃COOH)

---

## Properties of Acids

- Sour in taste.
- Turn **blue litmus red**.
- Conduct electricity in aqueous solution.
- React with metals to produce hydrogen gas.
- React with bases to form salts and water.
- Have **pH less than 7**.

---

# 2. Bases

## Definition (Arrhenius Theory)

A **Base** is a substance that produces **OH⁻ ions** in aqueous solution.

---

## Examples

- Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH)
- Potassium Hydroxide (KOH)
- Calcium Hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂]
- Ammonium Hydroxide (NH₄OH)

---

## Properties of Bases

- Bitter in taste.
- Slippery to touch.
- Turn **red litmus blue**.
- Conduct electricity in solution.
- Neutralize acids.
- Have **pH greater than 7**.

---

# 3. Alkalis

## Definition

An **Alkali** is a **water-soluble base**.

---

## Examples

- NaOH
- KOH
- NH₄OH

---

**Indicators** are substances that change colour in acidic or basic solutions.

## Interpretation

- pH = 7 → Neutral

# 6. Neutralization Reaction

A **Neutralization Reaction** is the reaction between an acid and a base to produce **salt and water**.

---

## General Equation

```

---

## Example

```

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Acids** release **H⁺ (or H₃O⁺) ions**, while **bases** release **OH⁻ ions** in aqueous solutions according to the Arrhenius theory.
- **Indicators** such as litmus, methyl orange, phenolphthalein, and universal indicator help identify acidic and basic solutions.
- The **pH scale** ranges from **0 to 14**, where values below **7** are acidic, **7** is neutral, and above **7** are basic.
- **Neutralization reactions** produce **salt and water** when acids react with bases.
- Salts are classified into **normal salts, acid salts, basic salts, and double salts**, and can be prepared by several methods such as neutralization, direct combination, precipitation, and reactions of acids with metals or carbonates.
- Heating certain salts causes decomposition, producing gases and new compounds depending on the type of salt.
- Acids, bases, and salts have numerous applications in **industry, agriculture, medicine, laboratories, and daily life**.

# Introduction

**Analytical Chemistry** is the branch of chemistry that deals with the **identification and analysis of chemical substances**. In qualitative analysis, different reagents are used to identify the presence of specific **cations (positive ions)** and **anions (negative ions)** by observing changes such as the formation of precipitates, colour changes, or gas evolution. At the ICSE Class 10 level, emphasis is placed on the use of **Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH)** and **Ammonium Hydroxide (NH₄OH)** as analytical reagents.

---

# 1. Analytical Chemistry

## Definition

**Analytical Chemistry** is the branch of chemistry that deals with the identification and analysis of substances based on their chemical properties.

---

## Objectives

- Identify unknown salts.
- Detect metal ions.
- Observe precipitate colours.
- Differentiate between similar ions.
- Study chemical reactions.

---

# 2. Reagents

## Definition

A **Reagent** is a chemical substance used to test or identify another substance.

---

## Common Reagents

- Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH)
- Ammonium Hydroxide (NH₄OH)
- Dilute Hydrochloric Acid
- Barium Chloride
- Silver Nitrate

---

# Introduction

The **Mole Concept** is one of the fundamental concepts in chemistry. It provides a link between the microscopic world of atoms and molecules and the measurable quantities used in laboratories. **Stoichiometry** deals with the quantitative relationship between reactants and products in a chemical reaction. Using balanced chemical equations, chemists can calculate masses, moles, volumes of gases, and the number of particles involved in chemical reactions.

---

# 1. Mole

## Definition

A **Mole** is the amount of substance containing **6.022 × 10²³** particles (atoms, molecules, or ions).

---

## Avogadro Constant

```
1 Mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles
```

This number is known as the **Avogadro Constant**.

---

# 2. Molar Mass

## Definition

**Molar Mass** is the mass of one mole of a substance.

---

## Unit

```
g/mol
```

---

## Example

- H₂O = 18 g/mol
- CO₂ = 44 g/mol
- NaCl = 58.5 g/mol

---

# 3. Relative Atomic Mass (Ar)

## Definition

The **Relative Atomic Mass (Ar)** is the average mass of an atom compared to **1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom**.

---

## Example

- H = 1
- O = 16
- C = 12
- Na = 23
- Cl = 35.5

---

# 4. Relative Molecular Mass (Mr)

## Definition

The **Relative Molecular Mass (Mr)** is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms present in a molecule.

---

## Formula

```
Mr = Sum of Atomic Masses
```

---

## Example

### Water

```
H₂O

Mr = (2 × 1) + 16

= 18
```

---

### Carbon Dioxide

```
CO₂

Mr = 12 + (2 × 16)

= 44
```

---

# 5. Mole Formula

## Formula

```
Number of Moles

      Mass
= ----------------
   Molar Mass
```

---

## Rearranged Formula

```
Mass = Moles × Molar Mass
```

---

# 6. Avogadro's Law

## Statement

**Equal volumes of all gases under the same temperature and pressure contain an equal number of molecules.**

---

## Importance

- Explains gas volume relationships.
- Used in stoichiometric calculations.
- Basis of Gram Molecular Volume.

---

# 7. Gay-Lussac's Law of Combining Volumes

## Statement

When gases react, they combine in **simple whole-number ratios by volume**, provided all measurements are made at the same temperature and pressure.

---

## Example

```
2H₂ + O₂

↓

2H₂O
```

Volume Ratio

```
2 : 1 : 2
```

---

# 8. Gram Molecular Volume (GMV)

## Definition

The volume occupied by **one mole of any gas at STP**.

---

## Value

```
22.4 dm³

or

22.4 litres
```

---

## Conditions

- Temperature = 273 K
- Pressure = 1 atm

---

# 9. Vapour Density (VD)

## Definition

**Vapour Density** is the ratio of the mass of a given volume of a gas to the mass of an equal volume of hydrogen under the same conditions.

---

## Formula

```
Mr = 2 × Vapour Density
```

---

## Example

If Vapour Density = 22

```
Mr = 2 × 22

= 44
```

---

# 10. Empirical Formula

## Definition

The **Empirical Formula** gives the **simplest whole-number ratio** of atoms in a compound.

---

## Example

```
Glucose

Molecular Formula = C₆H₁₂O₆

Empirical Formula = CH₂O
```

---

# 11. Molecular Formula

## Definition

The **Molecular Formula** gives the **actual number of atoms** present in one molecule.

---

## Formula

=

(Empirical Formula)n
```

---

## Relation

n

=

Molecular Mass
------------------------
Empirical Formula Mass
```

# 12. Stoichiometry

## Definition

**Stoichiometry** is the calculation of the quantities of reactants and products involved in a chemical reaction.

---

## Steps in Stoichiometric Calculations

1. Write the balanced equation.
2. Calculate molar masses.
3. Convert mass into moles.
4. Apply mole ratio.
5. Convert back to required unit.

---

# Introduction

**Electrolysis** is the chemical decomposition of an electrolyte by passing an electric current through its molten or aqueous solution. It is based on the movement of **ions** toward oppositely charged electrodes. Electrolysis is widely used in **electroplating, electro-refining, extraction of metals, and industrial chemical production**.

---

# 1. Electrolysis

## Definition

**Electrolysis** is the process of chemical decomposition of an electrolyte by the passage of electric current.

---

## Conditions Required

- An electrolyte.
- Two electrodes.
- A source of direct current (DC).

---

# 2. Electrolyte

## Definition

An **Electrolyte** is a substance that conducts electricity in its molten or aqueous state due to the presence of free ions.

---

## Examples

- Sodium chloride solution
- Copper sulphate solution
- Dilute sulfuric acid
- Molten lead bromide

---

# 3. Non-Electrolyte

## Definition

A **Non-Electrolyte** is a substance that does not conduct electricity because it does not produce ions.

---

## Examples

- Sugar solution
- Alcohol
- Distilled water
- Benzene

---

# 4. Electrolytic Cell

## Definition

An **Electrolytic Cell** is a device in which electrical energy is converted into chemical energy through electrolysis.

---

## Components

- Battery (DC source)
- Electrolyte
- Cathode
- Anode
- Connecting wires

---

# 5. Electrodes

---

## Cathode

### Definition

The **Cathode** is the **negative electrode** connected to the negative terminal of the battery.

### At the Cathode

- Cations move towards it.
- Reduction takes place.
- Electrons are gained.

---

## Anode

### Definition

The **Anode** is the **positive electrode** connected to the positive terminal of the battery.

### At the Anode

- Anions move towards it.
- Oxidation takes place.
- Electrons are lost.

---

# 6. Ions

---

## Cations

Positively charged ions.

### Examples

- H⁺
- Cu²⁺
- Na⁺
- Ag⁺

Move towards the **Cathode**.

---

## Anions

Negatively charged ions.

### Examples

- Cl⁻
- OH⁻
- SO₄²⁻
- NO₃⁻

Move towards the **Anode**.

---

- H₂SO₄
- NaCl

---

## Weak Electrolytes

### Definition

Partially ionize in solution.

### Examples

- CH₃COOH
- NH₄OH

---

## Non-Electrolytes

Do not ionize.

### Examples

- Sugar
- Alcohol
- Urea

---

# 8. Preferential Discharge of Ions

## Definition

When more than one ion is present, the ion discharged first at an electrode is determined by its **position in the electrochemical series** and **concentration**.

---

## Factors Affecting Preferential Discharge

- Electrochemical series.
- Concentration of ions.
- Nature of electrodes.

---

## General Rule

- Less reactive cations are discharged first at the cathode.
- Less reactive anions are discharged first at the anode.

# Chapter Summary

- **Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)** is a strong, dibasic mineral acid widely known as the **King of Chemicals** because of its immense industrial importance.
- It is manufactured commercially by the **Contact Process**, where sulfur dioxide is converted into sulfur trioxide using **vanadium(V) oxide (V₂O₅)** as a catalyst, followed by the formation of **oleum** and finally sulphuric acid.
- Sulphuric acid reacts with **metals, bases, and carbonates**, producing salts and characteristic products such as hydrogen gas, water, and carbon dioxide.
- Concentrated sulphuric acid exhibits **dehydrating**, **hygroscopic**, and **oxidizing** properties, making it valuable as a drying agent and industrial reagent.
- It is extensively used in the manufacture of **fertilizers, lead-acid batteries, detergents, explosives, petroleum products, dyes, and pharmaceuticals**.
- Proper handling is essential because sulphuric acid is highly corrosive, and **acid should always be added to water** during dilution to ensure safety.
