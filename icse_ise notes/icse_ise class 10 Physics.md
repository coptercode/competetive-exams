# Introduction

A **force** is a push or pull acting on an object that can change its state of rest or motion, direction, or shape. Forces are responsible for various physical phenomena such as rotation, balancing, circular motion, and the stability of objects. This chapter covers the concepts of **Turning Effect of Force (Torque), Principle of Moments, Centre of Gravity, Uniform Circular Motion, and Centrifugal Force**.

---

# 1. Force

## Definition

A **Force** is a push or pull that changes or tends to change the state of rest, motion, or shape of a body.

---

## SI Unit

```
Newton (N)
```

---

## Effects of Force

- Changes the speed of a body.
- Changes the direction of motion.
- Changes the shape or size.
- Produces rotation.
- Brings a moving body to rest.

---

# 2. Turning Effect of Force (Moment of Force / Torque)

## Definition

The **Moment of Force (Torque)** is the turning effect produced by a force about a fixed point or axis.

---

## Formula

```
Torque (τ) = Force × Perpendicular Distance

τ = F × d
```

Where,

- τ = Torque (N·m)
- F = Force (N)
- d = Perpendicular distance from pivot (m)

---

## SI Unit

```
Newton metre (N·m)
```

---

## Direction of Torque

### Clockwise Moment

Produces clockwise rotation.

```
↻
```

---

### Anticlockwise Moment

Produces anticlockwise rotation.

```
↺
```

---

## Factors Affecting Torque

- Magnitude of force.
- Perpendicular distance from pivot.
- Direction of applied force.

---

## Applications

- Opening a door.
- Using a spanner.
- Steering wheel.
- Seesaw.

---

# 3. Principle of Moments

## Statement

When a body is in equilibrium,

```
Sum of Clockwise Moments

=

Sum of Anticlockwise Moments
```

---

## Mathematical Form

```
CW Moment = ACW Moment
```

or

```
F₁ × d₁ = F₂ × d₂
```

---

## Conditions for Equilibrium

- Net force = 0
- Net moment = 0

---

## Applications

- Beam balance.
- Seesaw.
- Crowbar.
- Lever systems.

---

# 4. Centre of Gravity (CG)

## Definition

The **Centre of Gravity** is the point through which the entire weight of a body appears to act.

---

## Properties

- Weight acts vertically downward through CG.
- A body balances at its CG.
- Depends on shape and mass distribution.

---

## CG of Regular Bodies

| Object      | Centre of Gravity         |
| ----------- | ------------------------- |
| Uniform Rod | Midpoint                  |
| Square      | Intersection of diagonals |
| Rectangle   | Intersection of diagonals |
| Circle      | Centre                    |
| Sphere      | Centre                    |

---

## Shifting of Centre of Gravity

The CG shifts when:

- Shape changes.
- Mass distribution changes.
- Part of the object is removed.
- External load is added.

---

## Stability of Bodies

### Stable Equilibrium

CG rises slightly and returns to its original position.

Example:

```
Cone resting on its base.
```

---

### Unstable Equilibrium

CG lowers after disturbance.

Example:

```
Cone resting on its tip.
```

---

### Neutral Equilibrium

CG remains at the same height.

Example:

```
Sphere on a horizontal surface.
```

---

# 5. Uniform Circular Motion

## Definition

Motion of an object in a circular path with **constant speed**.

---

## Characteristics

- Speed remains constant.
- Velocity continuously changes.
- Acceleration acts towards the centre.
- Requires centripetal force.

---

## Centripetal Force

### Definition

The force acting towards the centre of a circular path.

---

## Formula

```
        mv²
Fc = --------
         r
```

Where,

- Fc = Centripetal force
- m = Mass
- v = Velocity
- r = Radius

---

## Centripetal Acceleration

```
        v²
a = --------
        r
```

---

## Examples

- Satellite motion.
- Earth revolving around the Sun.
- Stone tied to a string.
- Cars moving around curved roads.

---

# 6. Centrifugal Force

## Definition

A **Centrifugal Force** is the apparent (pseudo) force experienced by an object in a rotating frame, acting away from the centre of rotation.

---

## Characteristics

- Acts outward.
- Not a real force.
- Observed only in rotating reference frames.
- Equal in magnitude to centripetal force but opposite in direction.

---

## Applications

- Centrifuge machines.
- Spin dryers.
- Washing machines.
- Banking of roads.
- Merry-go-round.

---

# Difference Between Centripetal and Centrifugal Force

| Centripetal Force            | Centrifugal Force          |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Real force                   | Pseudo force               |
| Acts towards centre          | Acts away from centre      |
| Required for circular motion | Apparent in rotating frame |
| Provided by external agent   | Experienced by observer    |

---

# Difference Between Stable and Unstable Equilibrium

| Stable Equilibrium           | Unstable Equilibrium              |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Returns to original position | Moves away from original position |
| CG rises on disturbance      | CG lowers on disturbance          |
| More stable                  | Less stable                       |

# Solved Examples

## Example 1

### Question

A force of **20 N** acts at a perpendicular distance of **0.5 m** from the pivot. Calculate the torque.

### Solution

Given:

```
F = 20 N

d = 0.5 m
```

Using

```
τ = F × d
```

```
τ = 20 × 0.5

= 10 N·m
```

### Answer

```
Torque = 10 N·m
```

---

## Example 2

### Question

State the Principle of Moments.

### Solution

For a body in equilibrium, clockwise and anticlockwise moments balance each other.

### Answer

```
Clockwise Moment = Anticlockwise Moment
```

---

## Example 3

### Question

Calculate the centripetal force acting on a **2 kg** body moving at **6 m/s** in a circle of radius **3 m**.

### Solution

```
m = 2 kg

v = 6 m/s

r = 3 m
```

```
Fc = mv²/r

= 2 × 36 / 3

= 24 N
```

### Answer

```
24 N
```

---

## Example 4

### Question

Where is the centre of gravity of a uniform circular disc?

### Solution

For a uniform circular disc, the weight is evenly distributed.

### Answer

At the centre of the disc.

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **force** with **torque**.
- Forgetting that **distance must be perpendicular** in torque calculations.
- Mixing up **clockwise** and **anticlockwise** moments.
- Confusing **centripetal** and **centrifugal** forces.
- Assuming velocity remains constant in direction during circular motion.
- Forgetting that **centrifugal force is a pseudo force**.
- Ignoring SI units in numerical problems.

---

# Quick Revision

- Force → Push or pull.
- Torque → **τ = F × d**.
- SI Unit of Torque → **N·m**.
- Principle of Moments → **CW = ACW**.
- Centre of Gravity → Point through which weight acts.
- Uniform Circular Motion → Constant speed in a circular path.
- Centripetal Force → **mv²/r**.
- Centripetal Acceleration → **v²/r**.
- Centrifugal Force → Apparent outward force.
- Applications → Doors, levers, satellites, centrifuges.

---

# Chapter Summary

- A **force** is a push or pull that can change the motion, direction, or shape of an object.
- The **turning effect of a force**, called **torque (moment of force)**, depends on the applied force and its perpendicular distance from the pivot, and is given by **τ = F × d**.
- The **Principle of Moments** states that for a body in equilibrium, the total clockwise moments equal the total anticlockwise moments.
- The **Centre of Gravity (CG)** is the point through which the entire weight of a body acts and determines the stability of objects.
- In **uniform circular motion**, an object moves with constant speed but continuously changing direction due to **centripetal force**, which acts towards the centre of the circle.
- **Centrifugal force** is an apparent outward force observed in rotating reference frames and explains the operation of devices such as centrifuges and spin dryers.
- These concepts form the foundation for understanding mechanical equilibrium, rotational motion, and many real-life engineering and scientific applications.

# Introduction

**Work, Energy, and Power** are fundamental concepts in mechanics that describe how forces produce motion, how energy is transferred, and how quickly work is done. These concepts explain the operation of machines, engines, vehicles, and everyday activities. This chapter also introduces the **Work-Energy Theorem** and the **Law of Conservation of Energy**.

---

# 1. Work

## Definition

**Work** is said to be done when a force acting on a body causes displacement in the direction of the force.

---

## Conditions for Work

- A force must act on the body.
- The body must be displaced.
- The displacement should have a component in the direction of the applied force.

---

## Formula

```
Work = Force × Displacement × cos θ

W = Fd cosθ
```

Where,

- W = Work (J)
- F = Force (N)
- d = Displacement (m)
- θ = Angle between force and displacement

---

## SI Unit

```
Joule (J)
```

### 1 Joule

One joule is the work done when a force of **1 N** moves a body through **1 m** in the direction of the force.

---

## Types of Work

### Positive Work

Force and displacement are in the same direction.

```
θ = 0°

W = +Fd
```

Example:

- Pulling a cart.

---

### Negative Work

Force acts opposite to displacement.

```
θ = 180°

W = -Fd
```

Example:

- Friction.

---

### Zero Work

No displacement or force is perpendicular to displacement.

```
θ = 90°

W = 0
```

Example:

- Carrying a bag on a horizontal road.

---

# 2. Energy

## Definition

**Energy** is the capacity of a body to do work.

---

## SI Unit

```

# 3. Potential Energy (PE)

## Definition

**Potential Energy** is the energy possessed by a body due to its position or configuration.

---

## Gravitational Potential Energy

### Formula

```
PE = mgh
```

Where,

- m = Mass (kg)
- g = Acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)
- h = Height (m)

---

## SI Unit

```
Joule (J)
```

---

## Factors Affecting PE

- Mass.
- Height.
- Gravitational acceleration.

---

# 4. Kinetic Energy (KE)

## Definition

**Kinetic Energy** is the energy possessed by a body due to its motion.

---

## Formula

```
      1
KE = --- mv²
      2
```

Where,

- m = Mass (kg)
- v = Velocity (m/s)

---

## SI Unit

```
Joule (J)
```

---

## Factors Affecting KE

- Mass.
- Velocity (depends on the square of velocity).

---

# 5. Mechanical Energy

## Definition

The sum of kinetic energy and potential energy.

---

## Formula

```
Mechanical Energy

=

PE + KE
```

or

```
E = U + K
```

---

# 6. Conservation of Energy

## Law

**Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form to another.**

---

## Free Fall of a Body

### At the Highest Point

```
PE = Maximum

KE = Zero
```

---

### During Fall

```
PE decreases

↓

KE increases
```

---

### Just Before Reaching Ground

```
PE ≈ Zero

KE = Maximum
```

---

### Total Mechanical Energy

```
PE + KE

=

Constant
```

---

# 7. Work-Energy Theorem

## Statement

**The work done on a body is equal to the change in its kinetic energy.**

---

## Mathematical Form

```
Work Done

=

Change in Kinetic Energy
```

or

```
W = ΔKE
```

---

# 8. Power

## Definition

**Power** is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

---

## Formula

```
Power = Work / Time

P = W/t
```

---

## Alternative Formula

```
P = F × v
```

Where,

- P = Power (W)
- F = Force (N)
- v = Velocity (m/s)

---

## SI Unit

```
Watt (W)
```

---

## One Watt

A power of **1 watt** means **1 joule of work is done in 1 second**.

---

## Larger Units

```
1 kW = 1000 W

1 MW = 10⁶ W
```

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **work** with **force**.
- Forgetting the **cosθ** term in the work formula.
- Using **mass instead of weight** in potential energy calculations.
- Ignoring that **kinetic energy depends on the square of velocity**.
- Confusing **power** with **energy**.
- Forgetting SI units (**Joule** and **Watt**).
- Assuming energy is lost during transformation instead of converted.

---

# Quick Revision

- Work → **W = Fd cosθ**.
- SI Unit of Work → **Joule (J)**.
- Potential Energy → **PE = mgh**.
- Kinetic Energy → **KE = ½mv²**.
- Mechanical Energy → **PE + KE**.
- Conservation of Energy → Total energy remains constant.
- Work-Energy Theorem → **W = ΔKE**.
- Power → **P = W/t = Fv**.
- SI Unit of Power → **Watt (W)**.
- **1 kW = 1000 W**.

---

# Chapter Summary

- **Work** is done when a force causes displacement and is calculated using **W = Fd cosθ**.
- **Energy** is the ability to do work and exists in many forms, with **potential energy** depending on position (**PE = mgh**) and **kinetic energy** depending on motion (**KE = ½mv²**).
- The **Work-Energy Theorem** states that the work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy.
- **Mechanical energy** is the sum of potential and kinetic energies and remains constant in an isolated system according to the **Law of Conservation of Energy**.
- During **free fall**, potential energy decreases while kinetic energy increases, but the total mechanical energy remains constant.
- **Power** is the rate of doing work and is measured in **watts**, with formulas **P = W/t** and **P = Fv**.
- These concepts explain the operation of machines, vehicles, power plants, and countless everyday mechanical systems.

# Introduction

A **machine** is a device that makes work easier by multiplying force, changing the direction of force, increasing speed, or transferring force from one place to another. Machines do not reduce the total work done; instead, they help us perform work more conveniently and efficiently. This chapter covers **Mechanical Advantage (MA), Velocity Ratio (VR), Efficiency (η), Levers, Pulleys, and Block and Tackle Systems**.

---

# 1. Machine

## Definition

A **machine** is a device that helps us do work with less effort by changing the magnitude or direction of the applied force.

---

## Functions of a Machine

- Multiplies force.
- Changes the direction of force.
- Increases speed.
- Transfers force from one point to another.

---

# 2. Effort and Load

## Effort (E)

The force applied to operate a machine.

---

## Load (L)

The resistance or weight lifted by the machine.

---

# 3. Mechanical Advantage (MA)

## Definition

**Mechanical Advantage** is the ratio of the load lifted by a machine to the effort applied.

---

## Formula

```
          Load
MA = -------------
         Effort
```

or

```
MA = L / E
```

---

## SI Unit

```
No unit (Dimensionless)
```

---

## Interpretation

- MA > 1 → Machine multiplies force.
- MA = 1 → No force multiplication.
- MA < 1 → Machine increases speed instead of force.

---

# 4. Velocity Ratio (VR)

## Definition

**Velocity Ratio** is the ratio of the distance moved by the effort to the distance moved by the load.

---

## Formula

```
              Distance moved by Effort
VR = -----------------------------------------
              Distance moved by Load
```

or

```
VR = DE / DL
```

---

## SI Unit

```
No unit
```

---

# 5. Efficiency (η)

## Definition

Efficiency is the percentage of input work converted into useful output work.

---

## Formula

```
               Output Work
η = ---------------------------- × 100%
               Input Work
```

---

## Relation Between MA and VR

```
           MA
η = --------------- × 100%
           VR
```

or

```
MA = η × VR
```

(Here, η is expressed as a decimal in the second equation.)

---

## Important Points

- Efficiency can never exceed **100%**.
- Due to friction, practical machines always have efficiency less than 100%.

---

# 6. Lever

## Definition

A **lever** is a rigid bar capable of rotating about a fixed point called the **fulcrum**.

---

## Parts of a Lever

- **Fulcrum (F):** Fixed point.
- **Effort (E):** Applied force.
- **Load (L):** Resistance lifted.

---

# Principle of Lever

```
Effort × Effort Arm

=

Load × Load Arm
```

---

## Formula

```
E × EA = L × LA
```

---

# 7. Types of Levers

---

## (i) Class I Lever

### Arrangement

```
Effort ─ Fulcrum ─ Load

E ─ F ─ L
```

Fulcrum lies between effort and load.

---

### Examples

- Seesaw.
- Scissors.
- Crowbar.
- Beam balance.

---

## (ii) Class II Lever

### Arrangement

```
Fulcrum ─ Load ─ Effort

F ─ L ─ E
```

Load lies between fulcrum and effort.

---

### Examples

- Wheelbarrow.
- Nutcracker.
- Bottle opener.

---

## (iii) Class III Lever

### Arrangement

```
Fulcrum ─ Effort ─ Load

F ─ E ─ L
```

Effort lies between fulcrum and load.

---

### Examples

- Human forearm.
- Fishing rod.
- Tweezers.

---

# Comparison of Levers

| Class I                | Class II         | Class III        |
| ---------------------- | ---------------- | ---------------- |
| Fulcrum in middle      | Load in middle   | Effort in middle |
| MA may be <, =, or > 1 | MA > 1           | MA < 1           |
| Changes direction      | Multiplies force | Multiplies speed |

---

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **Mechanical Advantage (MA)** with **Velocity Ratio (VR)**.
- Forgetting that **efficiency is always less than or equal to 100%**.
- Mixing up the positions of **fulcrum, effort, and load** in different classes of levers.
- Assuming a **fixed pulley multiplies force** (it only changes the direction of effort).
- Using incorrect units for MA and VR (both are **dimensionless**).
- Forgetting that friction reduces the efficiency of real machines.

---

# Quick Revision

- Machine → Makes work easier.
- Mechanical Advantage → **MA = L/E**.
- Velocity Ratio → **VR = DE/DL**.
- Efficiency → **η = (MA/VR) × 100%**.
- Lever Principle → **E × EA = L × LA**.
- Class I Lever → **Fulcrum in middle**.
- Class II Lever → **Load in middle**.
- Class III Lever → **Effort in middle**.
- Single Fixed Pulley → **MA = 1, VR = 1**.
- Single Movable Pulley → **MA = 2, VR = 2 (ideal)**.
- Block and Tackle → **VR = Number of supporting rope segments**.

---

# Chapter Summary

- A **machine** is a device that makes work easier by multiplying force, changing its direction, increasing speed, or transferring force efficiently.
- The performance of a machine is measured using **Mechanical Advantage (MA)**, **Velocity Ratio (VR)**, and **Efficiency (η)**. These quantities are related by **η = (MA/VR) × 100%**.
- A **lever** is a simple machine that works on the **Principle of Moments**, where the effort moment equals the load moment.
- Levers are classified into **Class I, Class II, and Class III** based on the positions of the fulcrum, load, and effort, each suited for different practical applications.
- **Pulleys** are simple machines used to lift loads. A **single fixed pulley** changes the direction of effort, while a **single movable pulley** and a **block and tackle system** provide force multiplication.
- Machines are widely used in engineering, construction, transportation, manufacturing, and daily life to perform work more efficiently and safely.

# Introduction

**Refraction** is the phenomenon in which light changes its direction when it passes from one transparent medium to another due to a change in its speed. Refraction explains many natural phenomena such as the apparent bending of a pencil in water, the apparent depth of ponds, mirages, and the working of optical instruments. This chapter covers **Snell's Law, Refraction through Glass Slabs and Prisms, Refractive Index, Apparent Depth, Critical Angle, and Total Internal Reflection (TIR).**

---

# 1. Refraction of Light

## Definition

**Refraction** is the change in the direction of light when it passes obliquely from one transparent medium to another because its speed changes.

---

## Causes of Refraction

- Change in the speed of light.
- Change in the optical density of the medium.

---

## Rules of Refraction

### (i) From Rarer to Denser Medium

- Speed decreases.
- Light bends **towards the normal**.

```
Air → Glass

      \
       \
        \
---------|---------
         |\
         | \
         |  \
      Normal
```

- Speed increases.
- Light bends **away from the normal**.

```
Glass → Air

         /
        /
       /
---------|---------
        |
        |
     Normal
```

# 2. Refractive Index

## Definition

The **Refractive Index (μ)** of a medium is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in that medium.

## Formula

      c

      v

# 3. Snell's Law

          sin r

# Chapter Summary

- **Refraction** is the bending of light when it travels between transparent media of different optical densities due to a change in its speed.
- The amount of bending depends on the **refractive index**, which is related to the speed of light by **μ = c/v**, and follows **Snell's Law (μ = sin i / sin r)**.
- In a **glass slab**, the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but undergoes **lateral displacement**, while a **prism** causes deviation of light toward its base.
- Refraction also explains the phenomenon of **apparent depth**, making objects under water appear closer to the surface than they actually are.
- When light travels from a denser to a rarer medium and the angle of incidence exceeds the **critical angle**, **Total Internal Reflection (TIR)** occurs.
- TIR has important applications in **optical fibres, medical endoscopes, binoculars, periscopes, prisms, and communication systems**, making refraction one of the most important concepts in optics.

# Introduction

**Calorimetry** is the branch of physics that deals with the measurement of heat gained or lost by a body during physical or chemical processes. It is based on the **Principle of Heat Exchange**, which states that in an isolated system, the heat lost by the hotter body is equal to the heat gained by the colder body until thermal equilibrium is reached. This chapter covers **heat, temperature, specific heat capacity, heat capacity, latent heat, calorimeter, and methods of heat transfer**.

---

# 1. Heat

## Definition

**Heat** is a form of energy that flows from a body at a higher temperature to a body at a lower temperature.

---

## Characteristics

- Heat is a form of energy.
- It always flows from a hotter body to a colder body.
- It continues to flow until thermal equilibrium is reached.
- SI Unit: **Joule (J)**

---

**Temperature** is the degree of hotness or coldness of a body.

---

```

# 3. Specific Heat Capacity

## Definition

**Specific Heat Capacity (c)** is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of **1 kg** of a substance by **1°C (or 1 K)**.

      Mass × Rise in Temperature

```

```

# 5. Principle of Heat Exchange

## Statement

```
Heat Lost

=

Heat Gained

## Formula

m₂c₂(T − T₂)

- **T₂** = Initial temperature of cold body
- **T** = Final equilibrium temperature

# Introduction

**Radioactivity** is the spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei accompanied by the emission of invisible radiations. It was discovered by **Henri Becquerel** in **1896**, and later extensively studied by **Marie Curie** and **Pierre Curie**. Radioactivity has numerous applications in **medicine, agriculture, industry, archaeology, and nuclear power generation**, but excessive exposure can be harmful to living organisms.

# 1. Radioactivity

## Definition

**Radioactivity** is the spontaneous emission of radiations from the nucleus of an unstable atom without any external influence.

---

## Characteristics

- Spontaneous process.
- Random in nature.
- Independent of temperature, pressure, and chemical reactions.
- Accompanied by the emission of α, β, and γ radiations.
- Causes the nucleus to become more stable.

---

# 2. Discovery of Radioactivity

## Henri Becquerel (1896)

- Discovered natural radioactivity while working with uranium salts.
- Observed that uranium emitted invisible radiations capable of blackening photographic plates.

---

## Marie and Pierre Curie

- Discovered radioactive elements:
  - Radium (Ra)
  - Polonium (Po)
- Coined the term **Radioactivity**.

---

## Definition

**Half-life** is the time required for half of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay.

---

## Symbol

```

## Characteristics

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing **radioactivity** with **nuclear fission**.
- Mixing up the properties of **α, β, and γ rays**.
- Assuming **gamma rays have mass or charge** (they do not).
- Forgetting that **half-life is constant** for a given radioactive element.
- Confusing **penetrating power** with **ionising power**.
- Ignoring radiation safety measures.

# Chapter Summary

- **Radioactivity** is the spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei, discovered by **Henri Becquerel**, with important contributions from **Marie and Pierre Curie**.
- Radioactive substances emit **alpha (α)**, **beta (β)**, and **gamma (γ)** radiations, each having distinct properties in terms of **charge, mass, ionising power, and penetrating power**.
- The **half-life** of a radioactive substance is the fixed time required for half of its radioactive nuclei to decay, and it is unaffected by physical or chemical conditions.
- Radioactivity has numerous beneficial applications in **medicine, industry, agriculture, archaeology, scientific research, and nuclear power generation**.
- However, excessive exposure to radioactive radiation can cause **serious health hazards**, making proper **radiation safety measures** such as lead shielding and controlled exposure essential.
- The study of radioactivity has transformed modern science and technology, contributing significantly to healthcare, energy production, and our understanding of atomic structure.
